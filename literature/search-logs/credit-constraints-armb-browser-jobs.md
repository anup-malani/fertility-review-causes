## 1. *The Babies of Financial Deregulation* was never a separate study

OpenAlex lists it (2016, SSRN, no DOI) with **Isaac Hacamo** as sole author and an abstract describing
"a ruling by a U.S. federal regulator that created geographical heterogeneity in access to mortgage
credit" — the same design as the SSRN working paper already delivered, *The Babies of Mortgage Market
Deregulation* (2020), and as the *RFS* article. **One study, three versions.** Retrieved, extracted.

**Second identified estimate in the chapter:** young households fully exposed to mortgage-market
deregulation raise their probability of having a child by **+6 percentage points**. The author's
supplemental tests reject income and housing-wealth channels and point to **access to space** — which
sits on the C.2.c boundary even though the varying object is credit.

## 3. Ao, Chen and Tseng (2026) — the first identified NULL

Taiwan. **Difference-in-differences with matching**, exploiting preferential mortgage rates for
specific groups.

- First stage **verified**: mortgage burden falls **7.9%**, and **17.6%** outside the most expensive
  cities.
- **No effect on the probability of home purchase or on fertility.**

**This is an informative null, not an uninformative one.** The policy demonstrably cut the mortgage
burden, so the zero on fertility is not a failed first stage — unlike Grimm's bank-access interaction,
where the author says the measures may simply be too crude. The distinction matters for GRADE.

**And it may reconcile with the two positive identified estimates.** Those are credit **supply/access**
shocks — deregulation, a Bartik credit shock — which move the *extensive* margin: whether a household
can borrow at all. This is a **cost reduction for households already borrowing**: the intensive margin.
Access changes fertility; cheapness apparently does not. That is a testable reading and it should be
put to the remaining Arm B records rather than assumed.

## 4. Wang and He (2025) — an inverted U

China Family Panel Studies, three waves 2018–2022, **fixed-effects Poisson with an instrumental
variable**. Credit expansion has an **inverted-U effect** on fertility: raising it up to a point, then
lowering it, with the marginal effect heterogeneous in household structure and intergenerational
support.

That is a **within-setting** nonlinearity, distinct from the **cross-setting** sign flip in the
aggregate cross-country panel — but it is the same theme, and it is the first time the chapter has that
theme from an identified design. The paper also reports bequests raising fertility **intentions** via
liquidity while educational investment suppresses fertility via quantity–quality: different outcome
levels, to be recorded separately at full text.
