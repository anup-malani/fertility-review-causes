# Academic Voice — LAW-Review Register (Anup Malani)

Paragraphs below are quoted **verbatim** from Anup Malani's own law-review papers (corpus at
`assistants/research-manager/projects/style-analysis/corpus/`). Each teaches a distinct
rhetorical or sentence-level move. **CORE** is the default injection set — one paragraph per
archetype. **EXTENDED** holds additional non-redundant keepers.

**Transcription conventions.** Superscript footnote-reference numbers are dropped. A word split
across a line/page break in the source ("wel-/fare") is rejoined ("welfare"). Where the marker
extraction introduced OCR noise (em-dash rendered "?", "IRS" as "1RS", spaced hyphenation), the
text is restored to the true published wording and the fix is flagged on the passage. No other
change.

**Law-register em-dash note (READ THIS).** Malani's law-review prose uses the em-dash pervasively,
almost always as an **appositive or definitional pair** ("the marginal resident — the resident who
is indifferent — is willing to give up…"), not as a dramatic pause. `ai-tells.md` §9 hard-bans the
em-dash-as-pause across all writing. Per the brief, em-dashes authentic to this register are
**flagged, not excluded** — the user decides whether the law register gets an exception. CORE
passages 2, 4, and 5 contain them; each is flagged inline. If the exception is denied, the dashes
recast cleanly to commas or parentheses without touching the wording.

## Prioritization note — which papers, and why

Sampled his sole- and lead-authored **law-review** work, both single-authored and co-authored,
and skipped the formulaic/clinical pieces (JAMA/Lancet/BMJ/Sci Reports, medRxiv) and pure-methods
papers, which carry house style rather than his voice. The five distinct law-review papers drawn on:

- **Valuing Laws as Local Amenities** — Harvard Law Review (2008), sole. His signature article; source of the counterintuitive-opener and the compensating-differential concept move (CORE 1, 2).
- **Still in Mortal Peril?** — Journal of Legal Studies (2021), sole. Essayistic review of Epstein; source of the object-redefinition and the generous-concession moves (CORE 3, EXT 6).
- **The Case for For-Profit Charities** — Virginia Law Review (2007), Malani & Posner (lead). The coined-question critique engine and the concrete-hypothetical opener (CORE 4, EXT 7).
- **RICO Forfeiture note** — University of Chicago Law Review (1999), sole. Earliest law voice; the translate-doctrine-into-economic-categories move (CORE 5).
- **Raising the Stakes in Patent Cases** — Georgetown Law Journal (2013), Malani & Masur (lead). Instrumentalist counterintuitive proposal in first-person plural (EXT 8 — deprecated 2026-07-27 as duplicative of CORE 1; see Deprecated section).

Two of the three most-signature passages the prior single bank had parked in alternates only for
em-dash/OCR reasons — the compensating-differential ("she would say no thanks") and the coupling-
question critique — are **promoted to CORE here**, with the em-dashes flagged rather than the
paragraphs dropped.

**Through-line (one line):** a lawyer who thinks like an economist — states a counterintuitive
thesis flat in the first sentence, voices and answers the reader's "that seems odd" objection,
translates a doctrinal problem into economic categories or a one-cent thought experiment, and
concedes generously ("To be clear, I am not arguing…") without ever losing the thesis.

## CORE

### 1. Counterintuitive thesis-opener + voice-the-objection
**Source:** `2008_Malani_HarvLRev_LawAsLocalAmenity`, Introduction ¶1 (Harv. L. Rev., sole). Clean text.

> The value of a law to a jurisdiction can be judged by the extent to which it either raises short-run housing prices and lowers short-run wages or raises the long-run, aggregate value of residential land in that jurisdiction. This may seem an odd way to assess the welfare effect of a law. After all, higher housing prices and lower wages are thought to be bad outcomes, not good ones. But the proper way to understand these changes is as signals of positive outcomes, not as positive outcomes themselves. They indicate that something good has happened in the community. Housing prices go up because more people want to live there. Wages go down because more people want to work there. Phrased more formally, higher housing prices and lower wages are how markets ration an attractive local public good or amenity. Indeed, the increase in housing prices combined with the reduction in wages provides a measure of how much people are willing to give up to enjoy the amenity. In the long run, the supply of houses and jobs may increase in response to the rise in housing prices and fall in wages, but the maximum amount of land available to residential development is constant. Therefore, people's willingness to pay eventually settles into the aggregate value of residential land in the jurisdiction. Conventional economic thinking recognizes this when it comes to estimating the social value of a new park or a better school. The same logic, I argue here, applies when the local public good is anything from a better tort system to smarter rules regarding capital punishment.

*Move:* counterintuitive thesis stated flat in sentence one, then the reader's own objection
voiced ("This may seem an odd way… After all…") and dispatched ("But the proper way…"). Plain
declaratives do the work; no throat-clearing.

### 2. Coin a concept, operationalize it with a concrete device
**Source:** `2008_Malani_HarvLRev_LawAsLocalAmenity`, § II (Harv. L. Rev., sole). **FLAG: one appositive em-dash pair.**

> A useful byproduct of this equilibrating process is that we now have a measure of the value of the new law: the amount that housing prices rise plus the amount that wages fall. Economists call this the "compensating differential" for enjoying the law. That is the highest amount that the marginal resident — the resident who is indifferent between living in the first or second state — is willing to give up (or pay) to live under the law. In the abstract, if you offered that individual the ability to live under the new law at a price one cent below the compensating differential, she would accept. If you charged her one cent more, she would say no thanks.

*Move:* names the technical concept, then operationalizes it with a one-cent thought experiment
and an unmistakably human close ("she would say no thanks"). His single best concept-defining
paragraph. Promoted from the old bank's alternates; em-dash flagged, not dropped.

### 3. Redefine the object of study, defend it with a dinner-table test
**Source:** `2021_Malani_JLS_MortalPeril`, § 3.1 (J. Legal Stud., sole). Clean text.

> The primary source of risk in the health domain is sickness, which ranges from cuts and bruises to heart disease and diabetes. To see why sickness is the primary source of risk, ask oneself how often a person dons a Band-Aid or takes antibiotics or undergoes surgery without sickness. The answer is rarely. While medical bills may be a proximate source of risk, the root cause is sickness.

*Move:* redefines what the object of study actually is (risk = sickness, not medical bills) and
defends it with a homemade test anyone can run ("ask oneself how often a person dons a Band-Aid…").
Distinguishes proximate from root cause in one clean sentence.

### 4. The critique engine — one coined question run through every rival theory
**Source:** `2007_Malani_CaseForForProfitCharities`, § II (Va. L. Rev., Malani & Posner, lead). **FLAG: appositive em-dash pair; OCR-reconstructed** ("1RS" → "IRS"; "?" → em-dash — verified against source, line 73–75).

> We will argue that none of these theories answers what we will call the coupling or linkage question, that is, why a particular tax benefit designed to promote charitable activities is conditioned on a particular corporate form (the nonprofit form or, equivalently, the nondistribution constraint). The public goods theory explains why the government should use tax breaks—specifically the deduction for charitable donations—to encourage provision of public goods but fails to explain why the government should deny such tax breaks to for-profit firms that produce these goods. The agency theory explains why people might prefer to donate to a nonprofit firm but not why nonprofit firms should receive a tax benefit. The altruism theory may justify tax breaks for nonprofits that produce public goods, but it relies on questionable assumptions about the costs and prevalence of altruists to deny the same tax breaks to for-profits, which might be more efficient at producing public goods. The imperfect consumer theory cannot explain why traditional consumer protection laws are insufficient protection against fraud by charities. Finally, the administrability objection ignores simpler solutions such as charging firms that seek Section 501(c)(3) classification a user fee to fund IRS review.

*Move:* coins one operative question ("the coupling question") and runs every rival theory through
it in turn, granting what each explains before naming what it cannot. His most characteristic
argumentative engine. Promoted from the old bank's alternates; em-dashes flagged, not dropped.

### 5. Translate legal doctrine into economic categories
**Source:** `1999_Malani_RICOForfeiture`, § I (U. Chi. L. Rev., sole). **FLAG: two definitional em-dashes** (restored from OCR "—"; verified against source, lines 55, 73).

> The Courts of Appeals are split over the proper interpretation of the term "proceeds." The Seventh Circuit holds that proceeds are only "net profits," or total revenues minus both fixed and marginal costs. The Second Circuit and a lower court in the Third Circuit have held that proceeds are "gross profits"—total revenues minus marginal costs, but not fixed costs. The First, Fourth, and Eighth Circuits have adopted an expansive interpretation—"gross revenues," or all revenues from an activity without subtracting any costs, fixed or marginal. Finally, although the D.C. Circuit has rejected the Seventh Circuit's net profits approach, it has not specifically chosen any other definition. Most circuits that have examined the scope of proceeds subject to criminal RICO forfeiture acknowledge their disagreement.

*Move:* maps each competing legal interpretation onto a precise economic cost concept (net profits
= revenues − fixed − marginal; gross profits = revenues − marginal; gross revenues = no deduction).
Reconciling doctrine through economic definitions is the defining law-review move across his corpus.

## EXTENDED

### 6. Concede generously while holding the line
**Source:** `2021_Malani_JLS_MortalPeril`, § 2.3 Caveats (J. Legal Stud., sole). **FLAG: one appositive em-dash pair; one "Moreover."**

> To be clear, I am not arguing that there are no health benefits to health insurance, just that the evidence is mixed. Moreover, insurance certainly has financial benefits, may encourage innovation, and may reduce deadweight loss from patents. These are real and important. It is also likely the case that health insurance would have an effect on health if other safety nets — for example, EMTALA — were eliminated. But at a macro level, insurance may or may not have a first-order health benefit and thereby justify a large expansion in public health insurance.

*Move:* the "To be clear, I am not arguing X, just Y" concession, granting the opponent's real
points ("These are real and important") before returning to the thesis with "But at a macro level…".
The concession is genuine, which is what makes the hold land.

### 7. Frame the question through a single concrete actor
**Source:** `2007_Malani_CaseForForProfitCharities`, Introduction (Va. L. Rev., Malani & Posner, lead). Clean (spaced-hyphenation rejoined).

> Consider the following hypothetical. An entrepreneur wants to establish a charity to improve the health of children in developing countries. Specifically, the business plan envisions developing technology to improve the quality and supply of water in African nations. Running this charity will require the entrepreneur's time and effort, for which she would like to be compensated out of the funds that the organization obtains from donations or revenues from any sales made in developed countries.

*Move:* opens on one concrete actor and follows her incentives; the abstract policy question is
built out of a single person's problem rather than stated in the abstract.

### EXT 8 (old). Instrumentalist counterintuitive proposal, first-person plural — *deprecated (duplicative of CORE 1)*
**Source:** `2013_MalaniMasur_GeoLJ_PatentDamages`, Introduction (Geo. L.J., Malani & Masur, lead). No em-dash. (OCR bolding of stray letters removed.)

> In this Article, we suggest that it might be possible to improve the value of patent challenges even without increasing their accuracy. Put simply, we propose raising the stakes involved in patent litigation. A patent owner who prevails at trial should collect enhanced rewards, above and beyond the damages the owner would normally be paid in compensation for the infringement. A patent owner whose patent is invalidated at trial should be forced to pay significantly enhanced penalties. At first glance, our proposal might seem entirely counterintuitive. If patent adjudications are riddled with errors, one would think that it would be preferable to lower the stakes involved, rather than increase them. Scholars and courts have generally confined themselves to that approach.

*Why:* same move as **CORE 1** — counterintuitive thesis stated flat, then the reader's objection
voiced and set up for the turn — restated in first-person-plural, co-authored form. The entry's own
note already named it "the co-authored twin of CORE 1," and it stops before CORE 1's dispatch ("But
the proper way…"), so it is the weaker exemplar of an identical technique. Kept as provenance that
the move recurs in co-authored work, not as a second injection target; promote it back if a
plural-voice exemplar is ever wanted for its own sake.
