## CORE (default injection — one per distinct archetype)

### 1. Explains a mechanism, plainly (the big idea as one moving part)
*Source: "The big puzzles of economic development," 2023-10-12 — https://anupmalani.substack.com/p/the-big-puzzles-of-economic-development*

> The big change in the Industrial Revolution was that instead of being takers, humans became creators. Instead of each new human (on average) taking resources away from other humans, each new human was (on average) producing more than she consumed. This happened because humans began to innovate, producing new tools and social organizations that increased the consumption they could extract from Earth's finite resources. We entered a so-called neoclassical economy where humans created additional output, which begat more humans, a cycle that made possible exponential economic growth.

**Anchor:** *Move: compress a causal story into one moving part, state it as a claim, then unpack it cause-by-cause.* The single most important fact in economic history reduced to one mechanism (takers → creators). No jargon left undefined, no ornament. His "explain the machine" register.

### 3. Re-anchors the reader's wrong comparison (the signature move)
*Source: "Why do urban slums get demolished?," 2024-11-14 — https://anupmalani.substack.com/p/why-do-urban-slums-get-demolished*

> Although urban slums in lower income countries appear, at first blush, to be immensely poor, I have come to realize that is not the case. The reason why urban slums seem poor to other urbanites is that they imagine themselves living in slums. If you live in a proper apartment outside a slum, even in a lower-income country, moving to a slum would be a huge downgrade. Smaller quarters, open sewers, no running water, public toilets. The problem with this train of thought is that it is misguided. The implicit comparison assumes that the reasonable alternative for a person living in a slum is to live outside the slum, in a proper apartment in the city. That is not realistic. While residents of slums are quite poor, their next best option is not living in the city, but rather in rural villages. That life involves unemployment, and even less access to water, sewers, and indoor toilets. And we know that life is worse. Because people in slums left their villages for the city. They voted for urban slums with their feet. Their life may be worse compared to ours but it is better than their other options.

**Anchor:** *Move: name the reader's wrong default comparison, then swap in the one that actually governs the answer.* His signature move — the intuitive-but-wrong frame is "they imagine themselves living in slums"; the correct comparison is the village, not the apartment; the close is plain ("voted for urban slums with their feet"). This is how he flips a reader's prior without ever raising his voice. The spine of the whole essay register.

### 4. Closes an argument (lands the payoff on a concrete case)
*Source: "Why do some countries have lots of slums and others hardly any?," 2023-02-08 — https://anupmalani.substack.com/p/why-do-some-countries-have-lots-of*

> Finally, the relationship between the drivers of slum populations and economic growth helps us understand why it might be that income growth, and not just narrow policies such as tenure rights or public housing, are critical to the elimination of slums. As cities get better at housing low-income migrants in their borders, they will attract additional, lower-income migrants from villages. The task of improving slums will seem Sisyphean. However, if the country can increase rural incomes, the new migrants will be able to afford formal housing when they arrive in cities. That is the pattern observed in developed countries, like the US after World War II.

**Anchor:** *Move: state the upshot, name why the obvious fix looks endless, resolve it, ground the resolution in a concrete case.* A real closer — the fix looks Sisyphean, the resolution is income growth, and the case is postwar US housing. Argument, not summary.

### 5. Concrete anecdote + dry wit (dismantles a bad definition)
*Source: "What is a slum?," 2022-10-22 — https://anupmalani.substack.com/p/what-is-a-slum*

> The problem with this definition is that it is overbroad. Historically, non-solid walls, inadequate roofs, and lack of electricity might characterize the majority of homes. It doesn't seem helpful to think of housing before 1900 as mainly slums. There are many homes that do not have running water, a private toilet, or working electricity. Many rural cabins do not have running water. My college dorm had communal toilets. And electricity is a problem across economic classes in low and middle-income countries. Heck, even in my reasonably well-off neighborhood (Wicker Park) in Chicago there is a mansion on the corner that was abandoned, has boarded-up windows, is not heated, leaks, and probably has non-functional sewers. I don't think people think of any of those dwellings as slums.

**Anchor:** *Move: dismantle a bad definition by piling on concrete counterexamples that escalate to a funny one.* Here the pile lands on the abandoned Wicker Park mansion. Clean by the blocklist, and shows his humor. ### E1. Curiosity-driven opener (his own reading, his own surprise)
*Source: "The geography of caste in India I," 2022-10-23 — https://anupmalani.substack.com/p/the-geography-of-caste-in-india-i*

> I was reading Oscar Lewis's wonderful "Village Life in Northern India" and he mentioned that some villages are organized around a single caste — his example Jat farming villages in Haryana. This surprised me. First, my experience — from running surveys in hundreds of villages — is that villages typically have multiple castes, though they are segregated into different hamlets. Second, I had a simple mental model of castes — actually jati's, a further partition of caste — as occupations. I assumed that the 4 Varnas and the Dalits roughly covered the set of occupations that a society needs. That might imply each caste would be represented in a village.

**Anchor:** *Move: narrate your own learning — read something, get surprised, check it against your own evidence.* A second, quieter opener (reading-driven, and unpacked immediately via enumerated reasons) distinct from CORE #2 (podcast-driven, and deferred as a hook). *Curation note (2026-07-27), borderline — flagged, not cut: both entries open on a piece of consumed media that surprised him, making this the closest pair in the bank; kept because E1's payoff (checking the surprise against his own fieldwork data) is a move CORE #2 doesn't make. ### E2. Participatory demonstration (hands the reader a do-it-yourself proof)
*Source: "Why do urban slums get demolished?," 2024-11-14 — https://anupmalani.substack.com/p/why-do-urban-slums-get-demolished*

> Nor are slums stuck in the mud of poverty. They are actually communities with remarkable rates of economic growth. And it is easy to convince yourself of this. Go online to Google Earth. Pick out a slum, especially one in the periphery of a city. Now, use Google Earth's history feature to look at the same spot 5 or 10 years ago. There is a very good chance you will see a greenfield—empty space filled with grass or a forest. Now, use Google Earth's timeline feature to walk forward through the slums' history. You will see the field cleared and become a brownfield, and then some tents go up. Then the entire are will be filled with homes, and some will be converted to mud or even cinder block. Later, you will see all the homes convert to cinderblock, and some grow to 2 stories. It is likely this will occur in less than a decade. Now compare that to your own life. Have you experienced an improvement in your lifestyle comparable to going from living in a tent in an empty field to living in a dense community with multi-story masonry structures? That is real growth, not tinkering at the edges of a comfortable lifestyle.

**Anchor:** *Move: hand the reader a do-it-yourself demonstration they can run themselves to verify your claim.* Here it's Google Earth's timeline — arguably his most charming teaching move, and one that bridges into the explainer register's "address you" habit. *Flag: carries three blocklist features (an em-dash pause §9, a rhetorical question §6, and an "X, not Y" ending §1) — all authentically his, which is why it sits in EXTENDED. Also a small typo, "the entire are."*

### E3. Self-undermining institution (a crisp earned line)
*Source: "A political theory on the development of slums," 2023-06-25 — https://anupmalani.substack.com/p/a-political-theory-on-the-development*

> In the first part of the play, city bureaucracies make master plans for a city that guides their investments in infrastructure. This plan imagines residential and commercial parts of the city. Often these bureaucracies intentionally or unintentionally plan a smaller city than will actually exist, intentionally if they prefer a low-density city and unintentionally if they simply underestimate the extent of future urbanization. This part is surprising because the bureaucracy actually wants a productive city that generates high income, but that success attracts more density than the bureaucracy wants or expects. Success creates conditions for the plan's failure.

**Anchor:** *Move: explain an institution that defeats itself, and land the paradox on one crisp, earned line.* Here: "Success creates conditions for the plan's failure." Distinct from CORE #3: the counterintuition is structural (the institution defeats itself), not a reader-comparison flip.

### E4. Perspective-shift on scale (re-scales a familiar worry)
*Source: "The big puzzles of economic development," 2023-10-12 — https://anupmalani.substack.com/p/the-big-puzzles-of-economic-development*

> It is hard to relate to the idea that we live in miraculous times. In the US we worry about recessions and low growth rates relative to, e.g., China. It is sometimes hard to imagine things could be worse. But if human history were to fit into one day, all but the last second or so would involve basically no growth. As Greg Clark puts it "the average person in the world of 1800 was no better off than the average person of 100,000 BC" (p. 1). Compared to that, the United States average 2% growth over the last 150 years, a rate that doubles our per capita income every 36 years, is a continuous miracle.

**Anchor:** *Move: compress a vast quantity into one relatable unit to make scale legible.* Fits 200,000 years of human history into "one day," then re-scales a familiar worry (recessions) against it, landing on a hard number (income doubles every 36 years). *Curation note (2026-07-27), borderline — flagged, not folded in: this is related to CORE #3's re-anchoring move, but the device is distinct (temporal compression as analogy, not a lived spatial comparison), so it stays as its own entry — the second-closest call in the bank after E1/CORE #2. Anup reviewed it on 2026-07-27 and kept it.* *Flag: the original had a raw un-rendered citation code after the Clark quote — removed here; confirm before use.*

## Through-line of the essay register

He **re-anchors the reader's wrong default comparison**: he names the intuitive frame you already hold (you imagine yourself living in a slum; we worry about recessions), then swaps in the comparison that actually governs the answer (the village, not the apartment; 200,000 years, not this decade). Around that spine sit four habits: he grounds abstractions in something concrete and usually personal (a podcast that "upended" his view, the Wicker Park mansion, running surveys in hundreds of villages); he leads with the claim and unpacks it cause-by-cause; he closes plainly, sometimes with dry wit, rather than on a flourish; and he is comfortable admitting his own view was wrong.
