# Appellate Voice — The Systematic Techniques (curated for transfer)

Paragraphs below are quoted **verbatim** from John Roberts, Jeffrey Sutton, and Paul Clement —
writers whose craft Anup's own `~/.claude/refs/appellate-style.md` is distilled from. This is a bank
of prose to **aspire to and imitate**, not a sample of his own voice (for his own registers see
`academic.md` and `social.md`). Each entry is a *technique*, not a nice turn of phrase — a pattern
that recurs across good briefs and that Anup could lift into a policy memo.

## The master principle: brutal clarity

A judge with eighty briefs on the desk will not work to understand you. **The worst thing a brief
can do is make the reader stop and re-read a sentence.** Ambiguous antecedents, buried subjects,
constructions that have to be decoded — each one costs the reader effort and costs you the argument.
So every technique below serves one goal before persuasion: *the reader understands on the first
pass, fast.* Persuasion rides on comprehension.

The purest expression of the principle is **topic sentences** (T1). If a reader runs her eye down
only the first sentence of every paragraph, she should come away with the whole argument. That is
~100% true of good SG briefs, and it is the discipline the rest of these techniques feed.

## The bar for inclusion (three prongs)

An entry stays only if all three hold. Fail any one and it is deprecated (see the Deprecated
section), and replaced by a cleaner exemplar of the same technique where one exists.
1. **Systematic and common** — a recurring appellate strategy, not a one-off flourish or a lucky
   phrasing.
2. **Distinct** — it does something the other techniques don't. Near-duplicates are merged; a bank
   with five ways to say "find the real question" adds nothing after the first.
3. **Transferable** — the pattern would improve a memo or policy brief with nothing to do with law.

Each entry's note is a `*Move:*` line stated for a memo; law-flavored entries add a `*Leave behind:*`
line for the advocacy habit not to import.

## Transcription conventions

Quoted **verbatim**. In-line record/case citations that interrupt the prose are dropped at sentence
boundaries; mid-passage elisions marked `[…]`. Sources retain their own punctuation (em-dashes,
the occasional rhetorical question) — imitate the front-loading, the concrete image, the varied
cadence, the honest concession, **not** the dashes or the questions (`appellate-style.md` governs).
Each entry is tagged **[brief]** or **[opinion]**; former entry IDs are kept in parentheses for
traceability. Full sourcing trail is in the provenance footer.

## THE TECHNIQUES

### T1. Topic sentences carry the argument [brief]  *(newly mined)*
**Source:** Paul D. Clement (Solicitor General), Brief for the United States as Amicus Curiae, *District of Columbia v. Heller*, No. 07-290 (U.S. 2008), Summary of Argument §§ A–C, pp. 8–10.

The opening sentence of each paragraph of Clement's Summary of Argument, in order (openers only, verbatim). Read just these and you have the whole brief:

> 1. The court of appeals correctly held that the Second Amendment protects an individual right to possess firearms unrelated to militia operations.
> 2. The Second Amendment's prefatory language, which refers to the "necess[ity]" of a "well regulated Militia," does not negate the Amendment's operative guarantee.
> 3. Although the court of appeals correctly held that the Second Amendment protects an individual right, it did not apply the correct standard for evaluating respondent's Second Amendment claim.
> 4. When, as here, a law directly limits the private possession of "Arms" in a way that has no grounding in Framing-era practice, the Second Amendment requires that the law be subject to heightened scrutiny.
> 5. The court of appeals, by contrast, appears to have adopted a more categorical approach.
> 6. Given that the D.C. Code provisions at issue ban a commonly-used and commonly-possessed firearm in a way that has no grounding in Framing-era practice, those provisions warrant close scrutiny under the analysis described above and may well fail such scrutiny.

*Move:* the single most important clarity discipline — open every paragraph with a sentence that states its point, so a reader who reads *only* the first sentences gets the entire argument. It is Anup's own topic-sentence rule (`~/.claude/CLAUDE.md`; `ai-tells.md`) shown at brief length: the six openers above are a complete syllogism (individual right → the prefatory clause doesn't undo it → but the test is heightened scrutiny, not categorical invalidation → here is that test → the court below went too categorical → so the ban likely fails the right test). A memo built this way can be understood from its topic sentences alone; the body only supplies proof. Ubiquitous in SG briefs; the backbone of the register. *(Verified in the same form in Verrilli's NFIB severability SoA and the body of Clement's Bruen argument — this is the house style, not one brief's trick.)*

### T2. Frame the issue so your answer is the obvious one [opinion + brief]  *(was #1 + A2)*
**Source (a), the reframe:** Sutton, *DeBoer v. Snyder*, 772 F.3d 388 (6th Cir. 2014), opening. Slip op. 14a0275p-06, pp. 7–8. **Source (b), the pivot:** Roberts, Brief for Petitioner, *Alaska v. EPA*, No. 02-658 (U.S. 2003), Summary of Argument, p. 20.

> (a) This is a case about change—and how best to handle it under the United States Constitution. From the vantage point of 2014, it would now seem, the question is not whether American law will allow gay couples to marry; it is when and how that will happen.

> (b) At bottom, the EPA overturned the State's decision not because it believed that Alaska had failed to provide a "reasoned justification" for its BACT determination, but rather simply because the EPA preferred a different technology—SCR.

*Move:* whoever frames the question usually wins it. State the issue so that your answer is the natural one — (a) recharacterize what the dispute is *about* ("the question is not whether … it is when and how"), or (b) boil a tangled record or dataset down to the one thing actually in dispute ("not because X, but rather simply because Y"). Same technique at two altitudes. A memo lives or dies on this: name the real question in one sentence and the rest organizes itself. (Both use the "not X but Y" form inside a full clause where "not X" carries a real fact — the permitted form.) The most common opening strategy in the register.

### T3. Answer first, then roadmap the steps (BLUF) [brief]  *(was CL1)*
**Source:** Clement, Brief for Petitioners, *NYSRPA v. Bruen*, No. 20-843 (U.S. 2021), Summary of Argument, p. 22.

> New York's denial of petitioners' applications for licenses to carry handguns for self-defense plainly violated their rights under the Second Amendment. That conclusion is compelled by the text, history, and tradition of the Second Amendment, all of which confirm that the right it secures encompasses a right to carry handguns outside the home for self-defense.
>
> The text of the Second Amendment guarantees a right "to keep and bear arms." It is elementary that "to bear arms implies something more than the mere keeping." Otherwise, those words would serve no purpose at all, which would violate the cardinal principle of constitutional interpretation. Their purpose and meaning is clear; the reference to bearing arms secures the pre-existing, fundamental right to "carry weapons in case of confrontation." Of course, confrontations and the need for self-defense—at the time of the founding and today—are hardly limited to the home. To confine the Second Amendment to the home or keeping arms thus would defy both its text and common sense.

*Move:* bottom line up front — state the conclusion in sentence one, then, in the same breath, name the ground it rests on ("compelled by the text, history, and tradition … all of which confirm …"), which previews the structure of what follows. The reader never has to hold a question open waiting for the answer. The standard policy-memo opening; the transferable part is the *order* (conclusion, then support), not the textual method the support happens to use. *Leave behind:* "the cardinal principle of constitutional interpretation" — a term of art; say it plainly.

### T4. State the facts — or the statistics — as a story [brief]  *(was #2)*
**Source:** John G. Roberts, Jr., Brief for Petitioner, *Alaska Dep't of Envtl. Conservation v. EPA*, No. 02-658 (U.S. 2003), Statement of the Case ("The Red Dog Mine"), pp. 8–9. Hogan & Hartson.

> For generations, Inupiat Eskimos hunting and fishing in the DeLong Mountains in Northwest Alaska had been aware of orange- and red-stained creekbeds in which fish could not survive. In the 1960s, a bush pilot and part-time prospector by the name of Bob Baker noticed striking discolorations in the hills and creekbeds of a wide valley in the western DeLongs. Unable to land his plane on the rocky tundra to investigate, Baker alerted the U.S. Geological Survey. Exploration of the area eventually led to the discovery of a wealth of zinc and lead deposits. Although Baker died before the significance of his observations became known, his faithful traveling companion—an Irish Setter who often flew shotgun—was immortalized by a geologist who dubbed the creek Baker had spotted "Red Dog" Creek.

*Move:* present the facts (or the data) as a story with a subject the reader can follow — a discolored creek, a bush pilot, a dog who flew shotgun — so the reader is oriented and on your side before any argument. The advocacy is in what you select and the order you tell it, never asserted. A policy brief opens the same way: one concrete, well-chosen case (or a single figure told as a narrative) does work an abstract framing can't. The canonical statement-of-facts technique.

### T5. Make the abstraction concrete with one image [brief]  *(was #3)*
**Source:** John G. Roberts, Jr., Brief for Petitioner, *Alaska Dep't of Envtl. Conservation v. EPA*, No. 02-658 (U.S. 2003), Argument, pp. 24–25.

> Determining the "best" control technology is like asking different people to pick the "best" car. Mario Andretti may select a Ferrari; a college student may choose a Volkswagen Beetle; a family of six a mini-van. A Minnesotan's choice will doubtless have four-wheel drive; a Floridian's might well be a convertible. The choices would turn on how the decisionmaker weighed competing priorities such as cost, mileage, safety, cargo space, speed, handling, and so on. Substituting one decisionmaker for another may yield a different result, but not in any sense a more "correct" one. So too here.

*Move:* carry an abstraction the reader would have to work to grasp on a picture the reader gets instantly — "the best control technology is like asking different people to pick the best car" — then snap back to the point in three words ("So too here."). Any memo explaining why a judgment is discretionary, or why reasonable people weigh factors differently, can borrow it. Common in briefs, and one of the highest clarity-per-word moves there is.

### T6. Cabin the claim — say what you do NOT argue [brief]  *(was CL2)*
**Source:** Clement (Solicitor General), Brief for the United States as Amicus Curiae, *District of Columbia v. Heller*, No. 07-290 (U.S. 2008), Summary of Argument § B, pp. 8–9.

> Although the court of appeals correctly held that the Second Amendment protects an individual right, it did not apply the correct standard for evaluating respondent's Second Amendment claim. Like other provisions of the Constitution that secure individual rights, the Second Amendment's protection of individual rights does not render all laws limiting gun ownership automatically invalid. To the contrary, the Second Amendment, properly construed, allows for reasonable regulation of firearms, must be interpreted in light of context and history, and is subject to important exceptions, such as the rule that convicted felons may be denied firearms because those persons have never been understood to be within the Amendment's protections. Nothing in the Second Amendment properly understood—and certainly no principle necessary to decide this case—calls for invalidation of the numerous federal laws regulating firearms.

*Move:* draw the boundary of your own claim before you press it — "this does not mean the sweeping X; it means the narrower Y." Naming the limit removes the reader's biggest objection (that you're claiming too much) before it forms, and tells the reader exactly how far the holding travels. The same refuse-to-overclaim move as Anup's own economics bank (`academic-econ.md` EXT 6). A memo earns trust this way: say what your recommendation does *not* require before you argue for it. Standard in briefs seeking a limited, winnable holding. *Leave behind:* almost nothing; a clean grounding on its own.

### T7. Steelman the other side, then refute [brief]  *(was CL4)*
**Source:** Clement, Brief for Petitioners, *NYSRPA v. Bruen*, No. 20-843 (U.S. 2021), Statement of the Case § A, pp. 5–6.

> To be sure, the English right was not unfettered. For one thing, it was subjected to religious- and class-based distinctions—limitations that thankfully did not cross the Atlantic. The right to carry arms was also understood as compatible with the 1328 Statute of Northampton, which prohibited subjects from "bring[ing] ... force in affray of the peace" and enjoined them not "to go nor ride armed by night nor by day, in fairs, markets, nor in the presence of the justices or other ministers, nor in no part elsewhere, upon pain to forfeit their armour to the King, and their bodies to prison at the King's pleasure."

*Move:* state the other side's strongest point at full strength ("To be sure, …") before you answer it — a real concession, not a straw one, which is what makes the turn land and what stops the reader from mounting the objection herself. The core rebuttal technique for any policy brief: give the best case against your recommendation its due, then meet it. Universal in good briefs. *Leave behind:* the 1328-statute quotation is genre-bound — in a memo you concede a rival's *finding*, not medieval text.

### T8. Vary the rhythm; land the point on a short, plain sentence [brief]  *(was CL5)*
**Source:** Clement, Brief of State Petitioners on Medicaid, *Florida v. HHS*, No. 11-400 (U.S. 2012), Argument, pp. 22–23.

> Just as it is clear that there must be a judicially enforceable limit on Congress' spending power, it is equally clear that the ACA exceeds it. While difficult cases will surely arise about when persuasion crosses the line into coercion, this is not one of them. Congress itself recognized that the Medicaid expansion was not truly voluntary when it made that expansion critical to compliance with the individual mandate. Congress created a mandate for all individuals to obtain insurance while providing no alternative to Medicaid for the most needy to obtain the mandated insurance. Simply put, a program that is necessary for the satisfaction of a mandate is not voluntary. It is mandatory.

*Move:* after several long, qualified sentences, land the point on a short flat one the reader can't misread — "It is mandatory." The variation itself is the clarity device: the short sentence signals "this is the takeaway," and its plainness leaves no room to misparse. (This entry also carries the honest-concession pattern — concede the general question is hard, then commit on the case in front of you — but the systematic, portable feature is the rhythm.) Common across briefs and worth deliberate use in a memo's key sentences. *Leave behind:* the flat certainty of "It is mandatory" is advocacy; a memo commits only as far as its evidence.

## Deprecated (fails the three-prong bar)

Kept verbatim for the record; out of the active bank. Reason keyed to the prong each fails. Where
the technique is worth keeping, it survives in a cleaner entry (noted).

### A3. Sutton — "clear away what is not the question" [opinion]  — *deprecated (duplicative)*
**Source:** Sutton, *DeBoer v. Snyder*, 772 F.3d 388 (6th Cir. 2014). Slip op. 14a0275p-06, p. 8.
> Of all the ways to resolve this question, one option is not available: a poll of the three judges on this panel … What we have authority to decide instead is a legal question: Does the Fourteenth Amendment … prohibit a State from defining marriage as a relationship between one man and one woman?

*Why:* this is issue-framing (negative form) — the same technique as **T2**. A third framing exemplar adds nothing after T2's two. Merged into T2.

### A6. Sutton — "repeat the noun for emphasis" [opinion]  — *deprecated (bespoke, not systematic)*
**Source:** Sutton, concurring in *Bormuth v. County of Jackson*, 870 F.3d 494 (6th Cir. 2017) (en banc). Slip op. 17a0207p-06, p. 41.
> … A complaint is not evidence. Briefs are not evidence.

*Why:* a lovely one-off, but "repeat the noun instead of a pronoun" is a bespoke sentence-tactic, not a recurring strategy. The broader systematic pattern it gestures at — parallel structure for clarity — is worth an exemplar if a cleaner, more clearly *patterned* one turns up; this isn't it.

### #5 (old). Sutton — stance-closing / peroration [opinion]  — *deprecated (opinion-only, not a common brief technique)*
**Source:** Sutton, *DeBoer v. Snyder*, 772 F.3d 388 (6th Cir. 2014), closing. Slip op. 14a0275p-06, pp. 41–42.
> … Better in this instance, we think, to allow change through the customary political processes, in which the people, gay and straight alike, become the heroes of their own stories by meeting each other not as adversaries in a court system but as fellow citizens seeking to resolve a new social issue in a fair-minded way.

*Why:* a beautiful peroration, but the elaborate stance-closing is a *judicial-opinion* habit; briefs almost always close in two workmanlike sentences ("For the foregoing reasons, the judgment should be reversed."). Not systematic in the register we're modeling. If a "close on the stakes" technique is ever wanted, mine a brief *conclusion*, not an opinion's ending.

### A1 (old). Roberts — "governing frame, then structure" [brief]  — *deprecated (duplicative)*
**Source:** Roberts, Brief for Petitioner, *Alaska v. EPA*, No. 02-658, Summary of Argument, p. 17.
> The CAA is based on a "division of responsibilities" between the States and the federal government. Under this scheme of "cooperative federalism," … Determining BACT for particular sources is a key means by which the States weigh potentially competing demands …

*Why:* orienting the reader to the framework before arguing is real, but here it overlaps **T3** (answer first, then roadmap) and **T1** (topic-sentence discipline) without adding a distinct move, and it's the driest passage of the set. Folded into T3.

### #4 (old). Roberts — concession-then-answer, "canary in the mine shaft" [brief]  — *deprecated (law-bound; duplicative)*
**Source:** Roberts, Brief for Petitioner, *Alaska v. EPA*, No. 02-658, Summary of Argument, pp. 19–20.
> None of this is to say that there is no cure for the problem the EPA alleges … The awkwardness of considering whether the EPA was arbitrary or capricious in deciding that the State was arbitrary or capricious should be the canary in the mine shaft, signaling that something is very much amiss.

*Why:* the concession-then-turn move is **T7**, cleaner. This exemplar is buried in PSD-permit and arbitrary-or-capricious detail — it reads as regulatory law, not a portable pattern.

### A4 (old). Sutton — chiastic reversal, "History judges us. We do not judge history." [opinion]  — *deprecated (niche flourish)*
**Source:** Sutton, concurring in *Bormuth v. County of Jackson*, 870 F.3d 494 (6th Cir. 2017) (en banc). Slip op. 17a0207p-06, p. 37.
> … History judges us in this area. We do not judge history.

*Why:* a chiasmus — a rhetorical flourish useful in few situations, not a common structural strategy. Nothing relies on it.

### A5 (old). Sutton — line-drawing by rhetorical questions [opinion]  — *deprecated (fights house style)*
**Source:** Sutton, concurring in *Bormuth v. County of Jackson*, 870 F.3d 494 (6th Cir. 2017) (en banc). Slip op. 17a0207p-06, p. 40.
> And what of a legislator who is also a person of the cloth? Could John Danforth but not John McCain give an invocation? …

*Why:* the reductio is fine, but it's delivered through stacked rhetorical questions — a habit Anup's house style caps at one per section (`ai-tells.md` §6). Re-source as a declarative if the move is ever wanted.

### CL3 (old). Clement — hub/spokes image (severability) [brief]  — *deprecated (duplicative; law-bound)*
**Source:** Clement, Brief for State Petitioners on Severability, *NFIB v. Sebelius*, Nos. 11-393 & 11-400 (U.S. 2012), Summary of Argument, pp. 24–25.
> … This Court cannot remove the hub of the individual mandate while leaving the spokes in place without violating Congress' evident intent.

*Why:* same technique as **T5** (concrete image), but wrapped in "severability is a remedial inquiry that turns on legislative intent" — drier and more law-bound than the best-car image. Redundant with T5, the cleaner paragon.
