# Academic Voice — ECONOMICS-Journal Register (Anup Malani)

Paragraphs below are quoted **verbatim** from Anup Malani's own economics papers (corpus at
`assistants/research-manager/projects/style-analysis/corpus/`). Each teaches a distinct
rhetorical or sentence-level move. **CORE** is the default injection set — one paragraph per
archetype. **EXTENDED** holds additional non-redundant keepers.

**Transcription conventions.** Superscript footnote-reference numbers are dropped. A word split
across a line/page break is rejoined. Working-paper (NBER) sources are not yet copyedited; where
a passage carries a drafting typo, the text is transcribed **as printed** and the typo flagged
[sic]. No silent corrections.

**Register vs. the law bank.** This is the terser, formal, results-first Malani. He defines a
construct in plain prose, reframes it in economic vocabulary, walks a design through in the second
person ("Suppose the researcher also has…"), coins a mechanism and reuses it as a section-level
lens, and reports magnitudes to two decimals before any interpretation. Em-dashes are far rarer
here than in the law bank; the two that appear (CORE 2, EXT 7) are appositive and flagged. One
authentic rhetorical question appears in EXT 7, flagged per `ai-tells.md` §6.

the phenomenon in plain prose, reframes it in economic categories, coins and reuses a crisp
mechanism label, walks the design through step by step, reports the magnitude first and interprets
second, and hedges scope explicitly ("Our point is not that…") while still stating a preferred result.

## CORE

### 1. Walk the design through, one controlled change at a time (second person)
**Source:** `2006_Malani_JPE_PlaceboEffects`, Introduction (J. Pol. Econ., sole). Clean text.

> In this paper I present a simple insight about medical trials that suggests a novel test for placebo effects that overcomes the objections to prior tests for placebo effects. If a researcher has a single-blinded, randomized, controlled medical trial and she compares outcomes in the treatment arm with outcomes in the control arm, a "within-trial" comparison, it is well known that she will obtain the pharmacological effect of treatment. Suppose, however, that the researcher also has a second clinical trial of the same medication, with the same control and design. The only difference is that the second trial employs a higher probability of assignment to treatment. The main implication of this modification is that patients in the higher-probability trial will believe that they are more likely to receive treatment. It also implies that patients in the higher-probability trial will expect better health outcomes from their clinical trial, all other things being equal.

*Move:* turns a formal result into a story the reader can follow — the design walked through in the
second person, one controlled change at a time ("The only difference is…"), each step's implication
named before the next.

### 2. Grant the conventional wisdom at full strength, then turn
**Source:** `2008_Malani_JMetrics_PatientEnrollment`, opening (J. Econometrics, sole). **FLAG: one appositive em-dash** ("the treated — i.e.").

> Conventional wisdom holds that patient self-selection does not pose a problem for randomized medical trials. The reason is that random assignment eliminates unobservable differences between treatment groups. This is sufficient protection against bias if the researcher only cares about the effect of treatment on the treated — i.e. the effect of treatment on patients enrolled in the trial. Most trials, however, are concerned with predicting the effect of treatment on a patient population beyond the trial. In these cases, randomization does not prevent selection bias which arises from systematic differences between the trial population and the general patient population.

*Move:* states the opposing view at full strength and grants exactly the case where it is right
("This is sufficient protection… if the researcher only cares about the effect of treatment on the
treated"), then turns on the pivot ("Most trials, however…"). The concession is real, which makes
the rebuttal land.

### 3. Report the finding as a comparison; let the magnitude do the arguing
**Source:** `2015_MalaniReif_JPubEc_Anticipation`, Introduction (J. Pub. Econ., Malani & Reif, lead). Clean text.

> Our second contribution is that we explore the practical implications of the foregoing analysis in an empirical application. Specifically, we estimate the effect of punitive damage caps on equilibrium physician supply and show that accounting for anticipation could increase their estimated effect by a factor of two or more compared to a model that ignores anticipation. We first estimate a model that ignores anticipation and thus corresponds to prior analyses of tort reform, e.g., Klick and Stratmann (2007). We find that caps on punitive damages have a positive treatment effect on physician supply of 1.1% after implementation of caps. Then, we interpret the pre-period trends visible in Fig. 1 as evidence of anticipation effects and estimate the different regression models discussed in our framework. We find that damage caps have a 1.5 to 2.6% post-implementation effect after accounting for all prior anticipation effects. In addition, we estimate that damage caps had a 0.9 to 1.9% effect in each of the two years immediately preceding reform. By contrast, prior models implicitly assume zero treatment effects prior to reform.

*Move:* numbers reported plainly and immediately cashed against the alternative ("By contrast, prior
models implicitly assume zero treatment effects prior to reform"). The finding is framed as a
comparison, not a boast; the magnitude does the arguing.

### 4. Open on a taxonomy that scaffolds the whole paper
**Source:** `2024_Malani_NBERw33175_SurvivingChildren`, Introduction ¶1 (NBER wp, Malani & Jacob, lead). **FLAG: NBER working paper, pre-copyedit — two [sic] typos below.**

> Changes in fertility can be grouped into two buckets: changes that hold the number of surviving children produced by households constant and changes that alter number [sic] of surviving children per household. The first bucket merely compensates for changes in child survival rates. It has few first-order effects, positive or negative, on human welfare. While classical economic theory, such as the Malthusian model, can explain first-bucket changes, neo-classical theories focuses [sic] on changes in the number of surviving children.

*Move:* a plain taxonomy ("two buckets") stated in sentence one that then organizes the entire
paper — returned to in the empirics and the conclusion. His spare, current register. (Transcribed as
printed; the two grammatical slips are the working-paper's, not corrected here.)

### 5. Coin a mechanism, then reuse it as the paper's analytic lens
**Source:** `2019_MalaniPhilipson_JHC_TrialSubjectMarket`, § I (J. Health Econ., Malani & Philipson, lead). Clean text (one extraction spacing fix: "effect," is).

> We argue in Section I, however, that an important difference between medical and nonmedical R&D is a unique linkage between medical output markets and medical R&D inputs. This linkage is driven by the twin facts that medical R&D requires clinical trials on human subjects and that individuals who can serve as human subjects are also potential consumers of medical products as patients in the output market. In other words, input supply for medical R&D overlaps with output demand for medical products. The central implication of this overlap, which we term the "subject supply effect," is that improvements in output markets, for example, an increase in quality or a reduction in price of conventional medical care, make patients more reluctant to enroll as subjects in clinical trials.

*Move:* names the mechanism once ("which we term the 'subject supply effect'"), re-glosses it in lay
terms ("In other words, input supply … overlaps with output demand"), then carries that single label
as an analytic lens through the rest of the paper, including section headings.

## EXTENDED

### 6. Refuse to overclaim ("Our point is not that…")
**Source:** `2015_MalaniReif_JPubEc_Anticipation`, Introduction (J. Pub. Econ., Malani & Reif, lead). Clean text.

> Our point is not that the pre-trends in Fig. 1 must be anticipation rather than endogeneity. Rather, we argue that there is no good reason to estimate the treatment effect of punitive damage caps on physician supply assuming that pre-trends can only be evidence of endogeneity. They could also be due to anticipation. This matters because how one interprets these pre-trends has substantial implications for how one estimates treatment effects and how large those estimates are. For example, a researcher who does not account for anticipation effects with the same sign as post-adoption effects of a policy will underestimate the full treatment effect of that policy.

*Move:* states precisely what he is *not* arguing, then narrows to the claim he will defend. Recurs
across the corpus ("To be clear," "We do not endorse any particular parameterization").

### 7. Raise the strongest objection to your own method, in your own voice
**Source:** `2006_Malani_JPE_PlaceboEffects`, Introduction (J. Pol. Econ., sole). **FLAG: one authentic rhetorical question** (ai-tells §6).

> A natural concern with this test is whether it is robust to self-selection of patients into clinical trials. In other words, do trials with different probabilities of treatment attract the same patients, and if not, does a comparison of treatment groups properly identify placebo effects? A problem would arise if the higher-probability trial attracts patients who would have a better response to both treatment and placebo. In that case, one could not determine whether the test I propose identified self-selection or placebo effects.

*Move:* names the strongest objection to his own method ("A natural concern with this test is…")
before an adversary can, then works through exactly when it would bite. The rhetorical question is
his, not a setup for cadence — flagged for the register-exception decision.

### 8. State a counterintuitive result, flag it against named institutional wisdom
**Source:** `2011_MalaniLaxminarayan_JHR_IncentivesDiseaseReporting`, Introduction (J. Human Resources, Malani & Laxminarayan, lead). Clean text ("United Nation's" [sic] as printed).

> Second, improving the quality of a country's domestic surveillance may discourage a country from disclosing the results of that surveillance. This finding contradicts accepted wisdom at public health organizations such as WHO, the United Nation's Food and Agricultural Organization (FAO), and the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). Improving the predictive value of diagnostic testing lowers the likelihood of false positive results and thereby increases a country's confidence that medical assistance following disclosure will save lives. But improved testing also increases trading partners' confidence that a positive test result truly indicates the presence of an outbreak and therefore lowers trade following disclosure. If the trade response exceeds the expected gain from medical assistance, there will be less truthful disclosure.

*Move:* states a counterintuitive formal result, flags that it contradicts named institutions
(WHO, FAO, CDC), then gives the two-sided mechanism in plain prose — better testing cuts false
positives but also raises the trade penalty for reporting.

### 10. Report an RCT result in the pure empirical register
**Source:** `2024_Malani_NBERw32239_PricingHealthInsuranceIndia`, Introduction (NBER wp, sole). NBER wp; "effects" for "affects" upstream [sic].

> We find that insurance enrollment significantly and substantially increased utilization of insurance. Access to free insurance increased 6-month utilization of RSBY insurance by 6.73 pp over a control group mean of 3.86% at 18 months, a 74% increase in insurance utilization. However, enrollment had insubstantial and statistically insignificant effects on use of hospital care. Hospital utilization rose only 10-15%, and even that estimate is imprecise. Combined, the insurance and hospital utilization effects suggest the predominant benefit of insurance is reducing financial risk.

*Move:* the field-experiment results register — state the effect, report the magnitude against a
control-group mean in percentage points, then interpret in one plain sentence ("suggest the
predominant benefit of insurance is reducing financial risk"). Magnitude first, meaning second.
**FLAG (dedupe pass, 2026-07-27):** this overlaps with CORE 3's "let the magnitude do the arguing"
principle — both report a number and let it carry the interpretation. Kept as a separate entry
because the comparison target differs (a control-group mean typical of a field experiment, vs.
CORE 3's comparison to a rival model's implicit assumption). The overlap is real, but Anup reviewed the pair on 2026-07-27 and chose to keep both — the field-experiment results register earns its own exemplar.

### EXT 9 (old). Name a welfare concept, then decompose it — *deprecated (duplicative of CORE 5)*
**Source:** `2004_MalaniHu_OptionValueNewTherapeutics`, Introduction (working paper, Malani & Hu, lead). "Food and Drug Agency" [sic] as printed in this draft.

> The U.S. Food and Drug Agency (FDA) employs a simple decision-rule when deciding whether to approve a new drug for use by physicians: the average treatment effect of the new drug must be superior to the average effect of placebo. If there is ex post heterogeneity in treatment effects, however, this decision rule may reject drugs that have positive social value. If a drug does not ultimately have the same effect on each and every patient, then there may be some patients who are better off with the new drug than with placebo or conventional treatment, even though the average patient may be better off with placebo. We call this lost social value the option value of the drug because, if the drug is not approved, doctors will not have the option of using it on patients for whom other treatments are inferior.

*Why:* same move as **CORE 5** — coin a construct, then ground the coinage in exactly what it
captures that the naive account misses. The entry's own note already said so ("Same coinage
instinct as CORE 5, in the terser theory register"). CORE 5 is the stronger exemplar because it
also shows the coined label ("subject supply effect") reused as the paper's running analytic lens,
a fuller demonstration of the technique than this entry's single definition. Kept as provenance,
not as a second injection target.
