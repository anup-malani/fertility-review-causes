Perceptions of Medically Assisted Reproduction and
Fertility Postponement: Limited Evidence, Critical
Questions

Abstract
The number of women using medically assisted reproduction (MAR) is rising in
high-income countries. This trend is partly driven by the tendency to delay child-
bearing, yet infertility treatments cannot compensate for the decrease in reproduc-
tive capacity with age. Scholars are currently concerned that the increasing avail-
ability of MAR is fuelling the misconception that it can extend reproductive life.
High confidence in the success of MAR later in life would then encourage fertility
postponement. However, this assumption remains untested, likely due to limited
data availability. We examine this using panel survey data from the National Survey
of Fertility Barriers, conducted in the United States in 2004–2007 and 2007–2010.
Our findings support the technological salience hypothesis, which states that per-
ceptions of MAR would only influence fertility intentions at ages when women are
more likely to experience difficulties conceiving. Specifically, women aged 35 or
over who have high confidence in the success of MAR when used in the late 30s are
less likely to forego childbearing than those with low confidence. Overall, however,
perceptions of MAR play a limited role in explaining fertility decisions compared
to other socio-demographic characteristics. Nonetheless, taking them into account
improves our understanding of how technologies enter the realm of reproductive
decision-making, particularly at less fertile ages. In conclusion, we highlight the
need for further data collection on this issue, covering more recent periods and
countries where births are delayed and MAR availability is increasing.

1 Introduction

Many countries are introducing population policies aimed at increasing their fertility
rates, which have fallen below 1.5 children per woman in many cases (Gietel-Basten
et al., 2022). Alongside the usual family policy tools, such as parental leave, child
allowances or childcare subsidies, some countries have increased public coverage of
medically assisted reproduction (MAR)1 to enable more couples who face infertility
issues to have children.2 The demographic impact of MAR increases with its avail-
ability, and its contribution to domestic births is substantial in some contexts, ranging
between 4 and 7% in 2019 (Devolder & Borisova, 2022; Smeenk et al., 2023).
    While this makes MAR an attractive tool for policymakers, improving its availabil-
ity does not appear to be a long-term solution to support fertility levels (Kocourková
et al., 2014; Sobotka et al., 2008; Ziebe & Devroey, 2008). First, the success rate
of reproductive technologies is limited. For example, in Australia, it was estimated
that 52–65% of women who initiated a treatment would experience a live birth by
the fourth ART cycle (Chambers et al., 2017). Second, the demand for infertility
treatments is increasing at older reproductive ages due to delayed childbearing (Ben
Messaoud et al., 2020; Lazzari et al., 2021). However, the already low success rates
of these techniques decline quickly with age, meaning they are only partially effec-
tive in compensating for fertility postponement (Choi et al., 2023; Kocourková et
al., 2014; Leridon & Shapiro, 2017; Rainer et al., 2011). Third, some scholars argue
that promoting assisted reproduction could influence young people’s perceptions of
how much time they have to conceive, potentially encouraging them to postpone
childbearing and thus reinforcing the decline in fertility (Passet-Wittig & Bujard,
2021; Rainer et al., 2011; Sobotka et al., 2008; Ziebe & Devroey, 2008). This paper
examines the existence of this presumed effect by investigating the link between
perceptions of MAR success at late reproductive ages and individuals’ fertility inten-
tions, using panel data from the United States (US) which consists of two waves from
2004–2007 and 2007–2010. This is the only survey we identified that allows us to
explore this crucial question, indicating the need for further data collection.


2 Existing Literature

2.1 Does Confidence in the Use of MAR Encourage Childbearing Postponement?

fuel a false perception among the wider public that childbearing can be postponed
until late reproductive ages’ (Sobotka et al., 2008, p.96). Such an argument posits

that the availability of infertility treatments indirectly influences fertility behaviours
(Rainer et al., 2011; Ziebe & Devroey, 2008), through cognitive effects. It assumes
that people would anticipate the possibility of using MAR to achieve their desired
family size and, as a result, postpone childbearing towards later ages (Passet-Wittig
& Bujard, 2021). This assumption also relies on the fact that people tend to have
imprecise knowledge about the ages at which it becomes difficult to have a child,
both with and without medical interventions (Hammarberg et al., 2017).
   In line with this, delays in first marriages and first births were observed following
a policy change that made MAR treatments more affordable in Israel (Gershoni &
Low, 2021). However, while the causal design of that study suggests a possible direct
effect of MAR availability on behaviours, it does not assess the mechanisms behind
the change. The notion that people’s perceptions of MAR could alter their fertility
decisions, particularly leading to fertility postponement, touches upon reproductive
imaginaries and one’s perception of their ability to have children. In this study, we
examine the relationship between MAR perceptions and fertility intentions at the
individual level, using data representative of the US population.

2.2 Do MAR Perceptions Influence Fertility Choices at All Ages?

It is possible that MAR only influences choices and behaviours at ages when peo-
ple feel concerned by fertility-related topics, a phenomenon termed as technologi-
cal salience (Johnson & Simon, 2012). This suggests that infertility treatments may
influence perceptions and choices only when they are personally relevant, such as
when someone experiences infertility or is more at risk of experiencing it. A techno-
logical salience effect has been observed in the US, where confidence in MAR varies
by women’s characteristics and past reproductive experiences, but only among those
who had difficulties conceiving (Johnson & Simon, 2012). In line with this, research
on fertility education initiatives shows that information about infertility influences
childbearing timing only among people in a partnership (Maeda et al., 2018). Other
work from the US suggests the existence of a technological salience effect on family
behaviours. Indeed, greater access to infertility treatments through the implementa-
tion of insurance mandates, which vary by state, is associated with delays in marriage
and childbearing, but only from age 30 (Abramowitz, 2014, 2017). This suggests that
MAR availability increases fertility rates at late reproductive ages, but does not affect
fertility behaviours at early life-course stages.
    In our study, perceptions of MAR may be weakly related to delays in family for-
mation in youth because perceptions of personal reproductive capabilities are some-
what abstract, particularly for individuals who have never tried to conceive (Boivin et
al., 2019). In contrast, views regarding MAR may be more associated with people’s
fertility decisions when the possible use of infertility treatments is salient to them,
notably at late reproductive ages due to the increased likelihood of experiencing dif-
ficulties to conceive.

2.3 Importance of MAR Perceptions as Compared to Other Factors

Finally, according to some scholars, fertility levels tend to respond downward to
increases in MAR availability in high-fertility countries, but not in low-fertility coun-
tries (Rainer et al., 2011). Their interpretation is that greater access to MAR may
encourage people to delay childbearing in high-fertility contexts where births are not
yet postponed to later ages. In contrast, in countries where fertility levels are already
low and many births are delayed, the effect of MAR on macro indicators is expected
to be marginal.
    At the same time, desires and decisions to have children in high-income countries
have been extensively found to be influenced by social drivers such as partnership
and professional situations (Fahlén and Oláh 2018; Sturm et al., 2023), reproductive
histories (Guzzo et al., 2019) and family preferences (Billari et al., 2009; Dommer-
muth et al., 2011). Fertility intentions also greatly vary depending on life circum-
stances and age (Albertini & Brini, 2020; Iacovou & Tavares, 2011). Considering
this broad range of socio-economic and attitudinal factors throughout the life course,
MAR attitudes may not be particularly decisive for fertility intentions.


3 Research Question and Hypotheses in the US Context

Against the background described above, this paper asks: Does confidence in the
effectiveness of MAR when used in one’s late thirties encourage women to delay
childbearing? This question holds scientific and social relevance. From a scientific
perspective, it addresses a critical gap in research, as, to our knowledge, the micro
relationship between MAR perceptions and fertility timing has never been tested
using representative populations. By examining this issue, we empirically contribute
to theories about how technological confidence may shape fertility behaviours. From
a social perspective, understanding this relationship is important for reproductive
healthcare policies as systematic delays in childbearing based on MAR confidence
could have a negative impact on individuals’ ability to realise their fertility intentions.
   We examine this question by testing three hypotheses:

1. Confidence in MAR encourages fertility postponement. Women with positive per-
   ceptions of MAR success at late reproductive ages may more often delay child-
   bearing than their counterparts with negative perceptions. Greater confidence in
   the availability and success of MAR may reduce the perceived urgency to have a
   child. These women would also be less likely to abandon their desire for a child, as
   confidence in MAR can support the perceptions that motherhood remains achiev-
   able later in life.
2. Perceptions of late MAR success are related to fertility intentions only among
   women who are themselves in their late 30s (technological salience);
3. Socio-economic drivers of fertility postponement play a greater role in fertility
   decisions than perceptions of MAR.

We test these three hypotheses in the US context. There, MAR’s contribution to fertil-
ity is moderate but on the rise. In 2021, about 2.0% of births resulted from IVF and
related techniques (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 2023). The prominent
increase in treatment utilisation has been particularly pronounced in the 40–45 age
group, for which the proportion of IVF births went from 17% to 23% between 2010
and 2017 (Tierney & Cai, 2019). The mean age at birth rose from 27.9 in 2004 to 28.3
years old in 2010 and 29.8 years old in 2022 (according to the Human Fertility Data-
base), and births at later ages are gradually becoming more common (Beaujouan,
2023). In this context, the use of planned oocyte cryopreservation (POC), which is
among the MAR techniques that are the most likely to compensate for the age-related
fecundity decline, has significantly increased over time since the 2010s (Johnston
et al., 2021). In light of this, we can expect this parallel increase in both fertility
delay and MAR use at the country level to be reflected at the individual level, with
confidence in MAR success at late ages encouraging women to delay childbearing
(hypothesis 1).
   The US federal government plays a minor role in regulating access to MAR. Indi-
vidual states have the power to pass legislation on assisted reproduction, but only a
few have legislated in this area (Frith & Blyth, 2014). This lack of regulation is con-
comitant with the financialization of medical practice, notably allowing the adver-
tisement of fertility preservation (van de Wiel, 2020), which is an additional reason
to expect an encouragement effect of MAR on delayed childbearing (hypothesis 1).
On the other hand, there is no federal government reimbursement of treatment costs,
although several states provide insurance mandates for fertility treatment coverage
(Henne & Bundorf, 2008). Out-of-pocket costs in the US remain substantially higher
than in any other high-income country, with only a quarter of the demand for assisted
reproduction being met (Chambers et al., 2009). Given that the availability of infertil-
ity treatments is limited and highly dependent on women’s financial resources, MAR
perceptions and fertility decisions may only be associated when seeking medical
help is highly relevant and envisioned by women (hypothesis 2). Finally, Hypothesis
3, which suggests that women’s socioeconomic characteristics are more important
than perceptions of MAR for reproductive behaviour, is particularly relevant in the
US context. There are significant socioeconomic disparities in fertility timing, with
higher-educated women experiencing more pronounced delays in childbearing (Yang
& Morgan, 2003). Moreover, because access to MAR is highly stratified by socio-
economic status (Lazzari and Tierney, 2023), the high cost of MAR treatments and
limited insurance coverage may limit the importance of MAR perceptions in shaping
fertility intentions.


4 Data and Method

4.1 The National Survey of Fertility Barriers (NSFB)

In order to explore whether opinions about MAR might encourage childbearing delay,
we study the United States, which is the only country for which we could identify
suitable data. We use the National Survey of Fertility Barriers (NSFB), a two-wave

panel representative of the female population aged 25–45. This survey investigates
psychological, social, and biomedical obstacles to fertility desires. The first wave was
conducted in 2004–2007, among 4,794 women and 926 of their partners, and the sec-
ond wave was in 2007–2010 (2,222 women and 772 partners). Attrition was high, but
tests showed it was uniform for our main explanatory variable (confidence in MAR).3
    We construct an indicator of confidence in MAR success when used at older ages,
called confidence in late MAR hereafter. Overall, these data show widespread accep-
tance of MAR in the early 2000s. In Wave 1, almost all women strongly agreed or
agreed that ‘Medical science can be a big help to women having trouble getting
pregnant’ (98%, authors’ own calculation) and that ‘Women who have trouble getting
pregnant would benefit from consulting a doctor’ (98%, authors’ own calculation).
Confidence in MAR success at late reproductive ages is also high, as a smaller but
still large proportion (strongly) agreed that, thanks to these techniques, ‘Women can
wait to have a baby until their late thirties and still have a good chance of having a
baby’ (90%). This share has not changed across waves. We focus on this indicator.
    For fertility intentions, we use the panel dimension of the data. In both waves,
women were asked whether they ‘intend to have a baby’. We selected 619 women
with positive intentions in Wave 1 who were also asked about their fertility intentions
in Wave 2 (three years later). We excluded 25 women who had a miscarriage across
waves and were still intending to have a child at Wave 2. 46% of those with positive
fertility intentions in Wave 1 had a child between waves or were pregnant in Wave 2,
13% had revised their intentions downwards, and 41% were still intending to have a
child. We consider this last category as postponing their fertility plans, as their inten-
tions remain unchanged from one wave to the next, meaning they have not acted on
them. The delay may be voluntary or not, and life circumstances, including infertility
or a change in partnership, may contribute to it.

4.2 Method

The association between the timing of fertility intentions and confidence in late MAR
success (at one’s late thirties) is estimated with multinomial logistic models. This
type of model predicts categorical outcome variables with more than two catego-
ries. Here, it estimates whether women with positive intentions in Wave 1 (a) had
a(nother) child before Wave 2 or are pregnant at Wave 2 (realised intentions), (b) had
no (additional) child between Waves 1 and 2 and maintain positive fertility intentions
(postponed intentions, which is the reference category), and (c) had no (additional)
child and no intention to have a child anymore (foregone intentions).
   Models control for characteristics that are likely to influence fertility intentions
(Billari et al., 2009; Dommermuth et al., 2011; Fahlén & Oláh, 2018; Guzzo et al.,
2019; Sturm et al., 2023): age of the respondents (distinguishing between 25–29,
30–34 and 35+), whether respondents are childless, have a tertiary degree, are
employed, and are in a partnership. The latter is based on marital status (cohabiting
and married women vs. divorcees, widowed and singles). We also control for atti-

5 Findings

We test whether confidence in MAR success at late reproductive ages in the first
wave is associated with whether women have realised their positive fertility inten-
tions between Waves 1 and 2, are postponing a birth or have foregone their fertility
plans. This association is tested by holding other factors constant, which are shown
in the full model displayed in Table 1.
   We first describe the relationship between MAR perceptions and the three out-
comes, presented as predicted probabilities in Fig. 1. There are no significant dif-
ferences in predicted probabilities of postponing for women with high confidence
in MAR efficiency at late ages (45.2% [41.0–49.5]) and those with low confidence
(44.8% [34.6–55.5]). However, predicted probabilities of having had a child across
waves are higher for women with positive MAR perceptions (45.1% [40.7–49.5])
than for women with negative perceptions (33.6% [24.5–44.2]), and conversely for
probabilities of abandoning fertility intentions (9.7% [7.5–12.4] vs. 21.5% [14.0–
31.5]). In sum, women with high confidence in MAR efficiency when used in the late
thirties, in comparison to those with low confidence, are more likely to have realised
their fertility intentions and less likely to have abandoned them, while associations
with postponement do not significantly differ.
   These associations between MAR perception and fertility also vary by age, as
presented in Fig. 2 which displays the predicted probabilities of the model with an
age-perception interaction. For women aged 25–29, we observe the same patterns as
in the overall model: equivalent chances to postpone according to confidence in late
MAR success, more births and less abandonment among those with high confidence
compared to those with low confidence (Fig. 2a).
   Women in their early thirties in Wave 1 have, in general, high probability to become
mothers before Wave 2, but those who have greater confidence in MAR have a lower
predicted probability of giving birth compared to those with low confidence in MAR
(52.3% [45.3–59.2] vs. 73.0% [50.8–87.6]) (Fig. 2b). Variations in postponement are
not statistically significant in that age group, although women with positive percep-
tions of late MAR use exhibit higher predicted probabilities. Abandoning a positive
intention to have a child is rare, but slightly more likely among those with greater
MAR confidence. At these key reproductive ages, foregoing childbearing may be
linked to unobserved characteristics (e.g., partner’s fertility intentions, family-prone-
ness) associated with a greater propensity to adjust their fertility intentions. This dif-
ference should also be interpreted cautiously, given the small numbers involved.
   Although women aged 35 + are very likely to abandon a positive fertility intention,
they are much less likely to do so if they express confidence in the success of late
MAR (26.1% [20.8–32.4] of cases, compared to 67.2% [43.4–84.6] for those with

high MAR confidence is associated with having fewer children and a slightly higher

to more flexibility in fertility intentions. Finally, among women aged 35 and older
with positive intentions at Wave 1, confidence in MAR reduces the likelihood of
abandoning those intentions.

unlike education and traditional values, their contribution to the model is significant.

6 Conclusion and Implications

We aimed to test whether confidence in MAR’s ability to help women have children
in their thirties was associated with delayed fertility intentions at the individual level
in the US. The idea is that favourable views of MAR could lead people to believe that
they have a longer reproductive lifespan than they actually have.
    The analyses lend support to our second hypothesis about the existence of a tech-
nological salience effect (Johnson & Simon, 2012). This is hinted by our finding that
women at advanced reproductive ages (35+), with positive views of MAR use at late
ages, are less likely to forego fertility intentions compared to those with negative
perceptions (and instead either have a child or delay childbearing). This hypothesis
could gain additional support from the fact that women in their thirties, who are con-
fident in MAR success at older ages, are more likely to postpone their fertility plans
than those with negative perceptions of these technologies. However, this finding
lacks statistical significance, likely due to small sample size, and warrants replication
with higher-quality data. In any case, positive MAR perceptions do not encourage
childbearing postponement before women’s thirties, which contradicts hypothesis 1.
    Despite the fact that already well-established circumstances, such as parity,
relationship statuses or infertility issues, are crucial determinants of childbearing
behaviours, MAR perceptions have a significant and independent effect on fertility
intentions, but they contribute little to the overall predictive power of the model.
Although they appear to be associated with fertility decisions for some ages, this con-
firms our third hypothesis that socioeconomic aspects are more significant drivers.
    Taken together, these results suggest a possible but weak mediating pathway
through which MAR availability may partially influence fertility behaviours at
advanced ages (Abramowitz, 2017), namely, by reinforcing confidence in reproduc-
tive technologies as a reliable backup option when likely to be confronted with the
pressure associated with age-related fertility decline. However, this analysis is not
causal and cannot properly assess whether the greater availability and use of infertil-
ity treatments lead to higher MAR acceptance or whether other factors are at play. In
addition to fertility desires or intentions, testing the relationship between confidence
in MAR and perception of one’s reproductive lifespan would help illuminate how
perceptions of these technologies can shape fertility timing decisions. This is not
possible with the available data.5
    The question raised in this analysis is relevant to contemporary societies, but our
evidence dates back to the 2000–2010s. It is possible that MAR has become a more
significant determinant of fertility over time and across age groups. Indeed, there is a
high level of trust in reproductive technologies in the US, especially those aimed at
addressing age-related infertility, such as planned oocyte cryopreservation. Since the
2010s, both MAR’s contribution to births in the US, particularly at advanced repro-
ductive ages (Tierney & Guzzo, 2023), and the use of oocyte cryopreservation (John-
ston et al., 2021) have increased. Undergoing infertility treatments, and particularly
egg freezing, has also been heavily promoted as a means to postpone childbearing.

In particular, the idea of MAR as a form of ‘fertility insurance’ has gained popular-
ity following endorsement by large companies such as Apple and Facebook (van de
Wiel, 2020).
   Our analysis only lends limited support to concerns previously raised by scholars
(Passet-Wittig & Bujard, 2021; Rainer et al., 2011; Sobotka et al., 2008; Ziebe &
Devroey, 2008) that policies designed to improve MAR availability — which are
sometimes expected to have a significant impact on fertility levels — may have the
unintended consequence of encouraging delayed childbearing. However, while we
focus on the US, the tendency for positive perceptions of MAR to encourage post-
poned fertility may differ in other contexts. Unfortunately, there is a lack of data
allowing for cross-country comparison on this topic.6
   In conclusion, this work highlights the need to include relevant measures in sur-
veys that allow for cross-country comparisons in more recent periods, to better assess
whether the perception of MAR currently influences people’s fertility choices. As
births continue to be postponed and the demand for MAR is increasing in high-
income countries, clarifying the role of reproductive technologies in fertility deci-
sion-making is essential for evidence-based reproductive health policies.
