# Compulsory Education and the Economic Value of Children

> **Version: PI rewrite v3.** Drafted from an approved outline for a reader with undergraduate
> economics training. Substance, estimates, citations, GRADE ratings, and verdicts are unchanged from
> the source chapter; the prose is new. Not the chapter of record.

**Category:** Economic  
**Primary mechanism:** A compulsory-attendance law takes children out of paid work, which lowers the
economic return parents expect from an additional child, and so lowers completed fertility.  
**Fertility declines this chapter tests the hypothesis against:** pre-modern variation before 1870,
the First Demographic Transition (roughly 1870 to 1965), and the Second Demographic Transition (1965
to the present). We abbreviate the last two FDT and SDT.  
**Cross-references:** Tempo effects and birth postponement (compulsory schooling). Geruso and Royer
(2018) belongs to both chapters, Cummins (2025) is routed there by mechanism, and the six
own-schooling studies set aside here are on-mechanism there.

## 1. The claim

A school-attendance law takes a child out of the field, the workshop, or the family store. The hours
the child no longer works are earnings the parents no longer collect, so each child is worth less to
them in money. Parents who expect less money from each child have fewer children. All three links
have to hold.

One test decides which studies count. This hypothesis is measured by how many siblings a child
subject to the law turns out to have. A study that follows a girl bound by a school-leaving law and
counts her own children twenty years later is answering a different question, about how her own
education changed her own fertility, and that question belongs to the chapter on tempo effects and
birth postponement. One study in our corpus counts siblings.

The outcome here is completed fertility, the number of children a woman has by the end of her
childbearing years, rather than the age at which she has them.

## 2. Theoretical mechanism

The models add one step to that chain: parents who get less from each child shift toward spending
more on each one. Hazan and Berdugo (2001) give the formative statement, in which child labor and
high fertility hold each other up in a development trap, and a temporary prohibition or a
compulsory-schooling requirement pushes the economy toward a steady state with little child labor
and low fertility. Four papers reach the same sign by other routes: Mookherjee, Prina, and Ray
(2012) through the mix of occupations a regulated economy produces; Fioroni (2017) through the cost
side, since child work makes a child cheaper on net and cheaper things get bought in larger
quantities; Cigno and Rosati (2005) through a household deciding how many children to have, how many
survive, and how much schooling they get all at once; and Emerson and Knabb (2013) through
expectations, since parents who come to expect schooled children rather than employed ones stay in
the low-fertility pattern. Cigno and Rosati (2024b) add that a partial restriction can sustain a
higher-education equilibrium by itself, while a total ban may need insurance and education policy
alongside it.

Theory does not settle the sign. Baland and Robinson (2000) prove that a child-labor ban can raise
welfare and still leave its fertility effect ambiguous. Takakura (2023) gets opposite signs for
different households: a ban lowers fertility among unskilled parents and raises it among skilled
parents, so the national total depends on which parents the restriction binds.

One theory makes the law a consequence of the transformation rather than its cause. Greenwood and
Seshadri (2004) build the fertility decline, the collapse of child labor, and the rise in skill out
of technological change. On their account employers came to want skills rather than small hands,
children left work for that reason, and effective regulation arrived after much of the withdrawal
had already happened. If that history is right, a design that identifies the effect of a law from
the date a legislature passed it cannot separate the law from the earlier shift in demand for skill.
Legislatures act when child labor is already falling, and the estimate charges the law with a
decline it followed.

## 3. Search strategy

This chapter and the chapter on birth postponement share one literature search, because both
hypotheses turn on the same school-leaving and child-labor laws. The search did not begin from a
keyword query. It began from seven papers, each found independently, and followed citations in both
directions: every paper those seven cited, and every paper that cited them. A blinded screen of
titles and abstracts, a focused review of the exceptions that screen set aside, and a strict test
asking of each surviving paper whether it studied an actual schooling or child-labor law and
reported a fertility outcome produced the counts in Section 4. Geruso and Royer (2018) belongs to
both chapters, because it reports teenage fertility and completed fertility.

The 16 papers kept here split into 10 theory papers and six that estimate fertility under a
schooling reform. The six entered on a weaker test than the one this chapter applies, since
reporting fertility under a reform makes a paper relevant context rather than evidence about the
value of children. Shanan (2023) came to our attention after that review closed, and we added it as
a labeled supplement rather than folding it into the 16.

Of the reforms these six use, only the English 1947 reform sits inside the FDT window of roughly
1870 to 1965; Taiwan 1968, the 1972 British reform, and China 1986 all postdate it, and the
Continental extensions run from 1936 to 1975.

The six do not answer this chapter's question, so we do not weigh them against it. They do answer
the birth-postponement chapter's question, how a woman's own schooling changes her own fertility,
and they are assessed there.

## 6. Quantitative synthesis

We pool nothing, and the number of studies is not the reason. Averaging one sibling count with six
offspring counts mixes two quantities, and averaging the six own-schooling coefficients by
themselves would produce a well-behaved number about women's education and no number at all about
the economic value of children.

Shanan (2023) studies American states in the early twentieth century, which passed
compulsory-schooling laws and child-labor restrictions together at a time when children really did
work for pay. Exposure is measured over a woman's own ages 20 to 30, her main childbearing years, so
the restrictions in force are the ones that would govern her children's work.

The first link holds. Children covered by the restrictions were 0.029 less likely to be employed (SE
0.008) against a baseline employment rate of 0.118, a fall of about a quarter, and 0.042 more likely
to attend school (SE 0.008) against a baseline attendance rate of 0.882. Both effects are
statistically significant, so these laws did move children out of work and into classrooms.

The last link holds in the same study. A woman covered by any restriction had 0.301 fewer children
over her lifetime (SE 0.044) against a sample mean of 3.112, and a one-year rise in the average age
at which children in her state left school lowered her completed fertility by 0.108 children (SE
0.024). Both are statistically significant. Measured year by year rather than over a lifetime, the
same exposure lowered the annual probability of a birth by 0.85 percentage points (SE 0.11) against
a 14.3% baseline.

The middle link is inferred from the two ends. Shanan never puts a figure on what a child's work was
worth to their parents, and never traces the fertility effect through that lost money. The laws also
arrived as a bundle, since a state that restricted children's work usually raised the school-leaving
age at the same time, and a parent who expects a better-educated child may want fewer children for
reasons unrelated to lost wages.

The paper comes closest to separating the two by entering both policy margins in the same
specification: whether any restriction was in force lowers the annual birth probability by 0.0050
(SE 0.0013), and the average school exit age lowers it by 0.0018 (SE 0.0004), each holding the other
fixed. Both stay negative and statistically significant, so neither margin carries the whole effect.
Neither margin isolates lost earnings from expected quality on its own, so the separation stays out
of reach, and this is as close as the record comes.

Shanan is rated Serious overall, driven by serious risk of confounding: states did not adopt these
laws at random, and the laws bundle two channels. The chapter's only on-target study carries a
design rating one step from the worst on the scale.

## 7. Demographic significance

The three phenomena fail differently, and the difference tells a reader where this hypothesis can
still be tested. Testing it takes two things at once: children whose market work a law can remove,
and a law that removes it. Pre-modern societies have the first and not the second. Rich countries
during the SDT have the second and not the first. The FDT has both, which is why the corpus holds
one study rather than none.

### 7.1 Pre-modern

Children worked in every pre-modern economy this review covers, so the mechanism is plausible there
and probably operated. It cannot be tested with the tools this chapter uses, because the hypothesis
is identified from a law that pushed children out of work, and before 1870 no such law existed at a
scale that would generate a comparison. Insufficient data, and the reason is a missing identifying
policy rather than a missing mechanism.

### 7.2 FDT

Shanan's own decomposition attributes 8% of the 1900–1930 decline in the annual chance of a birth
among 30-year-old native-born American women to the spread of the restrictions. Three numbers
produce it. Exposure to the restrictions rose by 48 percentage points over those three decades. Full
exposure lowers the annual birth probability by 0.85 percentage points. The observed birth
probability fell by 5 percentage points over the same window. So `0.48 * 0.85 / 5 = 0.08`.

Three things limit the 8%. It covers women at a single age, 30, rather than the whole childbearing
span. It measures the chance of a birth in a given year rather than the number of children a woman
ends up with, and this hypothesis is about the second. Shanan attaches no uncertainty to it. And the
sensitivity check we ran does not fix that: carrying through only the uncertainty in Shanan's
coefficient, while treating the 48-point rise in exposure and the 5-point fall in birth probability
as known, gives an analytic 95% interval of 6.1% to 10.2%, and holding two of the three inputs fixed
makes that interval narrower than the truth.

Our review has two other ways of asking whether a cause is large enough to matter for a decline: it
asks whether an effect of the estimated size, applied over the range the cause actually moved, could
produce the decline that occurred, and it asks how much of the variation in fertility the cause
accounts for once standard controls are included. We checked both here, and neither strengthens the
case.

One figure in the literature looks like a second estimate and is not. Cigno and Rosati (2024a)
report that restrictions on children's work explain about 10% of the American fertility decline.
Their underlying primary study is Shanan, whose own back-of-envelope figure is 8%. There is one
calculation here, not two.

### 7.3 SDT

The SDT is a fertility transition rather than a date. A setting belongs to it when fertility there
is falling from just above replacement to below it and continuing down. Calendar period and national
income stand in for that condition badly, and a compulsory-schooling law passed in a poor country
this decade says nothing about the SDT unless that country's fertility is making the crossing at the
time.

Two answers follow. Where the SDT is most studied, in rich countries, the mechanism is largely spent
before the crossing begins. Children's market work is near zero, attendance is already universal,
and a law that pushes the leaving age from 16 to 17 removes almost no earnings a parent was counting
on. The hypothesis is close to inapplicable there, which limits the range over which it can apply
rather than leaving a hole in the evidence.

Elsewhere the mechanism could still operate, and the corpus does not reach those settings. Two of
its reforms fall in places where children still worked, Taiwan in 1968 and China in 1986, and
neither has been classified by the fertility level that governs the distinction, so neither can be
placed in the SDT on the evidence in hand. The record does carry one classification for the Chinese
pair: fertility there was under the one-child policy, which our review requires be flagged, and
under a binding state mandate a crossing below replacement reflects the mandate as much as the
process the SDT names. In any case every study of both reforms counts the mother's own children, so
none would test the sibling outcome even in a setting that qualified.

Insufficient data, for two distinct reasons: the hypothesis is close to inapplicable where the SDT
is best documented, and unclassified and untested where it might still apply.

## 8. GRADE rating

**FDT: Low.** GRADE runs from Very low through Low and Moderate to High, and three raters scored
this hypothesis independently at Low. Shanan supplies one credible historical quasi-experiment
reaching from a policy through children's work to completed fertility. Our review sets replication
across two or more settings as the boundary between Low and Moderate, and there is none here. Two
further reasons hold the rating down: states may have adopted these laws for reasons the design
cannot fully rule out, and the lost economic value of children is inferred from the two ends of the
chain rather than measured in the middle. The panel reached that rating without the risk-of-bias
assessment stated in front of it, and Shanan's Serious rating turns on the same confounding the
panel's own reasoning names.

**Pre-modern and SDT: no rating**, because neither has a study to rate.

## 9. Verdict

| Phenomenon | Causal credibility | Demographic significance |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-modern | Insufficient data | Insufficient data |
| FDT | Low | Partial; explains 8% of the 1900–1930 decline in the annual birth probability at age 30 |
| SDT | Insufficient data | Insufficient data |

The evidence supports a historically real relationship running from policy through children's work to
completed fertility, at modest scale. How much of that relationship runs through lost child earnings,
as against a parent's expectation of a better-educated child, is unresolved on the evidence in hand.

## 10. Open questions and recommended studies

Two studies would move the verdict. An independent replication of Shanan in a second setting would
move the rating. A formal mediation through reduced child earnings, or any direct measure of what
children contributed to a household, would turn the mechanism claim from an inference off the two
ends of the chain into a measurement of the middle. Better scaling is available now without new
data: Shanan reports a completed-fertility effect of −0.301 as well as a period effect, so a
decomposition of completed cohort fertility can be specified from what is already published, and it
would test the hypothesis on the outcome the hypothesis names.

Two of the three phenomena need further searching, and neither search is straightforward. For the
SDT the requirement is not a developing-country reform but one in a setting making the crossing
described in Section 7.3. Three conditions have to hold together: a reform sharp enough to identify
an effect; a setting in the crossing, established against fertility data rather than assumed from
income; and an outcome measured as the number of siblings a child subject to the law had. The
reforms and the classification method both exist, so this search is specifiable, and its feasibility
turns on whether the three conditions ever coincide in a studied setting.

For the pre-modern period the hypothesis needs a policy that forced children into school, and we
doubt one exists at any useful scale. A search should be attempted and may well return nothing. A
weaker alternative is worth naming for what it is: variation in children's productive contribution,
by crop, by inheritance regime, or by proximity to manufacturing, would identify the value of
children without identifying the effect of a schooling mandate. That tests an adjacent claim rather
than this one.
