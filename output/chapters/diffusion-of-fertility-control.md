# Chapter A.3: Diffusion and Social-Learning of Fertility Control

**Category:** Demographic (proximate mechanism; the underlying value change is cultural and lives in D.1)
**Primary mechanism:** The knowledge and social acceptability of deliberately limiting births spreads through social, linguistic, and religious contact, so that adoption at one place and time raises adoption nearby, faster and along different lines than economic conditions alone predict.
**Cross-references:** A.20 (cultural-diffusion *channels*); A.2 (contraceptive *technology*); A.6 (stigma *level*); A.19 (vertical transmission); A.5 (family-planning programs); D.1.a/D.1.b (the root value shift)

## 1. The claim

This chapter explores the effect of exposure to others' use of fertility control on a couple's own fertility.

**In plain terms: the idea that you can deliberately have fewer children — and the sense that doing so is acceptable — can spread from person to person.** It travels through neighbours, kin, and people who share your language or religion. Once some families in a place start limiting births, the families around them become more likely to do the same, and they do so sooner than their incomes or living conditions on their own would lead you to expect. The claim is that fertility decline is, in part, a *contagious idea*, not only a response to changing material circumstances.

Technically, the hypothesis is that fertility control is an innovation that diffuses. Adoption by socially proximate others — peers, prior cohorts, a linguistically or religiously connected community — raises a couple's own probability of adopting control, over and above what income, the price of children, the underlying desired family size, the available contraceptive technology, and any organized family-planning program would produce. Its canonical empirical signature is the Princeton European Fertility Project residual: across nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century Europe, the decline in *marital fertility* (births to married women — the margin the transition moved) clustered by language and religion and crossed provincial borders on a timetable that development indicators do not reproduce (Coale and Watkins 1986; Knodel and van de Walle 1979; Cleland and Wilson 1987).

The hypothesis is registered as **proximate**: it describes how fertility control *spreads*, not what is being spread or why couples come to want smaller families. The change in desired family size — the value shift toward autonomy, secular authority, and small-family norms — belongs to D.1 and is treated there. This chapter owns only the spread.

## 2. Theoretical mechanism

The mechanism is a social-multiplier on an adoption decision. In the reader's vocabulary: each couple faces a discrete choice — adopt deliberate fertility control or not — and the return to adopting depends on how many others have already adopted. That dependence can run through two distinct channels, which the chapter must keep separate because only one is this chapter's parameter.

The first is **social learning**: others' adoption is informative. Watching neighbours limit births and prosper resolves uncertainty about whether control works, whether it is safe, and whether it is compatible with a respectable life. The second is **social influence** (a norm or conformity effect): others' adoption changes the payoff directly, by shifting what is considered acceptable, independent of any information conveyed (Montgomery and Casterline 1996). Both produce the same aggregate signature — adoption spreading through a network faster than fundamentals move — and the title/abstract record rarely distinguishes them; full-text extraction must.

There is no accounting identity here. Unlike a mechanical channel (a twinning multiplier, a proximate-determinant identity), diffusion is *entirely* a behavioural claim: the hypothesis can be wrong. It is wrong if, once income, prices, desired family size, technology, and program supply are held fixed, a couple's adoption does not respond to socially proximate others' adoption — if the apparent clustering of fertility decline is simply the clustering of its economic and cultural causes. This is the mechanism's central vulnerability and it has a name (§2 of the search scope): the **reflection problem** (Manski). Neighbours adopt together both when one is imitating the other (true diffusion) and when a common shock — a factory closing, a school opening, a road arriving — hits them at the same time (no diffusion). A design that cannot separate the two has not identified this chapter's parameter, however tight its correlation.

The mechanism moves the **extensive margin** first (whether a couple ever practices deliberate control) and the intensive margin second (how much). The transition it is invoked to explain is overwhelmingly an extensive-margin event: the spread of *any* parity-specific stopping across a population that previously practiced none.

## 3. Search strategy

The search is documented in full and reproducibly in `literature/search-logs/diffusion-of-fertility-control-search-scope.md` and the build scripts `source/build/goldset/89`–`94`. It follows the review's Gold-Anchored Clustered Search: a cold-start anchor set (step 89), a two-tier citation frame (step 90), a blinded title/abstract screen (steps 91–94). Twenty canonical anchors were enumerated from the diffusion/social-learning canon and existence-verified against live Crossref and doi.org — 13 at the version of record, 7 real but version-drifted, zero ghosts. A backward-plus-forward citation snowball from those anchors produced a 3,524-record candidate frame, screened blind against a six-wall rubric.

**The walls, and their enforceability.** A.3 sits in a dense neighbourhood and shares vocabulary and seminals with several hypotheses. Six routing boundaries were declared in advance and enforced at screen:

- **Wall 1 — A.20 (cultural-diffusion *channels*).** Load-bearing. A.3 owns the diffused *content* (the knowledge and legitimacy of fertility control); A.20 owns the *channels* it travels through (social networks as architecture, mass media, linguistic borders as conduits). A study of a social network carrying *fertility-control knowledge specifically* stays here; a content-agnostic channel study — above all a mass-media exposure study — routes to A.20. This wall was **resolved on 2026-09-18 in favour of channel-excluded A.3**: the two hypotheses remain distinct as registered, and A.3's identified core is expected to be thin once channel evidence is removed. That thinness is accepted as a result to report, not a defect to fix. The wall held at screen: the cleanest identified estimates in the neighbourhood — the Brazil soap-opera and India cable-TV quasi-experiments — routed to A.20, as intended.
- **Wall 2 — A.2 (contraceptive *technology*).** Both hypotheses say "diffusion." A.2 is the spread of the physical methods (the Pill, the IUD); A.3 is the spread of the idea, which ran on withdrawal and abstinence in historical Europe. Technology-access designs route to A.2.
- **Wall 3 — A.6 (stigma *level*).** A.3 and A.6 share the word *legitimation*. A.6 is the *level* of acceptability (a stock) releasing latent demand; A.3 is its *spread* (a flow). Legitimation counts here only as a diffusing flow.
- **Wall 4 — A.19 (vertical transmission).** A.19 is parent-to-child; A.3 is horizontal, across peers and communities.
- **Wall 5 — A.5 (family-planning programs).** A.5 is exogenous program supply; A.3 is endogenous social spread. A program's diffusion *spillover* to untreated neighbours through social contact is the bridge cell and stays here.
- **Wall 6 — D.1 (the value shift).** A.3 is *how* the practice spread; D.1 is *why* desired family size fell.

No wall was declared unenforceable in advance. Phenomenon scope is **FDT and SDT** (per registry); there is no pre-modern cell, because the mechanism presupposes an innovation that spreads.

## 5. The ideal design

**5.1 The ideal estimand.** The change in the probability that a couple initiates deliberate fertility control, in percentage points, caused by a one-standard-deviation increase in the share of their socially proximate reference group (kin, co-village, or same-language/same-religion community) that has *already* initiated it, holding fixed household income and wealth, the local price and opportunity cost of children, the couple's desired family size, the contraceptive technology available, and any family-planning program exposure, measured among couples of reproductive age in a population at the onset of its fertility transition, observed over a horizon long enough (10–20 years) to capture completed-fertility consequences.

**5.2 The design that would identify it.** *Source of variation:* an instrument for peer adoption that shifts a couple's exposure to adopters without itself affecting their own fertility — for example, quasi-random variation in the composition of a couple's network (a resettlement lottery, a migration shock that imports adopters, a network-partitioning boundary), or a randomized information intervention with measured social spillovers to untreated neighbours. *Comparison group:* otherwise-identical couples with lower exposure to prior adopters. *Identifying assumption:* the exposure shock is independent of the couple's own fertility determinants — falsifiable by showing the shock does not predict pre-period fertility trends or the excluded fundamentals. *Estimating equation:* adoption (or completed fertility) on peer-adoption share, instrumented, with controls for the fundamentals above and community fixed effects. *Data required:* a sociometric panel (who is connected to whom) with fertility histories, over 10–20 years. *Sample size:* powered for a social-multiplier plausibly in the range of a 5–15 percentage-point adoption shift per SD of peer adoption.

**5.3 The distance table.** The named anchors (Kohler–Behrman–Watkins 2001, Munshi–Myaux 2006, Beach–Hanlon 2023, Spolaore–Wacziarg 2022, Daudin–Franck–Rapoport 2019) are the studies to beat, but all are paywalled and unretrieved; the table below scores the **10 identified-core studies open access returned**, on assignment (the ideal exogenous-exposure source vs. weaker vs. observational), exposure (registered diffusion treatment vs. proxy), outcome (registered vs. intermediate), and horizon.

## 6. Included studies

Ten of the 44 identified-core studies were retrieved through open access (the other 34, including every named anchor, are paywalled and queued for library retrieval). Full-text reading of the ten produced the chapter's most consequential finding, which is about the *evidence*, not the effect.

**The screen's "identified" tag over-credits identification.** The title/abstract screen tagged all ten as designs that separate diffusion from a common shock. Full text does not bear that out: **two** identify by design (Rossi & Xiao's instrument-plus-DiD; Anukriti et al.'s randomised peer arm), **three** are partial (Iyer & Weeks' structural GMM with a rainfall instrument; Behrman–Kohler–Watkins' individual fixed effects; Ciliberto et al.'s structural bounds), and **five are observational or descriptive with no exogenous exposure variation at all** (Serbia, England & Wales, Ethiopia, Bavaria, MENA). An abstract that says "we find social diffusion net of controls" reads as identification to a screener and turns out, at full text, to be the naive correlation with covariates. The true identified core is therefore thinner than even the screen's conservative tag implied — the reflection problem bites not only the literature but the abstracts that describe it.

**The naive-estimator check, confirmed at full text.** The comparison an author makes without thinking hard is a cross-sectional or cross-regional correlation of one group's fertility with a neighbouring group's adoption level. It conditions on a common environment — proximate groups share prices, weather, roads, schools, and clergy — so its bias runs *toward* finding diffusion, crediting to contagion any decline a shared shock produced at once. Five of the ten retrieved studies are exactly this estimator. Two full-text results show how much it matters: Behrman–Kohler–Watkins report that the naive OLS peer coefficient is inflated **180%** above their fixed-effects estimate, and Ciliberto et al. find that a naive positive coworker effect (+1 percentage point per childbearing coworker) **flips negative** once establishment random effects absorb the common workplace shock. Where the bias has been removed, the effect shrinks or reverses.

**Even the identified estimates are channel-contaminated.** The two designed-identified studies do not cleanly measure A.3's parameter. Rossi & Xiao's θ = 0.648 (a minority woman has 0.648 fewer children per one-child exogenous drop in her peer group's mean fertility; SE 0.102) blends social conformism with a quantity–quality economic response the design cannot separate. Anukriti et al.'s +6-percentage-point rise in modern contraceptive use is driven by a fee voucher (an A.5 family-planning-program input) and operates through instrumental accompaniment past a stigma barrier (A.6), not the normative social learning A.3 posits; the clean peer effect is significant only in socially constrained subgroups and is marginal overall (p ≈ 0.11). The cleanest A.3-mechanism estimates are the two partial Kenyan studies — Iyer & Weeks' conformist interaction (incidence-rate ratios 1.13–1.27, strongest among the Kikuyu at 1.267) and Behrman–Kohler–Watkins' +0.054 own-use probability per contraceptive-using network partner.

Per-study extraction, with effect sizes, identification, and ROBINS-I risk-of-bias coding, is in `extraction/diffusion-of-fertility-control.csv`.

## 7. Quantitative synthesis

**The answer in plain terms first.** On the studies we could read, the idea does appear to spread — where a design credibly isolates it, being surrounded by people who limit births makes a couple somewhat more likely to do the same. But the effect is small, it is measured in five different and incompatible ways, and the two cleanest studies cannot separate it from an economic response or a family-planning program riding alongside it. The honest reading is that social diffusion of fertility control is real at the individual level and modest, and that the retrieved evidence cannot yet say how much of any population's fertility decline it caused.

**The estimate.** No pooled estimate is reported, because no three of the ten share an outcome family and a harmonised exposure unit: the exposures are a peer group's mean fertility (China), contraceptive-using network partners (Kenya FE), an endogenous group-fertility term (Kenya GMM), childbearing coworkers (Denmark), a randomised support friend (India), and settlement/residential co-ethnic share (Serbia, England & Wales, Ethiopia); the outcomes span completed fertility, current contraceptive use, and births. Pooling across these would average a births-per-peer-child coefficient with an odds ratio for contraceptive uptake — not a common parameter. The synthesis is therefore narrative, as the scope anticipated.

Three findings survive the narrative synthesis. **First, sign and rough size where identified:** the credible individual-level estimates point the same way — more exposure to fertility control transmits more control (Iyer & Weeks IRR 1.13–1.27; Behrman–Kohler–Watkins +0.054 per using partner; Rossi & Xiao θ = 0.648 fewer children per peer-child, though channel-blended). **Second, the naive estimator overstates it:** the fixed-effects and structural corrections shrink the peer coefficient by 180% (Kenya) and flip its sign (Denmark), so the large diffusion effects in the descriptive literature are substantially selection and common shocks. **Third, the one retrieved study of the historical transition is counter-evidence:** Brown & Guinnane find Bavaria's apparent diffusion residual disappears once textile employment, female wages, and religion are measured — "time itself" was proxying for economic change, not contagion. The identified core confirms a modest micro-level diffusion effect; it does not license a claim that diffusion drove a transition.

## 8. Demographic significance

The phenomenon to be explained is measured in births per woman — a decline of roughly three to four children over a full transition; this mechanism offers a share of that decline's *timing and pace* attributable to social contagion net of economic and technological change. The units are commensurable: unlike a mechanism denominated in a bounded multiplier, a diffusion effect can in principle be expressed as a fraction of the birth decline it accelerates. Whether that fraction is large is an empirical question, and it cannot be answered from the descriptive residual alone — a residual shows that development does not explain the clustering, not that diffusion accounts for a given share of the fall. The denominator, when a share is computed, is the *change* in the outcome over the phenomenon's full window (PROTOCOL §4.2.1), not a level and not a study window.

### 8.1 Pre-modern
For pre-modern variation, the verdict is **NOT ASSESSED**, because the phenomenon is out of scope for this hypothesis in the registry: diffusion of a spreading innovation is not defined before there is an innovation to spread.

### 8.2 First Demographic Transition
For the FDT, the verdict is **NOT ASSESSED**, because the only retrieved study of the historical transition (Brown & Guinnane on Bavaria) reports no diffusion parameter and finds the apparent residual vanishes once economics is measured — so there is no identified magnitude to divide by the decline, and the one data point points against, not toward, a diffusion share. The sign, were a magnitude available, would be negative; the retrieved FDT evidence does not supply one.

### 8.3 Second Demographic Transition
For the SDT, the verdict is **NOT ASSESSED**, because the retrieved identified estimates are individual-level peer effects in incompatible units (a births-per-peer-child coefficient, a per-partner contraceptive-use probability, an odds ratio) with no population-level decomposition, and the macro fertility panel needed for a denominator (HFD/WPP) is not in this repository. A break-even framing bounds it: even the largest clean estimate — Rossi & Xiao's θ = 0.648 fewer births per one-child drop in peer fertility — is a within-population multiplier on an *already-falling* peer mean, and it blends an economic channel; for social learning *net of economics* to reach the MINOR band (5% of a ~3-child SDT decline) the pure-conformism component would have to be a large fraction of θ, which the retrieved evidence does not establish. The sign is negative; the share is not computable from what was retrieved.

## 10. Verdict

**Social diffusion of fertility control is a real but modest individual-level effect whose demographic significance is NOT ASSESSED — and, on the evidence retrieved so far, is more likely small than large.** Where a design credibly isolates it, exposure to others' fertility control does raise a couple's own (the two Kenyan partial-identified estimates, consistent in sign). But three facts pull against reading it as a driver of transitions: the naive correlation that dominates the descriptive literature overstates the effect by enough to flip its sign under proper controls (Denmark) or shrink it by 180% (Kenya); the two cleanest identified estimates are contaminated by an economic channel (China) and a family-planning program (India); and the single retrieved study of an actual historical transition finds the diffusion residual dissolves once economics is measured (Bavaria). The one figure to carry away is **2 of 10** — of the ten retrieved studies the screen called identified, only two identify by design, and even those do not cleanly measure diffusion of *control*. This verdict rests on 10 of 44 identified-core studies; the paywalled remainder, including every named anchor, could raise it, but the burden they must clear is now specific: an estimate of social learning net of the economic and program channels the retrieved core keeps blending in.

## Provenance and standing caveats

This chapter is written on 10 of 44 wanted full texts (23%).

The findings that would survive full retrieval are the direction of the individual-level effect (exposure to fertility control transmits it), the naive-estimator bias (the descriptive correlation overstates diffusion, shrinking or reversing under proper controls), and the channel-contamination of the identified core (economic in China, program in India); the finding that might not is the headline **2 of 10 identify** — the paywalled anchors were selected precisely because their designs promise cleaner identification, so the identified fraction should rise as they are retrieved, and the demographic-significance verdict could move off NOT ASSESSED if an anchor delivers a poolable, channel-clean magnitude.

Every count in §4 is a screen-level figure from blinded title/abstract classification by an automated recall filter (Haiku D2a). Every effect size in §§5.3–7 is a full-text extraction of one of the 10 retrieved studies (recorded in `extraction/diffusion-of-fertility-control.csv`); none is abstract-only. The GRADE rating (§9) is provisional pending the formal three-rater panel at full retrieval. The chapter was drafted over one standing objection — that writing before full retrieval risks overstating a partial evidence base — met by reporting the retrieval fraction in the status line and the verdict, computing no pooled estimate, marking demographic significance NOT ASSESSED, and capping GRADE at Very low. The A.20 channel-excluded ruling under which these studies were included and routed is an RA operational call still awaiting PI ratification (§11).
