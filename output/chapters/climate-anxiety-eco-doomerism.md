# Chapter: Climate Anxiety and Eco-Doomerism

**Primary mechanism:** Dread about the ecological future suppresses childbearing even where the desire for children remains intact, so fertility falls without a fall in how many children people say they want.

## 1. Verdict

**PENDING.** The verdict cannot be written before extraction. What can be stated now is the shape the
verdict will take, because the evidence base has already determined which questions it is capable of
answering.

The hypothesis makes one claim that is distinctive and one that is not. The non-distinctive claim is
that people who are worried about the climate report lower intentions to have children. That claim is
well populated: sixty-two studies bear on it. The distinctive claim is that ecological dread suppresses
childbearing *while the desire for children remains intact*, which is what separates this hypothesis
from the account in which people simply came to want fewer children. Four studies in the entire
retrieved literature address that claim.

Two structural facts will shape whatever verdict the extraction supports. First, only eight studies in
the whole evidence base measure realized fertility rather than stated intention or attitude, and of
those eight, one is a formal growth model and one identifies from air pollution rather than from
expressed dread. Second, the confound that most threatens the hypothesis is the same thing as the
boundary that separates it from its neighbour: left politics, higher education, and secularism predict
both climate concern and low fertility, so a study that fails to separate them is not evidence for this
chapter but evidence for the postmaterialism chapter.

## 2. The Claim and Mechanism

The claim is that a person can want children and not have them because of what they believe about the
future of the planet. Dread does the work that, in competing accounts, is done by preference. The
mechanism is affective rather than calculative: it runs through fear about the world a child would
inhabit, or through a moral judgement about the emissions a child would add, and not through a revised
estimate of the returns to childrearing.

That distinction is what gives the hypothesis its content, and it is also what makes it hard to test.
Three neighbouring accounts predict the same falling fertility among the same people.

The first is postmaterialist value change. On that account the desire for children has genuinely
fallen, displaced by self-actualization, autonomy, and career. A person who says they do not want
children because the world is burning may be describing a real fear, or may be giving a
socially-creditable reason for a preference they hold anyway. Separating those requires evidence that
the desire is live and the fear is what suppresses it. This chapter counts a study toward the
distinctive claim only when both conditions are shown.

The second is the general mental-health account. Anxiety about the future has risen across many
domains, and if childbearing falls with anxiety generally, the ecological content of the fear is
incidental rather than causal. The discriminating question is whether the feared object is specifically
ecological.

The third is economic uncertainty. The shape of the argument is identical, a future that looks bad
suppressing a present commitment, but the feared object is personal income and the mechanism is option
value rather than dread.

The hypothesis also has an unusually sharp reverse-causality problem. Parenthood itself changes how
people think about the future, so a correlation between climate worry and childlessness can run the
other way: people who have children may become more worried about the climate, not less likely to have
them. At least one retrieved study is designed around exactly this question.

## 3. How the Evidence Is Organized

The single most important organizing distinction is not topical but about what the outcome measures.

| Outcome level | What it measures | Role in the chapter |
|---|---|---|
| Stated intention or attitude | What people say about wanting, planning, or hesitating over children | The bulk of the evidence. Sixty-two studies. Retained in the primary synthesis and always flagged as stated. |
| Realized fertility | Births that actually occurred | The scarce and decisive stratum. Eight studies. |

These two are never combined. An intention is not a birth, and pooling them would let a well-populated
measure of what people say stand in for a nearly-absent measure of what they do. The chapter therefore
carries two synthesis tracks and deliberately produces no combined estimate.

Cutting the other way, the evidence sorts by which version of the ecological fear is doing the work.

| Evidence stream | What it measures | Count |
|---|---|---|
| Eco-pessimism | General pessimism about the ecological future and its association with childbearing | 34 stated, 7 realized |
| Habitability fear | Fear specifically about the world a child would have to live in | 18 stated |
| Carbon ethics | The moral judgement that having a child adds emissions | 6 stated, 1 realized |
| Desire independence | Fertility suppressed while the desire for children is shown to remain | 4 stated |
| Theory | Conceptual and ethical work, plus climate-anxiety measurement studies | 142, separate stream |

The last row is not evidence for the causal claim and never counts toward it. The fourth row is the
chapter's reason for existing.

## 4. Inclusion Approach

The review began with seventeen independently chosen anchor studies spanning the ecological-fear
streams and the three neighbouring topics a search could confuse with them. The anchors were verified
to exist before use, which caught one seminal-list entry that does not exist as described. They then
seeded a candidate pool built from the studies they cite and the studies that cite them, reaching
papers by a route that does not inherit a keyword search's blind spots. Every one of the 1,170
candidates was read blind at the title-and-abstract level and sorted into a stream, into a neighbouring
chapter, or out.

Three boundary rules were applied as hard lines rather than judgement calls.

- Against postmaterialism: a paper counts here only if it shows both ecological-fear content and a
  desire for children that has not simply been relabelled as a preference for freedom or career. A
  paper that merely mentions climate alongside a fallen preference routes to the postmaterialism chapter.
- Against the mental-health account: the feared object must be specifically ecological, not general
  anxiety that happens to be measured in a climate-aware sample.
- Against economic uncertainty: the feared object must be planetary habitability or the ethics of
  emissions, not personal job or income risk.

One further precision rule was added after a pilot: studies of physical climate exposure, meaning heat,
drought, or disaster, and its effect on fertility are not relevant here. That is a real literature and a
real causal channel, but it is a different mechanism, running through biology and economic shock rather
than through what people feel about the future.

## 5. Included Studies and Evidence Map

### 5.1 The stated-intention evidence

Sixty-two studies, of which fifty-six are observational, two quasi-experimental, three mechanism
studies, and one other. The literature is strikingly new: thirty-nine of the sixty-two are from 2024 or
later, and twenty-two are from 2025 alone. The anchor studies give the flavour of the stream, spanning
Canadian university students (2011), European reproductive intentions (2021), eco-reproductive concerns
(2020), and a 2025 Italian study of climate-change concerns and fertility intentions.

The dominant design is a cross-sectional survey measuring a climate-worry scale against a
fertility-intention item. **PENDING:** per-study extraction of the association, its sign, and whether the
design adjusts for the political and educational confounders that define the boundary with the
postmaterialism chapter.

realized-fertility base for this hypothesis is six studies, of which two are not yet in a peer-reviewed
journal.

The single most valuable design in the set is the 2023 study asking whether people are too worried to
have children or more worried after having them, because it addresses the reverse-causality problem
directly rather than assuming it away.

### 5.4 Theory stream

One hundred and forty-two studies, comprising eighty-three works of ecological ethics and
antinatalist argument and fifty-nine studies of the climate-anxiety construct itself. The theory
literature is more than twice the size of the empirical core and more than seventeen times the size of
the realized-fertility evidence. As in the decoupling chapter, that ratio is the organizing finding
rather than a gap to be closed: the ethical and conceptual literature on whether one should have
children in a climate crisis is mature, and the evidence on whether ecological dread actually changes
births is not.

## 6. Quantitative Synthesis

**PENDING in full.** No effect has been extracted and no pool has been computed. What is fixed in
advance is the structure the synthesis will take, and one decision that is already frozen.

Two pooling tracks, never combined: stated intention, and realized fertility. The frozen scope decision
is that stated-intention outcomes are retained as first-class primary synthesis rather than relegated
to a secondary section, on the grounds that intention is the outcome most of this literature actually
measures and burying it would misrepresent the evidence base. The corresponding obligation is that
every stated-intention result is flagged as such and never allowed to stand in for a birth.

Anticipated obstacles, recorded now so that the synthesis is not later described as having failed:

- The realized-fertility track has at most six clean studies. A random-effects pool below three studies
  is reported rather than pooled under the project's conservative rule, so a pooled realized-fertility
  estimate may be possible but will be fragile.
- Exposure measures are unlikely to be commensurable. Climate-anxiety scales, single-item worry
  questions, and environmental-identity measures are not the same instrument, and harmonizing them onto
  a common scale is a substantive decision rather than a mechanical one.
- The confounding structure is uniform rather than idiosyncratic. Every observational study in this
  literature faces the same political-and-educational confound, which means heterogeneity in results
  will not average it away.

## 7. Demographic Significance

**PENDING.** The demographic-significance pass requires extracted effects and cannot be run yet.

The question it will have to answer is unusually stark here, and worth stating in advance. Even if
ecological dread robustly reduces stated fertility intentions, the demographic significance of that
finding for the second transition depends on two further quantities that this literature has barely
measured: how much of the intention gap translates into realized births, and what share of the
population holds the dread strongly enough to act on it. The chapter should resist reporting a large
intention effect as though it were a large demographic effect, which is the specific failure mode this
hypothesis invites.

There is no pre-modern or first-transition cell. The mechanism requires knowledge of anthropogenic
climate change and is scoped to the 2020s.

## 8. External Validity and Transportability

The evidence base is geographically narrow and temporally almost undistributed. The retrieved studies
concentrate in Europe, North America, and educated survey populations, often university students, and
thirty-nine of the sixty-two stated-intention studies were published in 2024 or later. Both facts bound
what the chapter can claim.

The narrowness is not incidental to the hypothesis. Climate anxiety as a measured construct is most
prevalent in exactly the populations that are already at low fertility, which means the settings that
best identify the mechanism are the settings where the outcome has least room to move. Whether the
mechanism operates in high-fertility settings, where the ecological future is at least as threatening,
is not addressed by this literature.

The recency cuts two ways. It is appropriate, since the phenomenon is recent. But it means a
substantial share of the evidence has not been through peer review or replication, and a chapter
written now will date faster than the others in this review.

## 9. Risk of Bias and Identification Quality

**PENDING** as a per-study assessment. The dominant risks are already visible from the design
distribution, and there are three.

Confounding is the first and worst, and it is not a generic caution. Left political orientation, higher
education, and secularism each predict both climate concern and low fertility. A study that regresses
fertility intention on climate worry without holding those fixed has not identified this chapter's
mechanism; it has measured the postmaterialism chapter's. Because this confound is also the routing
rule that separates the two chapters, a misrouted study and a confounded estimate are the same error.

Reverse causality is the second. Having children changes how people appraise the future, so the
direction of any cross-sectional association is genuinely ambiguous. One retrieved study is built
around this; the rest are not.

Common-method bias is the third. Most studies measure both the exposure and the outcome by self-report
in a single survey instrument, often adjacent to each other, which inflates associations between them.
This is a well-understood problem in survey psychology and it applies to most of the stated-intention
stream.

## 11. Interpretation for Economics Readers

The hypothesis is best read as a claim that a fertility decline can occur without a change in
preferences, driven instead by a belief about the state of the world. In the standard model, children
are a good and fertility falls when demand falls or the price rises. This hypothesis holds demand
fixed and changes something else: the expected quality of the world the good is consumed in, or a moral
cost attached to producing it.

That framing makes the identification problem legible. The distinctive claim requires observing a
population whose measured desire for children is unchanged, whose ecological beliefs shift, and whose
births then fall. The literature has abundant measurements of belief, abundant measurements of stated
desire, and almost no linkage of either to births. Whether the eight realized-fertility studies close
any part of that gap is the empirical question this chapter exists to answer, and it is PENDING.

There is also an interpretive trap worth naming for economists specifically. A stated reason is not a
revealed preference. When a survey respondent says they are not having children because of the climate,
that statement is jointly produced by their actual constraint and by which reasons are socially
available to give. The chapter should treat stated ecological reasons as an outcome to be explained
rather than as an identified cause.

## 12. Limitations and Extensions

The chapter's limitations are known before its results are.

The first is the realized-fertility gap. Eight studies, and about six once the formal model and the
physical-exposure study are set aside, is a thin base for a demographic claim.

The second is that the distinctive claim rests on four studies, none measuring births.

The third is a demonstrated weakness in the search rather than in the literature, and it should be
stated plainly. The boundary against the general mental-health account is effectively untested. Of
1,170 screened candidates, exactly one routed to that neighbouring chapter. A citation neighbourhood
built from climate-specific anchors never reaches the general-anxiety literature, so the wall separating
ecological dread from ordinary anxiety was never exercised. The boundary currently rests on assertion
rather than on demonstrated routing. A targeted probe against the general-anxiety-and-fertility
literature would test it, and until that is run the discriminant validity of this chapter's core
construct is an assumption.

Three extensions would move the chapter. First, a design linking a validated climate-anxiety measure to
subsequent realized births in a panel, which would convert the entire stated-intention literature from
a proxy into a tested predictor. Second, a design that holds measured desire for children fixed while
ecological belief varies, which is the only thing that can identify the distinctive claim. Third, a
study in a high-fertility setting, which would establish whether the mechanism is a general one or an
artefact of populations already near the bottom.

## 13. Reproducibility Appendix

The chapter uses the project's gold-anchored clustered search workflow. Seventeen independently chosen
and existence-verified anchor studies produced a citation-based candidate pool of 1,170 deduplicated
records, screened blind at title and abstract into the evidence streams, the neighbouring chapters, and
out. The screen produced 224 relevant, 125 uncertain, and 821 not relevant, yielding a stated-intention
pool of 62 distinct studies, a realized-fertility pool of 8, and a separate theory stream of 142, with 7
reviews excluded from pooling.

Three notes on the pipeline's own errors, since a reader should be able to tell which numbers have been
audited.

The screening rubric was revised after a five-batch pilot exposed twelve defects, the largest being an
undefined verdict polarity for theory papers that governed a majority of the relevant set. The pilot
verdicts were discarded as diagnostic and the full corpus was re-screened under the revised rubric.
Re-running the batch builder left all thirty batch payloads byte-identical, so the two rubrics are
comparable on identical records.

A deduplication defect was found and fixed while drafting this chapter. Records were compared by
identifier or by title, rather than by both, so a preprint and its published version survived as two
studies. This inflated the scarcest count in the chapter. After the fix the pools are 62, 8, and 142,
revised down from 64, 9, and 145.

Retrieval-side checks: a duplicate record of one routing decoy entered the frame and correctly routed
away, giving one live end-to-end routing test. The overlap test confirmed that the five search families
are operationally distinct, with no pair reaching the merge threshold. The production query reaches
85.9% of the frozen gold on title and abstract, and 100% of the realized-fertility studies.
