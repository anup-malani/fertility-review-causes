# Chapter: Climate Anxiety and Eco-Doomerism

**Category:** Cultural and psychological
**Primary mechanism:** Dread about the ecological future suppresses childbearing even where the desire for children remains intact, so fertility falls without a fall in how many children people say they want.
**Target phenomena:** Second demographic transition only (a 21st-century phenomenon; there is no pre-modern or first-transition cell)
**Cross-references:** postmaterialist value change (D.1.a, in which the desire for children genuinely falls); the mental-health epidemic (D.3.a, in which the anxiety is general rather than ecological); economic uncertainty (C.5.a, in which the feared object is personal income rather than planetary habitability)

Two limits are stated rather than hidden: six of the twenty-two sampled stated-intention studies were never retrieved, and one study central to the chapter's distinctive claim exists here only as an abstract. A second-reader verification pass over the extracted values has not been run.

## 1. Verdict

Ecological dread is measurably widespread among young adults, and it is associated with lower stated intentions to have children. Whether it changes births is not established, and the evidence that bears on the question points in more than one direction.

| Phenomenon | Causal credibility | Demographic significance | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pre-modern | Not applicable | Not applicable | The mechanism requires knowledge of anthropogenic climate change. |
| First transition | Not applicable | Not applicable | Same. The hypothesis is scoped to the second transition only. |
| Second transition, stated intentions | Very low | Not identified | Associations are common, most clearly on whether to have any children at all. The outcome is not a birth. |
| Second transition, realized fertility | Very low | 0 to about 4.5% of the birth hazard, as an illustrative bound and not an estimate | One German panel shows an association; a second analysis of the same panel, a larger Swedish sample, and the cohort born after 1970 do not. |
| Second transition, the distinctive claim | Very low | Unidentified | One study separates fear from desire by design. Its full text could not be obtained. |

Three findings drive these ratings.

The first is that the strongest realized-fertility result fails its own test of what makes this hypothesis distinctive. Golovina and Jokela find that Germans very worried about climate change have an 18% lower likelihood of having children, adjusting for education, income, employment, personality, and political attitudes. It is the only study in the realized evidence that controls for the political orientation that otherwise confounds this literature. But when they additionally adjust for a respondent's summed worry across all other topics, climate worry ceases to be significant, while worry about personal health, crime, and immigration survives. The ecological content of the fear is not shown to be what matters.

The second is that the association is absent in the cohort the hypothesis is about. Peters and colleagues, using the same German panel over a longer window, find no relationship between environmental concern and first births overall, and a precise null among those born in 1970 or later. Their one significant estimate is among people born before 1970. A hypothesis about young adults in the 2020s is supported, in the only cohort-stratified evidence available, in the cohort it does not describe.

The third is that a substantial minority of this literature does not measure ecological fear at all. Of fifteen sampled stated-intention studies, one measures endorsement that government should address environmental problems and four use composites blending fear with pro-environmental behaviour or knowledge. Two of eight realized-fertility studies had to be reassigned to the postmaterialist-values chapter on the same grounds. This is a finding about the field, not a cleaning step, and it is discussed in Section 5.

## 2. The Claim and Mechanism

The claim is that a person can want children and not have them because of what they believe about the future of the planet. Dread does the work that, in competing accounts, is done by preference. The mechanism is affective rather than calculative: it runs through fear about the world a child would inhabit, or through a moral judgement about the emissions a child would add, and not through a revised estimate of the returns to childrearing.

That distinction gives the hypothesis its content and makes it hard to test. Three neighbouring accounts predict the same falling fertility among the same people.

The first is postmaterialist value change, on which the desire for children has genuinely fallen, displaced by self-actualization, autonomy, and career. Someone who says they do not want children because the world is burning may be describing a real fear, or may be giving a socially creditable reason for a preference they hold anyway. Separating those requires showing the desire is live and the fear is what suppresses it.

The second is the general mental-health account. Anxiety about the future has risen across many domains, and if childbearing falls with anxiety generally, the ecological content of the fear is incidental. The discriminating question is whether the feared object is specifically ecological.

The third is economic uncertainty. The shape is identical, a future that looks bad suppressing a present commitment, but the feared object is personal income and the mechanism is option value rather than dread.

The hypothesis also has a sharp reverse-causality problem. Parenthood changes how people think about the future, so a correlation between climate worry and childlessness can run the other way.

## 3. How the Evidence Is Organized

The most important distinction is not topical but concerns what the outcome measures.

| Outcome level | What it measures | Role in the chapter |
|---|---|---|
| Stated intention or attitude | What people say about wanting, planning, or hesitating over children | The bulk of the evidence, and always flagged as stated |
| Realized fertility | Births that actually occurred | The scarce and decisive stratum |

These are never combined. An intention is not a birth, and pooling them would let a well-populated measure of what people say stand in for a nearly absent measure of what they do. The chapter therefore carries two tracks and produces no combined estimate.

## 4. Inclusion Approach

The review began with seventeen independently chosen anchor studies spanning the ecological-fear streams and the three neighbouring topics a search could confuse with them. The anchors were verified to exist before use, which caught one seminal-list entry that does not exist as described. They then seeded a candidate pool built from the studies they cite and the studies that cite them, reaching papers by a route that does not inherit a keyword search's blind spots. All 1,170 candidates were read blind at title and abstract and sorted into a stream, into a neighbouring chapter, or out.

Three boundary rules were applied as hard lines. Against postmaterialist value change, a paper counts here only if the operative content is ecological fear and the desire for children has not simply been relabelled as a preference for freedom or career. Against the mental-health account, the feared object must be specifically ecological. Against economic uncertainty, the feared object must be planetary habitability or the ethics of emissions, not personal job or income risk.

One further rule was added after a pilot: studies of physical climate exposure (heat, drought, disaster) and its effect on fertility belong to a different mechanism, running through biology and economic shock rather than through what people feel. Where the exposure is physical but the outcome is a reproductive *intention*, the paper stays in scope.

**A human review of these boundary calls found that the automated screen cannot enforce the first of them.** Whether a study measures ecological fear or environmental values turns on what its exposure variable contains, and that lives in the methods section, invisible to any screen reading titles and abstracts. Two realized-fertility studies described themselves accurately as studying "environmental concern and fertility" while measuring, respectively, support for government environmental spending and an index of seven household habits: leaving lights on, running the tap, bringing a shopping bag. Both were reassigned. The boundary is therefore enforced at extraction, by recording what each exposure variable actually contains.

## 5. Included Studies and Evidence Map

### 5.1 The realized-fertility evidence

Eight studies were identified. Review reduced them to five: a formal growth model containing no empirical fertility moved to the theory stream, and two moved to the postmaterialist-values chapter on measure content. Of the five, four were retrieved.

| Study | Setting | Estimate | |
|---|---|---|---|
| Golovina & Jokela 2024 | German panel, 2009–2020 | Hazard ratio 0.82 (0.75, 0.90) for the very worried | Adjusts for political attitudes |
| | Same, childless subsample | 0.86 (0.79, 0.94) | |
| Peters et al. 2023 | Same German panel, 1984–2020 | 0.92 (0.82, 1.04) | Not significant |
| | Born before 1970 | 0.73 (0.57, 0.94) | Significant |
| | Born 1970 or later | 0.98 (0.85, 1.12) | Precise null |
| Jylhä et al. 2025 | Sweden, n = 8,027 | Climate worry on number of children, b = −0.01 (p = 0.45) | Null on all three outcomes |
| Weychert et al. 2026 | UK, preprint | Risk ratio 0.38 for high news exposure among the pro-environmental | Interaction only; main effect null |
| Air pollution and fertility, 2025 | Thailand | Not retrieved | Retained by reviewer decision |

Two features of this table matter more than any single row. Golovina and Peters analyse the same German panel and disagree; the specifications differ, but the disagreement is a fact about the evidence rather than something to resolve. And the largest sample, Sweden, is a well-powered null in which worry about future generations is associated with *more* children, not fewer, while worry about overpopulation is associated with fewer.

### 5.2 The stated-intention evidence

Sixty studies remained after review. Extracting all of them would have required retrieving some thirty-nine further papers from publishers that block automated access; extracting only the freely available ones would have selected on open-access status, which is the error that limits the companion chapter on evolutionary decoupling to a tenth of its intended evidence. A stratified random sample of twenty was therefore drawn, with the two studies bearing on the distinctive claim included by census. Sixteen of the twenty-two were retrieved.

The sample supports three observations.

**Associations are common but concentrated on one margin.** Bastianelli, using representative samples from Finland, Estonia, and Sweden, finds that the very worried are more likely to intend to remain childless and no more likely to intend fewer children overall. An effect on whether to have any children, with none on how many, is a different demographic object from a uniform reduction in family size.

**The nulls appear where an effect should be easiest to find.** Daeninck and colleagues surveyed environmental degree students in the United Kingdom, a population selected for ecological engagement, and found that climate anxiety strongly shaped career planning but not family planning. A null in that sample is more informative than a null in a general one.

**When ecological worry is set against its competitors in a single design, it loses.** Kurowska and colleagues ran a factorial survey in Poland (n = 1,337) in which employment stability, housing affordability, childcare, domestic labour, climate change, and abortion rights were varied against one another. Economic security dominated; climate concerns were less influential. This is the mirror image of what Golovina finds on German births, where climate worry is significant and economic worry is not. The two comparisons are internal to their studies and point in opposite directions.

### 5.3 The distinctive claim

The claim that separates this hypothesis from postmaterialist value change is that fertility falls while the desire for children remains. Four studies were originally assigned here. Review left one.

Two were reassigned: an interview-based humanities article with no estimand moved to the theory stream, and a survey study moved to a general ecological-pessimism cell because its measure asks respondents themselves to attribute a shortfall between wanted and planned children to climate emotions, which is a respondent's account of the mechanism rather than a design that separates fear from desire. A third, a study of Bangladeshi students, models intention and intended number separately and finds concerned students still want children while planning fewer; the separation is real but weak, and its only significant environmental predictor is an ethical item about the emissions of an additional child.

That leaves Helm and colleagues, who compare people at different levels of climate-reproductive concern and find no differences in parenthood attitudes or pro-reproductive attitudes while the preferred number of children does differ. Separately measured attitudes held flat while intended quantity moves is genuine design-level separation. **This study's full text could not be obtained through any open route, and what is reported here comes from its abstract.** No effect size is available, and it cannot enter any synthesis.

### 5.4 Theory stream

One hundred and forty-four studies of ecological ethics, antinatalist argument, and the climate-anxiety construct itself. The theory literature is more than twice the size of the empirical core and more than twenty times the size of the surviving realized-fertility evidence. That ratio is the organizing finding rather than a gap: the ethical and conceptual literature on whether one should have children in a climate crisis is mature, and the evidence on whether ecological dread changes births is not.

## 6. Quantitative Synthesis

**No pooled estimate is reported for either track, and the reason is not that the pooling failed.** The review's rule requires a common outcome family, a harmonised effect metric, and at least three independent studies. The realized evidence does not meet it, and would not meet it even if every study were retrieved.

Four realized estimates were poolable in principle. They reduce to two studies, and both analyse the same German panel, and counting them separately would double-count one dataset. The Swedish study reports ordinary-least-squares coefficients on completed family size, which is a different quantity from a hazard of birth and cannot be harmonised with it. The UK preprint reports no confidence interval for any coefficient. That leaves one independent estimate, which is not a meta-analysis.

The stated track is not pooled either, for a different reason: it was sampled rather than censused, and a pooled estimate over sixteen studies drawn from sixty would misrepresent its own coverage.

The pre-specified test of this chapter, whether associations survive adjustment for political orientation, education, and religiosity, could not be estimated. With three independent data sources, one of which adjusts for political attitudes, there is no contrast to estimate. That test remains the right one and remains unanswered.

What can be said is narrower and firmer. Where ecological worry is measured well and adjusted properly, one German panel finds an association with births of about 18%. Another analysis of that same panel does not. A larger Swedish sample does not. And the effect is absent in the cohort born after 1970.

## 7. Demographic Significance

There is no pre-modern or first-transition cell.

For stated intentions, no demographic share can be assigned. Converting an intention association into a share of fertility decline requires knowing how much of an intention gap becomes a birth gap, and this literature does not estimate that. The intention effect is also concentrated on the extensive margin, so it is not a uniform reduction in family size even on its own terms.

For realized fertility, an illustrative upper bound can be constructed and should be read as an upper bound rather than a finding. About a quarter of United States youth aged 16 to 24 agree that climate change makes them question whether they will have children, with a further third saying maybe (Vercammen et al., n = 2,834). That is the best prevalence estimate available. Applying the German hazard reduction of 18% to that quarter gives roughly 4.5% of the birth hazard.

Every step of that calculation is weak. The prevalence is American and the hazard German. Questioning whether one will have children is not intending to forgo them, and neither is a birth. The hazard reduction comes from a single estimate that does not survive adjustment for general worry. And the floor of the range is zero for a substantive reason, not as a formality: the only cohort-stratified estimate available finds no effect among those born after 1970.

For the distinctive claim, no share can be assigned. This mirrors the evolutionary-decoupling chapter, where the distinctive claim was likewise recorded as unidentified.

## 8. External Validity and Transportability

The evidence concentrates in Europe, North America, and Australia, and in educated survey populations. Two exceptions are worth naming, because they are the only evidence on whether the mechanism travels: a study of university students in Bangladesh, and a study of fertility intentions in rural Malawi following Cyclone Idai. Both are in higher-fertility settings, and neither measures births.

The literature is also very new: most stated-intention studies date from 2024 or later. That is appropriate to a recent phenomenon, but it means a substantial share of the evidence has not been through replication, and a chapter written now will date faster than others in this review.

The narrowness is not incidental. Climate anxiety as a measured construct is most prevalent in populations already at low fertility, so the settings that best identify the mechanism are the settings where the outcome has least room to move.

## 9. Risk of Bias and Identification Quality

Six studies were assessed: three serious, two moderate, one not assessable because its full text could not be obtained. Risk of bias was not graded from an abstract.

The pattern matters more than the counts. **No study in the realized evidence is strong on both of the dimensions that matter here.** Golovina and Jokela handle the confounding between political orientation and fertility better than anything else in the literature, and fail the test of whether the fear's ecological content is what does the work. Peters and colleagues have the only design in the review that models the relationship in both directions across thirty-six years of panel data, and control for no political variables at all.

Three risks recur. Confounding is the first: political orientation, education, and secularism each predict both climate concern and low fertility, and only one study adjusts for the first of these. Reverse causality is the second, and only one study addresses it. Common-method bias is the third: most stated-intention studies measure exposure and outcome by self-report in a single instrument.

Two reporting problems were found by reading tables against text. One paper's abstract reports a 16% reduction where its own figure gives 14%. Another narrates "a roughly 15% lower likelihood of entering fatherhood" for a coefficient whose confidence interval includes no effect. Neither is fatal; both are reasons to quote figures rather than prose.

### 10.1 Rating

Both empirical tracks rate **very low**. Starting from the low certainty appropriate to observational evidence, the realized track is downgraded for risk of bias, for inconsistency between two analyses of one panel and between two internal comparisons that point opposite ways, for indirectness because the ecological content of the fear is not shown to matter, and for imprecision because no pooled estimate is possible. The stated track is downgraded for design, for indirectness on both the outcome and the exposure, for inconsistency, and for sampled coverage.

These ratings are generated from the evidence tables rather than entered by hand, so they cannot drift from the underlying counts.

## 11. Interpretation for Economics Readers

The hypothesis claims a fertility decline without a change in preferences, driven by a belief about the state of the world. In the standard model children are a good, and fertility falls when demand falls or the price rises. This hypothesis holds demand fixed and changes the expected quality of the world the good is consumed in, or attaches a moral cost to producing it.

That framing makes the identification problem legible, and the literature's central weakness is that it has abundant measurements of belief, abundant measurements of stated desire, and almost no linkage of either to births.

There is an interpretive trap worth naming. A stated reason is not a revealed preference. When a survey respondent says they are not having children because of the climate, that statement is jointly produced by their actual constraint and by which reasons are socially available to give. One study in the sample recruited participants *because* they had already limited their childbearing for environmental reasons; it characterises their reasoning well and can say nothing about whether ecological concern causes the choice.

The most useful comparison in the evidence may be incidental. In the same German models, worry about personal health, crime, and immigration predicts lower fertility more robustly than worry about climate does. Whatever is happening, it does not look specific to the planet.

## 12. Limitations and Extensions

The realized evidence is thin and unreplicated, and the one clear positive result fails its own specificity test.

The distinctive claim rests on a single study whose full text could not be obtained. Six of twenty-two sampled stated-intention studies were never retrieved, so the sample is usable but incomplete.

A second-reader verification pass over the extracted values has not been run. This is not a formality: the first pass through this evidence produced two errors that were caught only by returning to the original tables and figures, and one of them reversed a reported conclusion.

**On the boundary with general anxiety**, this review deliberately scoped itself to fear with specifically ecological content and did not assemble a comparison with the general-anxiety literature. That remains a limit on interpretation. But the chapter is no longer silent on the question. Golovina and Jokela's robustness check, in which climate worry loses significance once general worry is controlled, is a within-study test of exactly this, and it does not favour the hypothesis. Helm and colleagues report that climate anxiety distinguishes levels of reproductive concern where general depressive symptoms do not, which cuts the other way. Two studies, pointing in opposite directions, is not a resolution, but it is not nothing either.

Three extensions would move the chapter. A design linking a validated climate-anxiety measure to subsequent births in a panel, which would convert the stated-intention literature from a proxy into a tested predictor. A design holding measured desire for children fixed while ecological belief varies, which is the only thing that can identify the distinctive claim. And any study reporting climate worry alongside general worry in the same model, since the handful of studies that do so are currently the chapter's best evidence on whether the ecological content matters at all.

## 13. Reproducibility Appendix

Seventeen existence-verified anchor studies produced a citation-based candidate pool of 1,170 deduplicated records, screened blind at title and abstract. The screen yielded 224 relevant, 125 uncertain, and 821 not relevant.

A human review of the decisive studies overturned five of the twelve it examined, including the two reassignments to the postmaterialist-values chapter described in Section 4. Retrieval reached 41 of 67 pool studies; the single most productive source was Europe PMC, which serves deposited copies of papers whose publishers block automated access.

Three pipeline errors were found and fixed, and are recorded because a reader should be able to tell which numbers have been audited. A deduplication defect compared records by identifier or by title rather than both, so a preprint and its published version survived as two studies. A retrieval defect treated the absence of a direct file link as evidence that no copy existed, causing three freely available studies to be logged unreachable. And one bibliographic record carried the wrong title for a published article, which the chapter reproduced until the paper itself was opened.
