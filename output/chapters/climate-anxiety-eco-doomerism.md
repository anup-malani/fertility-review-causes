# Climate Anxiety and Eco-Doomerism

**Category:** Cultural and psychological
**Primary mechanism:** Dread about the ecological future suppresses childbearing even where the desire for children remains intact, so fertility falls without a fall in how many children people say they want.
**Cross-references:** D.1.a postmaterialist value change (where the desire for children genuinely falls); D.3.a the mental-health epidemic (where the anxiety is general rather than ecological); C.5.a economic uncertainty (where the feared object is personal income rather than planetary habitability). Two realized-fertility studies were reassigned to D.1.a during this review on measure content.
**Status:** TICK-047–053. Rewritten against `docs/chapter-template.md` on 2026-08-29; previous draft 2026-07-27. Not PI-reviewed in this form, and no second-reader verification pass has been run over the extracted values. Written on 20 of 26 wanted full texts (77%).

## 1. The claim

This chapter explores the effect of ecological dread on fertility.

### 1.1 In plain terms

In plain terms: some people are frightened about what is happening to the planet, and the claim is that this fear stops them having children they would otherwise have had. The point is not that they have stopped wanting children. It is that they want them and do not have them, because of what they believe the world will be like.

That distinction is the whole claim, and it is what makes it hard to check. Plenty of people who are worried about the climate also have fewer children — but so do people who are worried about money, about crime, or about their health, and so do people who simply want fewer children and mention the climate when asked why. To show that this particular fear is doing the work, you have to show that the wish for children is still there and that the fear is what is standing in its way.

Almost everything measured in this literature is what people *say* about children they might have one day. Very little of it is children who were or were not born. Those are different things, and the chapter keeps them apart from beginning to end.

### 1.2 The claim precisely

The parameter this chapter estimates is the change in the probability of a birth caused by an increase in specifically ecological dread, holding the desire for children fixed, measured as a proportional change in the first-birth hazard, signed so that a negative value means fewer births.

The chapter is scoped to the **SDT** (the Second Demographic Transition, the fall to below-replacement fertility from roughly 1965 onward) alone. **PM** (pre-modern variation) and **FDT** (the First Demographic Transition, roughly 1870–1965) have no cell here: the mechanism requires knowledge of anthropogenic climate change, which did not exist.

The registered claim decomposes into two sub-claims that this literature routinely runs together:

- **Sub-claim A — the association.** Ecological dread is associated with lower fertility. This is well populated and weakly identified.
- **Sub-claim B — the distinctive claim.** The association survives holding the *desire* for children fixed, and depends on the fear's specifically **ecological** content rather than on anxiety in general. This is what separates the hypothesis from D.1.a and D.3.a. Without it, the hypothesis has no independent content.

The counterfactual is a ceteris-paribus perturbation: hold desired family size, political orientation, education, religiosity and general anxiety fixed, and raise ecological dread. No study in the evidence base delivers this. One study approaches it by design (§6.3) and its full text could not be obtained.

**This is a behavioural parameter with no identity arm.** Nothing here must hold by accounting; the mechanism can fail at every link, and §8 does not compute a decomposition from arithmetic.

**Margin.** The best-identified stated-intention finding is on the **extensive** margin — whether to have any children at all — with no association on how many (Bastianelli). An extensive-margin effect is a different demographic object from a uniform reduction in family size, and §8 does not treat them as interchangeable.

**The reverse-causality problem is unusually sharp.** Parenthood changes how people think about the future, so a correlation between climate worry and childlessness can run either way. One study in the entire evidence base models both directions.

## 2. Theoretical mechanism

In the reader's own vocabulary: this hypothesis holds demand for children fixed and changes the expected quality of the world in which the good is consumed, or attaches a moral cost to producing it. It is not a price effect and not a preference shift — which is precisely why it is hard to separate from the two neighbouring accounts that *are*.

**D.1.a, postmaterialist value change**, predicts the same falling fertility among the same people through a genuine fall in the desire for children. A respondent who says they do not want children because the world is burning may be reporting a real fear, or supplying a socially creditable reason for a preference they hold anyway.

**D.3.a, the general mental-health account**, predicts the same fall if anxiety has risen across many domains and childbearing falls with anxiety generally. The discriminating question is whether the feared object is specifically ecological.

**C.5.a, economic uncertainty**, has an identical shape — a bad-looking future suppressing a present commitment — but the feared object is personal income and the mechanism is option value rather than dread.

**What would make the hypothesis wrong.** It is wrong if the association disappears once general worry is controlled, or once desired family size is held fixed. Both tests have been run, once each. §6.2 reports what they found.

## 5. The ideal design

Written before the literature was read, so that §6 can be compared against a fixed yardstick rather than against the best paper that happens to exist.

### 5.1 The ideal estimand

The change in the probability of a first birth within five years, in percentage points, caused by a one-standard-deviation increase in **validated, climate-specific dread**, **holding desired family size and general anxiety fixed**, among childless adults aged 20–35 in a below-replacement setting.

Three clauses carry the weight. *Validated and climate-specific* excludes the environmental-values and household-habit composites that §4 shows contaminate a quarter to a third of this literature. *Holding desired family size fixed* is the distinctive claim — without it the estimand is D.1.a's, not this chapter's. *Holding general anxiety fixed* is the boundary against D.3.a. An estimate missing any one of the three does not identify this hypothesis, whatever its standard error.

### 5.2 The design that would identify it

**Source of variation — and why the obvious instrument is unavailable.** Ecological dread cannot be randomly assigned. The tempting instrument is a salient local climate event, but its exclusion restriction is almost certainly false: a flood or wildfire also destroys property and income, which is C.5.a's mechanism, and affects reproductive biology directly, which W4 excludes from this chapter. An instrument that moves three mechanisms at once identifies none of them. **The credible design here is therefore not an experiment but a selection-on-observables panel design with the right observables** — and this section's job is to say exactly which observables those are.

**Comparison group.** Childless adults in the same panel, same waves, differing in baseline climate dread.

**Identifying assumption.** Conditional on desired family size, a multi-domain worry battery, political orientation, education and religiosity, baseline climate dread is as good as randomly assigned with respect to subsequent births.

**The assumption is partly falsifiable, and this is the design's best feature.** The non-climate worry domains are built-in placebos. If worry about health, crime or immigration predicts births as strongly as climate worry does, the ecological content is not doing the work and the hypothesis fails on its own terms. This is not hypothetical: it is exactly the robustness check Golovina and Jokela ran, and §6.2 reports that it failed.

**Estimating equation.** A discrete-time hazard of first birth on baseline climate dread with the full control set, re-estimated substituting each non-climate worry domain in turn, plus a specification adding desired family size as a control to test whether the association survives it.

**Data required.** One panel carrying all four of: a validated climate-anxiety scale; a desired-family-size item; a multi-domain worry battery; and linked subsequent births. GSOEP has the worry battery and the births. It has neither of the other two.

**Sample size.** To detect a hazard ratio of 0.90 at 80% power requires about **2,830 observed first births**; the literature's headline estimate of 0.82 would need about **800**. With roughly a fifth of a childless young-adult sample having a first birth over a five-year window, that is a panel in the tens of thousands.

**No study implements the ideal design, and the reason is unusually specific: the ideal design is split across two studies, neither of which has the other's half.**

Golovina and Jokela have the outcome, the panel, the political controls and the general-worry battery — everything except a validated dread measure and any way to hold desire fixed. Helm et al. have the one design in the entire literature that holds attitudes fixed while intended quantity moves — and their outcome is an intention, they report no effect size, and their full text could not be obtained.

Neither is a partial version of the ideal. Each is one half of it. Splicing them is not possible, because the missing link between them is precisely the intention-to-birth translation rate that §6.4 identifies as unestimated anywhere in this literature.

That is why §9 rates every cell in this chapter NOT ASSESSED, and it was foreseeable from §5.1 without reading a paper: an estimand defined by three simultaneous conditions will not be identified by a literature in which no dataset satisfies more than two.

### 6.2 The naive estimator, and the two tests the hypothesis has actually faced

**What is the naive estimator here?** Correlate a measure of environmental concern with a fertility outcome in a cross-section.

It is confounded three ways at once, and all three run the same direction. Political orientation, education and secularism each independently predict *both* higher climate concern and lower fertility. Reverse causation compounds it: parenthood changes how people regard the future. And because most stated-intention studies measure exposure and outcome by self-report in one instrument, common-method bias adds a fourth. **Every one of these biases inflates the apparent effect.** There is no offsetting force.

**How many included studies use it?** Most of the stated track, and two of the four retrieved realized studies adjust for none of the confounders named above. Only Golovina and Jokela adjust for political attitudes. Only Peters et al. model the relationship in both directions.

**And no study in the realized evidence is strong on both dimensions.** This is the chapter's central structural fact. Golovina handles political confounding better than anything else in the literature and fails the specificity test. Peters has the only bidirectional design across thirty-six years of panel and controls for no political variables at all. The two strengths never appear in the same study.

**The hypothesis has faced its two decisive tests once each, and lost one and a half.**

*Test 1 — is the ecological content what matters?* In Golovina and Jokela's own fully adjusted model, worry about **economic** matters gives HR 0.94 (0.86, 1.04), not significant, against ecological worry's 0.82. That looks like a win for the hypothesis. But their further robustness check, adjusting for a respondent's summed worry across all other topics, removes climate worry's significance while worry about personal health, crime and immigration survives. The load-bearing result fails its own specificity test inside the paper that reports it.

*Test 2 — does the association hold in the cohort the hypothesis describes?* Peters et al. supply the only estimates in the evidence base split by **birth cohort** (a group of people born in the same period, followed over time, as against a snapshot of everyone alive in one year). Among those born before 1970: HR 0.73, significant. Among those born 1970 or later: **HR 0.98 (0.85, 1.12)** — a precise null, not an underpowered one. A hypothesis about young adults in the 2020s is supported only in the cohort it does not describe.

**Are the two GSOEP analyses a disagreement to resolve, or one to average?** Neither. They are the same dataset analysed with different specifications, so they are not independent evidence and cannot be pooled; and the difference between them is not an estimator defect one of them corrects. It is a genuine specification disagreement about one panel, and the correct report is both numbers with the note that there is one dataset underneath.

### 6.3 The distinctive claim — one design, no effect size

Four studies were assigned here; review left one. Two were reassigned (an interview-based humanities article with no estimand, to theory; and a survey whose measure asks respondents to *attribute* a shortfall between wanted and planned children to climate emotions, which is a respondent's account of the mechanism rather than a design separating fear from desire). A third, on Bangladeshi students, models intention and intended number separately and finds concerned students still want children while planning fewer — real separation, but weak, and its only significant environmental predictor is an ethical item about the emissions of an additional child.

That leaves **Helm et al.**, who compare people at different levels of climate-reproductive concern and find no differences in parenthood attitudes or pro-reproductive attitudes while the preferred number of children does differ. Attitudes held flat while intended quantity moves is genuine design-level separation, and it is the only such design in the chapter.

**Its full text could not be obtained through any open route. What is reported here comes from its abstract. No effect size is available and it enters no synthesis.**

### 6.4 The transmission ledger

| Stage | Question | Sign |
|---|---|---|
| Ecological dread felt → measured as dread | Does the instrument capture fear rather than values or habits? | **Fails 25–37% of the time** (§4) |
| Measured dread → stated intention | Does it move what people say? | Holds; concentrated on the extensive margin |
| Stated intention → birth foregone | What fraction of an intention gap becomes a birth gap? | **Unknown — not estimated anywhere in this literature** |
| Birth foregone → foregone for life | Postponed or abandoned? | Not measured; no study follows cohorts to completion |
| Aggregate → the SDT | Was the mechanism operating over the period being explained? | Attenuates sharply: the construct postdates most of the SDT |

The third row is the chapter's binding constraint. Everything upstream of it is measured, everything downstream depends on a translation rate nobody has estimated, and no amount of further stated-intention evidence supplies it.

## 7. Quantitative synthesis

### 7.1 The answer in plain terms

Lots of young people say the state of the planet makes them think twice about having children, and that is well established. Whether it changes how many children get born is not, and the small amount of evidence that speaks to it does not agree with itself.

The one clear positive result comes from a German survey, where people who were very worried about the climate were about 18% less likely to have a first child. Two things undercut it. The same team found that once you account for how much a person worries about *everything* — their health, crime, immigration — the climate part stops mattering, while those other worries keep mattering. And a second study of the very same German data found nothing at all among people born after 1970, which is the group the whole idea is about.

The larger Swedish study found nothing either. So there is one positive finding, from one country's survey, that does not survive its authors' own check and does not appear in the right age group.

### 7.2 The estimate

**No pooled estimate is reported for either track, and for the realized track that is not a failed pooling.** The rule requires a common outcome family, a harmonised metric, and at least three independent studies. Four realized estimates are poolable in principle; they reduce to two studies, and **both analyse GSOEP**. Counting them separately would double-count one dataset. Sweden reports OLS coefficients on completed family size, a different quantity from a birth hazard. The UK preprint reports no confidence interval. That leaves **one independent estimate**, which is not a meta-analysis.

**For the stated track, the chapter must correct its predecessor.** The previous draft said the stated track was not pooled because "it was sampled rather than censused" — a design choice. The generated pooling-decision table says something materially different: *"61 studies in the frozen stated pool; no effect extraction has been run. Absence of a pooled estimate here reflects work not done, not evidence weighed."* Those are not the same statement, and the pipeline's is the accurate one. No effect extraction exists for the stated track at all.

**The chapter's pre-specified test could not be run.** Whether associations survive adjustment for political orientation, education and religiosity requires a contrast across studies that differ in their adjustment. With one independent data source, there is no contrast. The test remains the right one and remains unanswered.

## 8. Demographic significance

The phenomenon to be explained is measured in whole children of **completed fertility** (the number of children a woman has actually borne by the end of her childbearing years) — the SDT is a fall of roughly one and a half births per woman; this mechanism offers a proportional reduction in the first-birth hazard, whose only available magnitude is an illustrative bound built from two borrowed inputs.

**A units error in the previous draft has to be corrected before the bound is used.** That draft reported the realized-fertility significance as "0 to about 4.5% of the birth hazard" and placed it in a demographic-significance table whose verdict vocabulary is defined as a percentage *of the phenomenon*. Those are different denominators. 4.5% is a share of the birth **level**; the phenomenon is a **decline**. A mechanism accounting for 4.5% of the level accounts for a considerably larger share of a decline that is itself a fraction of the level — if the SDT decline is roughly half the starting level, the corrected share is closer to **9%**, which is MINOR rather than NEGLIGIBLE.

This does not make the hypothesis better supported; identification is unchanged and very low. It means the reassuringly small number in the previous draft was small partly because it was measured against the wrong thing. The corrected share cannot be computed exactly here, for the same reason as in the OAS chapter: the UN panel of **total fertility rates** (TFR: the number of children a woman would bear if she experienced current age-specific birth rates throughout her reproductive life) is not reachable from this repository. **Whether the bound is stated against the level or the decline is PI call 2**, and it moves the verdict across a band boundary.

**The endogeneity check** is unusually favourable here and should be said: climate anxiety is not plausibly *caused by* the fertility decline, so the feedback problem that afflicts most chapters in this review does not arise. The chapter's problems are confounding and reverse causation at the individual level, not endogeneity of the mechanism to the phenomenon.

### 8.1 Pre-modern fertility variation

For pre-modern variation, the verdict is NOT ASSESSED, because the phenomenon is out of scope for this hypothesis in the registry rather than an empty in-scope cell — the mechanism requires knowledge of anthropogenic climate change, which did not exist.

Were it assessable, the expected sign would be zero: there is no treatment to vary.

### 8.2 First Demographic Transition

For the First Demographic Transition, the verdict is NOT ASSESSED, on the same ground: the hypothesis is scoped to the second transition only and the cell is out of scope rather than empty.

### 8.3 Second Demographic Transition

For the Second Demographic Transition, the verdict is NOT ASSESSED for realized fertility, because the one quantity available is an illustrative bound rather than an estimate, its denominator is unresolved (§8 above), and it rests on a single dataset whose result does not survive its own authors' specificity check.

This needs stating carefully, because NOT ASSESSED here means something different from §§8.1–7.2. There the cell is out of scope. Here it is **in scope, populated, and still unassessable**: five studies exist, four were retrieved, and they yield one independent estimate that cannot bear a decomposition. Reporting this as NEGLIGIBLE would claim a measurement that was not made; reporting it as MINOR would credit an illustrative bound as an estimate.

Three points support the reading, and one qualifies it.

The association is absent in the cohort the hypothesis describes — HR 0.98 (0.85, 1.12) for those born from 1970 — and that is the single most informative number in the chapter, because it is precise rather than merely non-significant.

The largest sample is a null, and points partly the other way: in Sweden, worry about future generations is associated with *more* children, while worry about overpopulation is associated with fewer.

The construct postdates most of the SDT. Fertility in the rich world had fallen most of the way to its present level before climate anxiety was a measurable population phenomenon, which bounds how much of the transition it could explain regardless of its individual-level effect.

The qualification is that for **stated intentions** the picture is genuinely different: associations are common and reasonably consistent. But an intention is not a birth, the translation rate is unestimated (§6.4), and the effect is concentrated on whether to have any children rather than on how many. That cell is NOT ASSESSED as well, and for the cleanest of reasons — the outcome is not the outcome.

## 10. Verdict

Ecological dread is real, widespread among young adults, and associated with what people say about having children. Whether it changes births is not established, and the small evidence that bears on it does not agree with itself.

**The one number to carry away: HR 0.98, with a confidence interval of 0.85 to 1.12.** That is the association between environmental concern and first birth among Germans born in 1970 or later — the only cohort-stratified estimate in the evidence base, and the cohort the hypothesis is actually about. It is a precise null, not an underpowered one.

Three qualifications belong inside this verdict.

The one clear positive result — an 18% lower first-birth hazard among the very worried — fails its own specificity test inside the paper reporting it: adjusting for a respondent's total worry removes it, while worry about health, crime and immigration survives. Whatever is happening does not look specific to the planet.

There is one independent dataset in the realized evidence. Two of the four retrieved studies analyse the same German panel and disagree; the largest sample, Sweden, is a well-powered null in which worry about future generations predicts *more* children.

The claim that distinguishes this hypothesis from the postmaterialist-values chapter — fertility falling while desire holds — has exactly one study of the right design, and its full text could not be obtained.

**What would change it:** a design linking a validated climate-anxiety measure to subsequent births in a panel, reporting climate worry alongside general worry in the same model. That single specification would settle both the association and the specificity question, and no study currently reports it.

## 11. Open questions

**PI calls required.**

1. **Should the stated-intention track be extracted at all?** The pooling table records that no effect extraction has been run on 61 studies. Given that the intention-to-birth translation rate is unestimated (§6.4), extraction may produce a well-populated table that still cannot support a demographic verdict. This is a decision about whether to spend the effort.
2. **Is the demographic-significance bound stated against the fertility level or the decline?** §8 shows the previous draft's 4.5% is a share of the level; against the decline it is roughly 9%, which crosses from NEGLIGIBLE into MINOR. The template's vocabulary is defined as a share of the phenomenon and does not require the denominator to be named. It should.
3. **What GRADE value applies to a cell that is populated but yields no extractable estimate?** The distinctive-claim cell has one study of the right design and no effect size. "No evidence" is defined for an empty cell; VERY LOW misdescribes a cell with nothing to be uncertain about.
4. **Does the W1 bleed-in rate belong in every chapter?** It is measured here (25–37% on the realized track) and is the most useful single diagnostic in this chapter. No other chapter in the review reports one.

**Retrieval and extraction priorities.**

5. Obtain Helm et al. — the distinctive claim rests on it and it exists here only as an abstract.
6. Retrieve the six sampled stated-intention studies not obtained, and the Thailand air-pollution study.
7. Run the second-reader verification pass. This is not a formality: the first pass produced two errors caught only by returning to original tables, and one reversed a reported conclusion.
8. Two reporting problems found by reading tables against text should be logged against their studies: one paper's abstract reports a 16% reduction where its own figure gives 14%; another narrates "a roughly 15% lower likelihood of entering fatherhood" for a coefficient whose interval includes no effect.

**Studies that do not exist and should.** A panel linking a validated climate-anxiety measure to subsequent births, holding desired family size fixed and reporting general worry in the same model. Every component exists separately; no study combines them, which is why the distinctive claim is untested rather than refuted.
