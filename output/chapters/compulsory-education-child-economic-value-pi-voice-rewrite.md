**Category:** Economic  
**Primary mechanism:** A school-attendance law stops children from working, the lost work makes a
child worth less in money to their parents, and parents therefore have fewer children.  
**Fertility decline this chapter tests the hypothesis against:** the First Demographic Transition,
roughly 1870 to 1965. We abbreviate it FDT.

## 1. The claim

Compulsory schooling lowers fertility by making children less profitable to their parents. That is
the claim this chapter evaluates, and it runs as a chain with three links. A school-attendance law
takes a child out of the field, the workshop, or the family store. The hours the child no longer
works are earnings the parents no longer collect, so each child is worth less to them in money. And
parents who expect less money from each child have fewer children.

Every link has to hold for the claim to be true. A law can raise school attendance without taking
children out of work, if they work after school and on weekends. Children's work can fall without
changing what parents want, if the money children brought in was never why parents wanted them. This
chapter asks whether the evidence carries the chain all the way through.

The claim is about how many children a woman has in total, not about when she has them. Demographers
call that total completed fertility: the number of children a woman has by the end of her
childbearing years. A reform that moves a birth from age 17 to age 22, and leaves the woman with the
same number of children at 45, leaves this hypothesis untouched. Birth timing is the subject of a
separate chapter in this review, on tempo effects and birth postponement.

## 2. Almost every available study changes the wrong generation's schooling

The hypothesis needs a law that changes what a woman's children could have earned. Almost every study
we found changes what the woman herself learned in school. Those are two different laws acting on two
different generations, and only one of them is the subject of this chapter.

The strongest of these studies rest on a birth-date cutoff. A country raises its school-leaving age
from 14 to 16 on a fixed date, so a girl born a week before the cutoff may leave school at 14 and a
girl born a week after may not. Researchers then compare the two groups, on the reasoning that they
differ in the law that governed them and in almost nothing else. Economists call a study built on an accident of
history like that one a quasi-experiment, because the law does the sorting that a coin flip would do
in a real experiment. The comparison is clean, and it measures one thing: what two more years of her
own schooling did to her own fertility. Her children were born decades later, under whatever laws and
labor markets prevailed then. Nothing in the comparison says what those children could have earned.

An estimate of that kind reports a total and hides a mechanism. Economists call it a reduced-form
estimate: it gives the whole effect of the law on the woman's births and says nothing about which
link in the chain produced it. Such an estimate can be perfectly credible and still be silent on the
question this chapter asks, which is whether parents had fewer children because children stopped
earning.

We call the mismatch the wrong-generation problem, and it governs how we read every study below. The
literature that looks like a test of this hypothesis mostly is not one. One study escapes the
problem, and Section 7 is about that study.

## 3. Theoretical mechanism

Economic models of child labor agree that where children work, parents have more of them. In any
model where a child contributes labor to the farm or the household, an extra child carries a return
as well as a cost. Child work therefore makes a child cheaper on net, and cheaper things get bought
in larger quantities. Fioroni derives exactly that: child work lowers the cost of a child and raises
fertility.

The models disagree about what a law against child labor does to fertility. Hazan and Berdugo give
the strongest version of the answer this chapter's hypothesis wants. In their model an economy can be
stuck in a trap where child labor and fertility are both high and each holds the other up, and a
temporary ban, or a compulsory-schooling requirement, can push the economy out of the trap toward a
lasting state with little child labor and low fertility. Mookherjee, Prina, and Ray reach a similar
conclusion in a different model, in which tighter regulation of child labor moves education, the mix
of occupations, and long-run fertility together. Emerson and Knabb add what parents expect of the
future: a temporary ban paired with mandatory schooling can move households out of a high-fertility,
low-education pattern and hold them in a low-fertility one, because parents come to expect that their
children will be schooled rather than employed.

Takakura shows that the same ban can raise fertility for some parents and lower it for others. In his
model, banning child labor lowers fertility among unskilled parents and raises it among skilled
parents. The number of births in the country as a whole therefore depends on which parents the ban
actually bites, and the total can move either way. His result is the sharpest piece of theoretical
counterevidence in the set, and it is a warning against reading any single average estimate as the
effect the models predict.

Two more papers hold the theory back from a clean policy conclusion. Baland and Robinson show that
child labor can be inefficient and a ban can improve welfare, and that the effect of the ban on
fertility remains ambiguous in theory. Cigno and Rosati, synthesizing the policy literature, find
that compulsory education or a partial restriction on children's work can establish a self-sustaining
equilibrium in which more schooling happens, while a complete ban may need insurance and education
policy alongside it to work at all.

Greenwood, Seshadri, and Vandenbroucke challenge the premise that the law did the work. Their model
formalizes a closely related mechanism running through children's productivity, and their history
runs the other way around: technology changed, employers wanted skills instead of small hands, and
children left work before effective child-labor regulation arrived. On that account, schooling and
child-labor laws are consequences or companions of an economic transformation rather than its cause.
Their challenge is aimed at what a law can be credited with, and it bears directly on how much weight
the estimates in Sections 6 and 7 can carry.

## 4. How we found the studies

One search supplies this chapter and the chapter on birth postponement, because both hypotheses turn
on the same school-leaving laws. The search did not begin from a keyword query. It began from seven
papers, each found independently, and then followed citations in both directions: every paper those
seven cited, and every paper that cited them. Removing duplicates by DOI and title left 1,255
records.

A blinded screen of titles and abstracts sorted the 1,255 records into three piles: 233 Relevant, 128
Uncertain, and 894 Not relevant. A focused review of exceptions covered 42 records the first screen
had set aside. A strict test then asked of each remaining paper whether it studied an actual
schooling or child-labor law and reported a fertility outcome, and 28 papers passed. Some of those 28
turned out to be two versions of the same study, a working paper and its published article, and some
belonged to the birth-postponement hypothesis rather than this one.

Our review left 16 distinct papers for this chapter: 10 that build or test a theory, and six that
report an estimate of fertility under a schooling reform. We obtained and read all 16. Shanan (2023)
came to our attention after that review had closed, and we added it as a labeled supplement rather
than folding it into the count of 16.

The six empirical papers earned their place by reporting completed or desired fertility under a
compulsory-schooling reform, and not by testing the mechanism. A later full-text review of what each
one actually measures classified none of the six as direct evidence on the economic value of
children. Cummins belongs with the hypothesis about falling teenage pregnancy. Fort and coauthors,
Chen and Guo, and Kan and Lee never say explicitly that they are testing the value of children. Lei
and coauthors belong to neither hypothesis. Keeping the six here preserves useful context and
establishes nothing about the value of children.

## 6. The six fertility studies, and whose schooling each one changes

The six studies that measure fertility all change the mother's own schooling, and none of them
changes what her children could have earned. The table names the law each one uses and the generation
that law bound.

| Study | Setting and law | Whose schooling the law changed | What it found about fertility |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fort, Schneeweis, and Winter-Ebmer | Compulsory-schooling reforms in England and Continental Europe | The mother's own | More schooling goes with lower completed fertility in England; on the Continent the reforms neither reduce biological children nor raise childlessness |
| Geruso and Royer | Britain, the 1972 school-leaving reform | The mother's own | Births fall at ages 16 and 17, effects disappear after the teenage years, and completed family size by age 45 is precisely unchanged |
| Cummins | England, the 1947 and 1972 reforms, using linked historical registration records | The mother's own | Effects on first-marriage fertility are effectively zero, and the analysis rules out a substantial negative causal effect |
| Kan and Lee | Taiwan, the 1968 extension of compulsory schooling, using exact birth dates and whole-population censuses | The mother's own | The reform raised women's education and did not change fertility, completed fertility included |
| Chen and Guo | China, reform-induced female education | The mother's own | One more year of education reduces total live births by 0.238 (SE 0.059), with no significant effect on childlessness |
| Lei and coauthors | China, exposure to the compulsory-schooling law | The mother's own | Each year of exposure lowers desired family size by 0.009 child; completed fertility is not measured |
| Shanan (added after the review) | United States, early twentieth century, compulsory schooling and child-labor restrictions together | The children's | Less child employment, higher school attendance, and lower completed fertility, all inside one study |

"SE" in that table means standard error, the number that says how precisely an estimate was measured.
A coefficient of 0.238 with a standard error of 0.059 is measured tightly enough that the true value
is very unlikely to be zero.

The studies that follow women to the end of their childbearing years find no effect on the number of
children they had. Geruso and Royer measure completed family size at age 45 and find it precisely
unchanged, and their own evidence about mechanism points to conceptions prevented while girls were
required to be in a classroom rather than to parents placing a lower value on a future child. Kan and
Lee's reform raised women's education and left fertility where it was. Cummins rules out a
substantial negative effect on first-marriage fertility.

The two Chinese studies do find effects, and they credit other mechanisms. Chen and Guo attribute the
0.238-birth reduction to delayed marriage and birth, to what women gave up by having a child, to who
marries whom, and to child mortality, and their channel analysis does not credit the work value of
children. Lei and coauthors locate their much smaller effect on desired family size in what women
gave up by having a child and in who wins the argument about contraception inside the household.

Taken together, the six studies neither test the chain in Section 1 nor deliver a consistent negative
effect on completed fertility. Geruso and Royer, Cummins, and Kan and Lee find no effect on the
number of children a woman ends up with. Fort and coauthors find an effect in England and none on the
Continent. Chen and Guo find an effect and credit other causes for it. Lei and coauthors measure what
women say they want rather than what they had. Anyone who reads this literature as support for the
value-of-children claim is reading estimates that were never about the value of children.

## 7. Shanan (2023): the one study that moves the right generation

Shanan (2023) studies a setting where the law fell on children's work, and it is the closest primary
evidence to the claim this chapter evaluates. American states in the early twentieth century passed
compulsory-schooling laws and child-labor restrictions together, at a time when children really did
work for pay. Shanan measures how much of that policy each woman was exposed to and then counts her
births.

The first link in the chain holds in Shanan's data. Children covered by the restrictions worked less
and attended school more (Table 2). Economists call that check a first stage, and it matters here for
an ordinary reason: before anyone can claim that a law changed births by changing children's work,
they have to show that the law changed children's work.

The last link holds too, in the same study. Exposure to any restriction at ages 20 to 30 predicts
0.301 fewer children over a woman's lifetime (SE 0.044; Table 4, column 2). A one-year increase in
the average age at which children left school predicts 0.108 fewer children (SE 0.024; Table 5,
column 2). Both estimates are several times their own standard errors, so neither is easily explained
by chance.

The same policy also lowered the chance of a birth in any given year. Exposure to any restriction at
ages 20 to 30 predicts a 0.85 percentage-point reduction in the annual probability of a birth (SE
0.11 percentage points; Table 7, column 2), measured against a baseline probability of 14.3%. A
one-year increase in the average school exit age predicts a 0.27 percentage-point reduction (SE 0.03;
Table 8, column 2).

Shanan stops short of the middle link, which is the part the hypothesis most needs. The study never
puts a figure on what a child's work was worth to their parents, and it never measures how much of
the fertility effect ran through that lost money rather than through something else. Its treatment
also bundles compulsory schooling together with child-labor restrictions, and the two arrived
together, so no estimate in the paper can separate the earnings a family lost from the extra
schooling a family gained. Parents who expect a better-educated child may want fewer children for
reasons that have nothing to do with lost wages.

## 8. Why we do not average these studies into one number

Averaging these studies into a single estimate would answer a question none of them asked. Combining
estimates into one number with one margin of error is the standard output of a meta-analysis, and it
works only when the numbers being combined measure the same thing.

These numbers do not measure the same thing. Shanan is the only study that verifies children's work
fell and then reports completed fertility. The six approved studies differ from Shanan and from one
another in country, in the reform they use, in the outcome they report, and in the quantity they
estimate: total live births in one paper, desired family size in another, first-marriage fertility in
a third. All six change the mother's own schooling, so averaging their coefficients would produce a
well-behaved number about women's education and no number at all about the economic value of
children.

## 9. How much of the FDT decline this could explain

Expanded exposure to schooling and child-work restrictions accounts for 8% of the decline in the
annual chance of a birth among 30-year-old native-born American women between 1900 and 1930. Shanan
gives the arithmetic in full. Exposure to the restrictions rose by 48 percentage points over those
three decades. Exposure lowers the annual chance of a birth by 0.85 percentage points. The observed
birth probability fell by five percentage points. Multiplying and dividing gives
`0.48 * 0.85 / 5 = 0.08`.

Our review calls a cause demographically significant for a fertility decline when it accounts for at
least 10% of that decline. The 8% figure falls below the bar. It supports a verdict of Partial, which
in this review means the cause is real and moves fertility in the claimed direction while accounting
for too small a share of the decline to clear the threshold.

Readers should treat the 8% as a fragile number for three reasons. The calculation covers one age,
30-year-old women, rather than the whole childbearing span. It is a back-of-envelope calculation,
meaning arithmetic run on three headline numbers rather than a statistical model fitted to the data.
And it comes with no uncertainty attached, because Shanan reports no interval around it.

We ran a sensitivity check on the third of those problems and it does not resolve the question.
Carrying through only the uncertainty in Shanan's own coefficient, while treating the 48-point rise
in exposure and the five-point fall in birth probability as fixed and known, gives a 95% interval of
6.1% to 10.2%. The point estimate stays below the threshold and the upper end of the interval crosses
it narrowly. Our review prefers a bootstrap interval, which re-draws the sample thousands of times
and lets every input vary at once, and no such interval exists here.

One further caveat sits underneath the arithmetic. A different reading of the same 8% is already in
print: Cigno and Rosati's evidence chapter describes combined schooling and child-work restrictions
as explaining about 10% of the observed American fertility decline. The underlying primary study is
Shanan, and Shanan's own back-of-envelope figure is 8%. A reader who takes the 10% from the review
chapter would place this hypothesis exactly at the threshold; a reader who goes to the primary study
finds it below.

## 10. Provisional GRADE rating

**FDT causal mechanism: Low.** GRADE is the scale this review uses to record how much confidence the
evidence supports, running from Very low through Low and Moderate to High. A renewed panel of three
independent raters scored this hypothesis and all three said Low. Shanan supplies one credible
historical quasi-experiment that connects the restrictions, children's work and attendance, and
completed fertility. The rating stops below Moderate for three reasons: no independent study
replicates the full chain, the states that adopted these laws may have differed from the states that
did not in ways the design cannot rule out, and the lost economic value of children is inferred from
the pattern rather than measured. This rating is provisional, and so is the verdict below.

## 11. Provisional verdict

| Fertility decline | Causal credibility | Demographic significance |
|---|---|---|
| FDT | Low | Partial; 8% decomposition is below the 10% threshold |

The evidence supports a historically real relationship running from policy to children's work to
completed fertility. How much of that relationship runs specifically through the earnings a family
lost, as against a parent's expectation of a better-educated child or the other things a schooling
law changes at the same time, remains uncertain on the evidence in hand. Both entries in the table
are provisional.

## 12. Evidence that would change the verdict

The decisive next study would replicate Shanan independently and then measure the middle link. Two
things would have to happen. A second researcher, using different data or a different country, would
have to find the same chain from restrictions to children's work to completed fertility. And someone
would have to trace the fertility effect through a direct measure of what children earned, or of
whatever else children contributed to a household, rather than inferring it from the fact that both
ends of the chain moved.

Any replication will also have to answer Greenwood, Seshadri, and Vandenbroucke. A study that
identifies the effect only from the dates on which legislatures acted cannot separate the law from
the shift in demand for skill that came before it, and on their history that shift emptied the
workshops before the law did.

A wider scaling exercise would also help. The 8% in Section 9 covers one age group in one country
over three decades. A decomposition of the total fertility rate, or of completed fertility, across a
whole population and reported with uncertainty attached, would tell us what this hypothesis is worth
at national scale. No paper we approved and no supplement remains unretrieved, so the constraint on
this chapter is new analysis rather than missing PDFs.
