## 1. Verdict

A pension reform moves fertility through two channels that carry opposite signs, and grading "pensions" as one treatment is what makes this literature look contradictory. We call the first the **retirement-asset channel**: children are one way to insure old age, so a credible non-child substitute lowers the retirement return to an additional birth. We call the second the **grandparent-time channel**: retirement and pension eligibility free an older adult's time for childcare, which lowers the cost of a birth to that adult's own children. The retirement-asset channel predicts lower fertility, the grandparent-time channel predicts higher fertility, and which one is live depends on the demographic regime. We carry both labels through the chapter and never pool estimates across them.

We find support for a narrow version of the retirement-asset argument. Where children still plausibly insure old age, a credible non-child substitute lowers fertility. Settings outside that condition do not line up: long-term-care insurance in China, social insurance in Bismarckian Prussia, and U.S. Social Security during the baby boom do not all show the same negative fertility response. What decides the response is the kind of old-age security that changes and the demographic setting in which it changes.

State pensions did not cause the First Demographic Transition, because most of them arrived after that decline had begun. The better-timed version of the retirement-asset argument runs through financial development. Banks and savings instruments gave households a non-child way to move resources into old age, and they spread early enough to matter.

The Second Demographic Transition poses a different question: whether pension policy still moves fertility once public pensions and private savings are already widespread. Rich low-fertility countries leave the retirement-asset channel little room, because the substitute for children is already in place. The stronger evidence for these settings supports the grandparent-time channel. Retirement and pension eligibility change how much childcare grandparents can supply, and that supply changes adult children's fertility: more grandparent availability raises fertility, and delayed retirement lowers it.

|---|---|---|---|

## 2. The Claim and the Two Channels

Parents want support in old age, and where children are the main way to obtain it, a child is partly an investment asset. Give parents another asset — a public pension, private insurance, or a usable financial market — and the retirement return to an additional child falls. Fertility should fall with it.

The grandparent-time channel starts from the same policy instrument and reaches the opposite prediction. Retirement changes how much time an older adult has, and a retired grandparent who supplies childcare lowers the cost of a child to that grandparent's adult children. Pension policy can therefore raise fertility in the same country where it lowers fertility through the retirement-asset channel. Keeping the two apart is this chapter's organizing discipline.

### 2.1 Children as an Old-Age Asset

The retirement-asset channel has two theoretical roots, and the older one treats children as part of an intergenerational transfer system rather than only as a consumption good. Neher (1971), Willis (1980), and Nugent (1985) are the central statements of that view; Caldwell (1976) and Cigno (1993) connect it to broader intergenerational-transfer theory. In this tradition the fertility decision turns on the expected old-age return to a child, counting support, care, and insurance against risk.

Caldwell's wealth-flow argument gives the demographic transition its framing because it ties fertility incentives to the direction of net transfers between generations. High fertility can be privately valuable when the net economic flow runs from children to parents. When transfers reverse, or when non-child assets replace children, the private demand for an additional birth should fall.

The comparison that identifies the channel is between families that gain a non-child source of old-age support and otherwise similar families that do not. Fertility should fall most where children are still a meaningful old-age asset, so the prediction is strongest where formal pensions, private savings, and insurance markets are incomplete, and weakest where public pensions and private savings are already mature.

### 2.2 Formal Pension Models

The second root is the formal economic fertility literature, which supplies the policy instrument the older tradition lacks. Becker (1960), Becker and Barro (1988), and Barro and Becker (1989) set the background framework in which parents choose child quantity against resources, preferences, and the cost of children. Ehrlich and Lui (1991), Nishimura and Zhang (1992), and Sinn (2004) then model how public pensions and private family transfers interact with fertility incentives, and Boldrin, De Nardi, and Jones (2015) tie those mechanisms to social-security systems more directly. A "pay-as-you-go" system in this literature is one in which current workers finance current retirees' benefits.

These models matter because they show why a public pension can move fertility even when households are forward-looking. A pay-as-you-go system replaces the implicit family contract under which children support parents, and it changes the enforcement problem: children need not repay parental investment directly once the state taxes the next generation to finance benefits. Some models therefore predict fertility decline after public pension expansion; others ask what policy correction is needed when a pension system leaves too little private incentive to have children.

The underlying intergenerational transfer does not disappear in any of these models. The transfer moves from a household contract, in which parents expect support from their own children, toward a state contract, in which workers finance retirees through taxes and benefits. Fertility falls because an additional child no longer delivers the same private retirement return to the parent.

### 2.3 What Counts as Evidence

The primary causal question is the effect of an exogenous increase in non-child old-age security on fertility, working through the retirement-asset channel, and that question excludes many papers that mention pensions. A pension study with savings, schooling, or elderly health as the outcome tests an important link in the mechanism without estimating the fertility effect. Where fertility causes pension take-up or private insurance purchase, the study is mechanism evidence rather than the primary effect. Studies in which pension eligibility changes grandparents' childcare time belong to the grandparent-time channel, which predicts that pension access can raise fertility rather than lower it.

That last exclusion does the real work. A pension can lower fertility by replacing children as old-age insurance, and the same pension can raise fertility by retiring grandparents into childcare, so the two effects cannot be pooled as if they measured one object. The theory justifies both channels. The quantitative synthesis keeps them in separate cells because they answer different causal questions.

### 2.4 What the Theory Predicts for PM, FDT, and SDT

Pre-modern fertility variation is where the retirement-asset channel is most plausible and least useful. If nearly every household lacks formal old-age security, reliance on children for old-age support explains why desired fertility was high better than it explains why fertility differed across populations. Some pre-modern variation could still come from differences in family systems, communal care, inheritance rules, and local expectations about who supports older adults. We therefore treat the channel as relevant to pre-modern fertility and as an insufficient explanation of pre-modern variation absent evidence on those institutional differences.

The First Demographic Transition is where the public-pension version is usually late and the broader version is not. Financial markets, savings, and insurance reduce the need to rely on children before modern pension systems reach broad coverage, which makes asset substitution the better-timed theoretical mechanism for the FDT.

The Second Demographic Transition leaves little room for the retirement-asset channel and a good deal for the grandparent-time channel. Public pensions and private savings are already widespread in rich countries by the time the SDT begins, so the live pension margin is the cost of children rather than the retirement value of children. Retirement policy changes grandparents' availability for childcare, and greater availability can raise fertility by making children less costly for adult children.

## 3. Sorting Studies by Causal Question

Pension-and-fertility papers become legible once they are sorted by the causal question they answer rather than by their topic.

| Evidence stream | What it measures | Role in the chapter |
|---|---|---|
| Children as old-age support | Non-child old-age security affects fertility by reducing the retirement value of children. | Direct evidence on whether pensions, insurance, or financial markets reduce fertility by replacing children as old-age support. |
| Mechanism evidence | Children affect private insurance, assets, or old-age support. | Evidence on whether children behave like old-age-security assets. It informs the mechanism but is not pooled with fertility effects. |
| Grandparental childcare | Pension or retirement policy changes childcare help and fertility. | Evidence on whether retirement policy raises fertility by increasing grandparents' childcare availability. |
| Indirect links | Pensions affect savings, schooling, migration, coresidence, health, or other links short of fertility. | Background evidence on the causal chain. It updates mechanism plausibility without estimating fertility effects. |

The binding filter is the causal question rather than topical relevance. A paper can be about pensions and fertility and still answer the wrong causal question for the retirement-asset channel. Our synthesis therefore separates primary fertility effects, mechanism validation, grandparent-time evidence, and indirect chain links.

## 4. Search and Inclusion

We began with a broad search for old-age security, pensions, and fertility, and then applied one inclusion rule: a paper enters the review only if it identifies one of the causal questions in Section 3. The rule does most of the screening work because pension papers often study outcomes other than fertility, or study fertility through a channel other than the retirement-asset channel.

The main synthesis uses studies that estimate fertility responses to non-child old-age security or to grandparental availability. We retain mechanism studies that test whether children behave like old-age-security assets, and we keep them out of the fertility-effect pool. We retain historical and aggregate studies for timing and external-validity evidence, and we carry their higher confounding risk into the risk-of-bias assessment rather than into the pooled estimates. Section 13 lists the operational search logs, extraction sheets, and reproducibility files.

For each quantitative study we take the paper's own preferred fertility estimate where the paper supplies one, and we keep the paper's original units and uncertainty measures where possible. We orient every estimate so that a negative value means lower fertility after an increase in non-child old-age security. The main analytic limit is treatment-scale comparability, since the studies do not all estimate the same policy contrast.

### 5.2 Mechanism Evidence

Ci (2024) belongs outside the fertility-effect synthesis because its outcome is private insurance adoption, private pension adoption, or private health insurance rather than fertility. The study asks whether children substitute for purchased old-age security, which is valuable mechanism evidence: if children reduce private insurance demand, the retirement-asset premise becomes more plausible. The primary causal question runs the other way.

Ruthbah (2022), from Bangladesh, appears to be a parallel mechanism paper. We treat it as a candidate mechanism addition rather than as part of the primary fertility-effect synthesis.

### 5.3 Evidence on the Grandparent-Time Channel

Three studies anchor the grandparent-time channel, and the SDT case rests on them: Eibich and Siedler (Germany), Ilciukas (Netherlands), and Akyol and Atalay (Australia). None of the three tests whether parents use children as pensions. Each tests whether pension or retirement policy changes grandparents' availability for childcare, and whether that availability then changes adult children's fertility.

The two channels predict fertility effects in opposite directions, which is why we never pool them. More non-child old-age security should reduce fertility where children are retirement assets. More grandparent availability should raise fertility where retired grandparents lower childcare costs for adult children. Expanding retirement eligibility can therefore raise fertility, while raising retirement ages or shrinking early pensions keeps grandparents at work, cuts childcare help, and can lower fertility. The grandparent-time channel, in which pension policy raises fertility by lowering childcare costs, matches the SDT era better than the retirement-asset channel does. Our extracted grandparent-time evidence covers Eibich and Siedler on parental retirement in Germany, Ilciukas on delayed maternal retirement in the Netherlands, and Akyol and Atalay on grandmothers' pension eligibility in Australia. All three sit in below-replacement, rich-country settings under the TFR classification rule, and after we orient every estimate to "more grandparent availability," all three imply that more grandparent availability raises fertility.

The scale check compares those availability-oriented effects with observed TFR changes in the same country and period. Ilciukas's Netherlands estimates and Akyol and Atalay's Australia estimates are large relative to the fertility declines observed in those windows. Eibich and Siedler's Germany estimates support the same direction, and Germany's TFR rises slightly over their 1984-2017 SOEP window, so no fertility-decline denominator exists for a comparable scale calculation. The exercise is a scale check rather than a decomposition share, and it shows that the grandparent-time channel is quantitatively nontrivial where the target window actually has falling TFR.

### 5.4 Indirect Chain Evidence

The indirect evidence covers pension effects on savings, children's schooling, and coresidence, and it answers whether the intermediate links in the mechanism exist. None of it tells us how much fertility changes when old-age security changes.

## 6. Quantitative Synthesis

Our synthesis keeps the estimates close to the way the papers report them, comparing effects only after preserving each paper's outcome wording, treatment contrast, uncertainty, and follow-up window. Unit conversions are mechanical: percentage-point effects are expressed as probabilities, and completed-fertility estimates stay in births per woman.

### 6.1 Pooled Summaries Within Outcome Family

We report two pooled summaries for the retirement-asset channel, pooled within outcome family rather than across all pension-related treatments. The birth probability summary pools five independent studies: more non-child old-age security is associated with a 0.006954 lower probability of birth, with a standard error of 0.002037 and a 95 percent confidence interval from -0.010946 to -0.002963. The completed fertility summary pools three independent studies: more non-child old-age security is associated with 0.067672 fewer births per woman, with a standard error of 0.026971 and a 95 percent confidence interval from -0.120536 to -0.014808.

Neither summary is one structural pension effect. The birth probability pool combines pension expansion, pension-value exposure, pension participation, pension cuts, and long-term-care insurance; the completed fertility pool combines pension-value exposure, pension cuts, and pension participation. Both numbers summarize direction and approximate magnitude within an outcome family, and interpretation still depends on treatment type, setting, and identification quality.

The stricter pooling rule still binds. A single coefficient for "the effect of pensions" would require studies to share the same mechanism, outcome, unit, treatment scale, and usable standard error across at least three independent studies, and the current evidence does not meet that standard.

The newly retrieved Ghana working paper is consistent with the developing-country pattern and stays out of these summaries. Zelu, Iranzo, and Pérez-Laborda compare private-sector women newly covered by Ghana's Act 766 reform with public-sector women who already received more generous pensions. Their controlled DiD estimate is a 6-percentage-point reduction in the probability of pregnancy in the previous year (standard error 3 percentage points; Table 2, p. 14). The controlled full-sample estimate for number of children is negative but not statistically precise (Table 7, p. 17). We hold both estimates as provisional because the paper is a working draft that has not completed the chapter's RA extraction and risk-of-bias review.

The two channels stay in separate cells throughout. The retirement-asset estimates answer whether non-child old-age security lowers fertility by replacing children as old-age insurance; the grandparent-time estimates answer whether grandparent availability raises fertility by lowering childcare costs. Mechanism-validation and indirect-chain evidence stay outside the fertility-effect synthesis altogether.

Every grandparent-time estimate is coded as the effect of greater grandparent availability. Eibich and Siedler's retirement estimates and Akyol and Atalay's pension eligibility estimates are left as reported, because both treatments increase grandparent availability, and we reverse Ilciukas, because the Dutch reform delayed maternal retirement and so reduced grandparent availability. We then compare the availability-coded effects with observed TFR declines in the same country and period. Ilciukas's Netherlands estimates and Akyol and Atalay's Australia estimates are large relative to the fertility declines observed in those study windows. Eibich and Siedler's Germany estimates are not scaled, because Germany's TFR does not decline over their SOEP study window.

We treat the remaining evidence narratively where the outcome, treatment scale, or uncertainty measure is not comparable enough for pooling. Historical aggregate outcomes and mechanism-only estimates remain useful, and they answer different questions from the pooled fertility estimates.

Treatment comparability is the limitation that survives every choice above. Binary pension expansions, continuous pension-value exposure, pension cuts, LTCI pilots, financial access, and broad social spending do not define one common treatment contrast.

## 7. Demographic Significance

We assess demographic significance by matching each study window to the replacement status of fertility in the relevant country or region. A setting whose first and last available in-window TFR observations both lie above 2.1 is coded FDT-relevant; a setting whose observations both lie below 2.1 is coded SDT-relevant; a window that crosses from above to below replacement is coded FDT|SDT. The rule yields Brazil as a crossing case, Namibia and the postwar United States as above-replacement cases, and Italy plus the China pension studies as below-replacement cases. Prussia, Imperial Germany, and the 1850 U.S. financial-development evidence remain historical cases without in-window TFR coverage. China stays difficult to interpret because its low fertility is policy-constrained.

Four verdicts follow from that classification. PM has insufficient direct evidence. FDT is partial: the primary evidence supports the retirement-asset channel in above-replacement or crossing settings, state pensions are often late for the Western FDT, and the pooled estimates do not supply one structural treatment coefficient. The retirement-asset channel is not significant or only contextual for the SDT, since the below-replacement evidence that exists comes from policy-constrained China and since mature rich-country pension systems leave little new variation on this margin. The grandparent-time channel is the live SDT channel: its evidence comes from rich, below-replacement settings, it consistently implies that greater grandparent availability raises fertility, and it is large in the Netherlands and Australia scale check.

### 7.1 Pre-Modern Fertility Variation

No direct pre-modern causal evidence exists. The channel almost certainly mattered as a background condition, since a world without public pensions, formal insurance, or deep financial markets leaves children as old-age support. A universal condition does not explain variation: if nearly everyone depends on children for old age, that dependence explains high fertility levels better than differences across populations.

The pre-modern verdict is therefore low. Mortality, marriage systems, and inheritance rules are more plausible explanations for pre-modern variation, and old-age security is better read as a baseline motive than as the main source of pre-modern differences.

### 7.2 First Demographic Transition

State pensions are poorly timed as the initiating cause of the FDT. The Western transition begins in the late nineteenth century, Bismarck's pension system dates to the late 1880s, and U.S. Social Security begins in 1935. Reforms of that vintage can deepen or redirect a decline already under way; they cannot explain why it began.

Financial development is the stronger FDT route. Households did not need a public pension to reduce the retirement value of children if the real mechanism is non-child old-age security; they needed credible saving and borrowing institutions. Basso, Bodenhorn, and Cuberes speak directly to that margin in the nineteenth-century United States, which makes financial development the better-timed old-age-security substitute for FDT purposes.

The FDT verdict is partial. The channel is plausible and empirically supported, and state pensions are too late and their early coverage too limited to be the main cause, so the best version of the FDT argument is broader asset substitution with financial markets doing more work than public pensions.

### 7.3 Second Demographic Transition

The SDT needs two judgments, one per channel.

The retirement-asset channel is weak as a rich-country SDT explanation. Rich countries already have mature pension systems by the time the SDT begins, and an already-established source of old-age support has little new variation left with which to explain a further fertility decline. The predicted direction is also uncomfortable: retrenchment in pension generosity or later eligibility should raise fertility through this channel, because children become more valuable as old-age support, and fertility fell.

The grandparent-time channel changes that conclusion. Later retirement and tighter pension eligibility can keep grandparents in paid work, reduce childcare help for adult children, and so lower fertility. Our extracted grandparent-time studies come from rich, below-replacement settings and agree in the availability-oriented direction. Ilciukas's Netherlands estimates and Akyol and Atalay's Australia estimates are large relative to observed TFR declines in their study windows. Eibich and Siedler's Germany evidence supports the same direction, and Germany's TFR rises slightly over their study window, so we do not convert the Germany estimates into shares of a fertility decline.

China does not settle the SDT question. The Chinese pension and long-term-care insurance studies are useful evidence that replacing children with non-child old-age security can still move fertility where old-age-security policy is newly expanding. China reached below replacement under a policy-constrained fertility regime, which makes it weak evidence for the SDT process of postponement, individualization, and low desired family size.

## 8. External Validity and Transportability

The studies are not all about the same social world, and that is the main transportability problem. Rural Brazil, Namibia, and rural China differ sharply from Imperial Germany, nineteenth-century U.S. counties, and late twentieth-century Italy, and the differences that matter are family-transfer norms, pension coverage, and baseline fertility.

Those differences determine whether a channel has room to operate. The retirement-asset channel should matter most where children remain a credible retirement asset and non-child old-age security is newly introduced or newly expanded, and least where public pensions and private savings are already universal. The grandparent-time channel should be strongest where grandparents are available, formal childcare is costly, and retirement policy sharply changes older adults' time.

| Setting group | Strongest channel | Transportability reading |
|---|---|---|
| Rural or developing settings with family old-age support | Replacing children with non-child old-age security | High mechanism relevance, but limited direct transport to rich-country SDT. |
| Historical Western FDT settings | Financial-market or early social-insurance substitution | Relevant to FDT only if timing and coverage precede or overlap the decline. |
| Rich below-replacement settings | Grandparental childcare | Most relevant to SDT, but effect size must be checked against the scale of the fertility decline. |
| Policy-constrained low-fertility settings such as China | New policy expansion replacing children as old-age support | Useful mechanism evidence, weak as SDT evidence. |

A study can be causally credible in its own setting and still weak evidence for a target transition, so we judge internal validity and external validity separately.

## 9. Risk of Bias and Identification Quality

We assess risk of bias across confounding, selection, and identification credibility, and the distinction that carries the most weight is between policy-generated micro variation, which is often credible without being randomized, and historical aggregate variation, which is valuable for timing and more exposed to ecological confounding.

The strongest direct evidence comes from quasi-experimental pension expansions or eligibility changes with credible comparison groups, of which Rossi and Godard, Danzer and Zyska, and the grandparent-time studies are the clearest examples. We rate these moderate rather than low risk, because treatment exposure is policy-generated rather than individually randomized and because missing-data documentation is not always fully audited. Zelu, Iranzo, and Pérez-Laborda also use policy-generated DiD variation, and their Ghana working paper remains ungraded pending RA review; their public/private-sector comparison requires particular scrutiny because the two groups differ substantially in employment composition.

The historical evidence is important and more exposed to confounding, measurement, and ecological inference concerns. We therefore treat Guinnane and Streb, Fenge and Scheubel, and Galofre-Vila as serious risk in the overall risk-of-bias field even where they are valuable for timing and mechanism. The China evidence is useful and needs careful treatment, because fertility policy and pension policy overlap in time and because SDT transportability is weak. Mechanism studies such as Ci are internally relevant and do not estimate the fertility outcome.

## 11. Interpretation for Economics Readers

The evidence supports a substitution model in which the substitute changes across time. Children, kin, land, and family transfers supplied old-age security in pre-modern settings. Formal financial markets may have mattered more than state pensions during the FDT, because they arrived early enough to affect the decline. The relevant margin shifts again in the SDT: rich countries no longer need children as old-age insurance in the same way, many families still rely on grandparents for childcare, and retirement policy moves that availability.

This chapter should not ask whether pensions reduce fertility as a generic question, because the answerable question is which pension channel operates in which demographic regime. The retirement-asset channel lowers fertility where old-age support is scarce and children are useful assets. The grandparent-time channel raises fertility where retired grandparents supply time to adult children. A policy that expands pensions can activate both channels at once, which makes the net effect an empirical question.

## 12. Limitations and a Research Agenda

The limitation common to the whole evidence base is that the studies do not estimate one common treatment contrast. Binary pension expansions, pension-wealth cuts, and long-term-care insurance pilots all bear on old-age security and are not the same treatment, which is why we report pooled summaries within each outcome family rather than a single statistically pooled pension coefficient.

Two extensions would strengthen the chapter. Additional same-scale estimates would make treatment-specific pooling possible within a narrow outcome family. Organizing mechanism-only studies such as Ruthbah and the indirect chain-link evidence into an appendix would show how children, savings, insurance, and old-age support interact outside the primary fertility causal question.
