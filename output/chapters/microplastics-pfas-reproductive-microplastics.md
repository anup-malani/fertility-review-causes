# Microplastics and Nanoplastics in Reproductive Tissue

**Category:** Biological
**Primary mechanism:** Plastic particles small enough to cross biological barriers reach the ovary, testis and placenta, where they are proposed to damage the cells that produce eggs and sperm.

## 1. The claim

This chapter explores the effect of microplastic exposure on fertility.

### 1.1 In plain terms

In plain terms: tiny fragments of plastic are now found inside people — in blood, in the placenta, in testicles, and in the fluid surrounding a developing egg. Almost everyone has them, and there are more of them every year as more plastic is made. The claim is that these fragments damage the parts of the body that make eggs and sperm, so fewer children are conceived — and that this has been quietly reducing births since the 1960s, with no one choosing anything.

The first part is now beyond doubt. The particles really are there, measured directly rather than guessed at from how much plastic is in the sea.

The second part is the whole question, and it does not follow from the first. Finding something inside a body tells you the body is exposed. It tells you nothing about whether the thing does any harm. Plenty of substances are found in human tissue and do nothing at all.

And there is a specific reason to be careful here. Much of the evidence comes from fertility clinics — because that is where you can get a sample of the fluid around an egg, since the woman is already having eggs collected. So the people being measured are people already having trouble conceiving, before anyone measures anything.

### 1.2 The claim precisely

The parameter this chapter estimates is the change in the probability of conception, and thereby in completed fertility in births per woman, caused by an increase in internal microplastic burden, signed so that a negative value means fewer births.

The chapter is scoped to the **SDT** (the Second Demographic Transition, roughly 1965 onward). There is no **PM** (pre-modern) cell — mass plastic production began in the 1950s — and no **FDT** (First Demographic Transition) cell, since production before 1965 was negligible against the reproductive-age population.

**The claim is a two-step chain and the steps are of completely different evidential status:**

> particles reach reproductive tissue → particles impair the production of eggs and sperm → fewer conceptions

**Step one is established and this review treats it as such.** Ragusa and colleagues (2020) found microplastics in human placenta; Leslie and colleagues (2022) found particles in blood, which shows they travel through the body rather than staying in the gut; Zhao and colleagues (2023) found them in testis and semen; a 2025 study found them in ovarian follicular fluid.

**Step two is the claim, and this chapter's finding is that no human study has estimated it.**

**Detection is not effect, and the flagship detection study says so itself.** Montano and colleagues (2025), reporting the first microplastics found in human follicular fluid, measured four fertility endpoints in their 18 women and found **no association with any of them**: anti-Müllerian hormone, fertilisation outcomes, miscarriage, or live birth. **This review therefore separates detection from effect and gives detection no weight in the causal rating.**

**This is a behavioural parameter only in the sense that fertility is the outcome; the mechanism is entirely biological, with no identity arm.**

**Margin.** Predicted on the extensive margin of conception per cycle, and — if the mechanism operates on gamete production — potentially on the capacity to conceive at all.

**What would make the hypothesis wrong.** It is wrong if internal burden does not predict conception in a population not selected on subfecundity. Notably, **the one study that looked for fertility endpoints alongside detection found none.**

## 2. Theoretical mechanism

In the reader's own vocabulary: this is a pure supply-side shock to the biological capacity to conceive, with no price, no preference and no choice anywhere in it. If it operates, it lowers realised fertility across the whole population simultaneously and progressively, which is a distinctive prediction — most mechanisms in this review act on subgroups or through decisions.

**Laboratory work supplies a candidate route:** particles trigger oxidative stress, damage the blood-testis barrier protecting developing sperm, and disturb the granulosa cells supporting a growing egg.

**This review screened 174 animal studies and 46 cell-culture studies making that case, and counts none of it as human evidence.** Almost all of it uses mice, rats, fish, oysters or cell lines, and **dose, particle type and species all differ from human exposure in ways nobody has calibrated.** A mouse dosed with polystyrene beads at concentrations orders of magnitude above human burden establishes that the mechanism *can* operate, not that it *does*.

**The gap between the two steps is the whole difficulty**, and it is worth naming why it persists: establishing presence is publishable, technically demanding and complete in itself, while estimating an effect requires a cohort, a conception outcome, and a comparison group. The field has done the first repeatedly and the second not at all.

## 3. Search strategy

**This chapter and the PFAS chapter come from one search, run once over both chemical families**, because they share every boundary rule and separating them would have doubled the screening cost. Records split at extraction on which compound each study measured.

| Wall | Rule | Enforceable at title/abstract? |
|---|---|---|
| **W1 Species** | Human subjects only. Checked on **every** record rather than inferred. | Partially — and it must be checked, because studies of marine and rodent reproduction occupy the exact vocabulary this hypothesis uses. **The most-cited papers matching "microplastics" with "fecundity" study copepods and oysters.** |
| **W2 Exposure locus** | Internal human exposure, not environmental occurrence. 28,000 records concern where particles are found in soil and water. | Yes |

**One rule was added for this half specifically, and it is the reason the chapter's central claim can be made at all.** Because we expected to find no human effect estimates, **we screened every record carrying both a plastic term and a fertility term at any rank, not only those inside the budget.** An empty cell and an unread cell produce identical evidence and mean opposite things, and we wanted to be able to tell them apart.

## 5. The ideal design

Written before the literature was read, so §6 can be measured against a fixed yardstick.

### 5.1 The ideal estimand

The change in the probability of conception within a defined attempt window, caused by an increase in internal microplastic burden, **among people not selected on fertility status**, with burden measured under **reported procedural blanks**.

Both clauses are the specification and both fail in every existing study. *Not selected on fertility status* excludes the clinic samples that supply most of this literature. *Reported procedural blanks* is not a quality refinement but a precondition for the exposure meaning anything (§6.2).

### 5.2 The design that would identify it

**Source of variation.** Burden differences arising from **behaviour rather than biology** — how much packaged food a person eats, how often they use plastic tableware, their water source, their occupation. **Behaviour can differ between people for reasons unrelated to their fertility, which is exactly what an estimate needs**, and a blood draw alone cannot supply it.

**Comparison group.** People in the same population with lower burden on the same behavioural contrast, matched on age, income and known fertility determinants.

**Identifying assumption.** The behavioural contrast is unrelated to fertility except through burden. Falsifiable: balance on known fertility determinants; a placebo on a behaviour that raises a *different* exposure with no reproductive route.

**Estimating equation.** A prospective preconception cohort — burden measured at enrolment, time-to-pregnancy as the outcome — with procedural blanks reported and the exposure metric pre-specified.

**Data required.** A general-population preconception cohort with banked biospecimens. **No part of this design is difficult or expensive by the standards of environmental epidemiology.**

**Sample size.** With near-universal exposure and contrast only in degree, this needs thousands rather than the tens or low hundreds in the current literature.

**What the ideal design excludes.** Animal and cell-culture work, at any dose. Detection studies without a fertility endpoint. IVF treatment outcomes, which are A.17's. And clinic-recruited samples, which are **selected on the outcome**.

### 5.3 Distance from the ideal

| Study | Human? | Outcome a conception or birth? | Sample not selected on fertility? | Procedural blanks reported? | Distance |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Qu 2025** (plastic tableware) | Yes | **No** — sperm motion | Unclear | Not established | **Closest: the only behavioural exposure contrast in the literature** |
| Zhang 2024 | Yes | No — sperm concentration | Multi-site, China | Not established | Mid |
| Guo 2025 | Yes | No — semen quality | Not established | Not established | Mid |
| Kong 2025 | Yes | **No — IVF fertilisation rate** | **No — IVF patients** | Not established | **Routes to A.17** |
| Ferraz 2026 | Yes | No — retrieved oocytes | **No — assisted reproduction** | Not established | Far; preprint |
| Montano 2025 | Yes | **Yes — four endpoints** | No — 18 clinic women | n/a | **Detection study; found no association** |
| The 174 animal + 46 cell studies | **No** | n/a | n/a | n/a | Excluded by W1 |

**No study implements the ideal design, and none is close: not one estimates microplastic exposure against a fertility quantity in humans.** The five studies reporting original human estimates all measure **inputs** to fertility — sperm concentration, motility, oocyte counts — rather than fertility itself.

**The design that would work already exists in miniature and nobody has scaled it.** Qu and colleagues tied microplastic burden to how often participants used plastic tableware, **the only exposure contrast in this literature that comes from behaviour rather than from a blood draw.** A larger study on that design, with a conception outcome, would produce the first interpretable number here.

The five microplastics studies reporting original human estimates:

| Study | Design | Sample frame | Outcome | Result | Risk of bias |

| Guo 2025 | Cross-sectional | Not established from text | Semen quality, immotile sperm | p = 0.056 and 0.080, both null | Small |
| Qu 2025 | Cross-sectional | Not established from text | Sperm motion parameters | Numeric values not recoverable | Exposure self-reported |
| Kong 2025 | Cross-sectional | IVF patients | Fertilisation rate | p = 0.0003 | Treatment outcome; routes to A.17 |
| Ferraz 2026 | Cross-sectional | Assisted reproduction | Retrieved oocytes | p = 0.008, negative | Selected on subfecundity; preprint |

Two cautions attach to this table. Ferraz and colleagues appear as a Research Square preprint and have not passed peer review. **Several point estimates could not be recovered because decimal points split when the documents were converted to text, and those were recorded as missing rather than reconstructed** — a wrongly transcribed number would do more damage here than an absent one.

### 6.1 The naive estimator

**What is the naive estimator?** Compare microplastic burden between people with and without fertility problems.

**It is selected on the outcome by construction, and the selection is not incidental but structural.** Follicular fluid is obtainable because a woman is undergoing egg retrieval, so **the only convenient source of the exposure measurement is a population defined by difficulty conceiving.** **Three of the five samples above come from fertility clinics.**

The direction of that bias is not straightforwardly signed, which is worse than a signed bias: clinic populations differ from the general population on age, on income, on prior reproductive history and on the reason they are there, and the net effect on a burden–fertility association is unknown.

**How many studies escape it?** None fully. Qu's behavioural exposure contrast is the only design element in the literature that points at an escape.

### 6.2 Contamination control is the unresolved measurement question

**Plastic labware and airborne synthetic fibres deposit particles into samples**, so a detection study without procedural blanks reports an unknown mixture of biology and laboratory. **We could not establish from the retrieved text whether any of the studies above ran them.**

This is not a fastidious objection. It bears directly on whether two studies reporting different burdens differ in their populations or in their laboratories — and §11 argues measurement standards need settling **before** effect estimates accumulate, because a literature built on incomparable exposure metrics cannot be repaired retrospectively.

### 6.3 The transmission ledger

| Stage | Question | Sign |
|---|---|---|
| Particles in environment → particles in tissue | **Established** | Holds |
| Particles in tissue → measured burden | Contamination control unresolved | **Unknown** |
| Burden → gamete impairment | Five studies, all on inputs, p-values at the boundary | **Marginal at best** |
| Gamete impairment → fewer conceptions | **Never measured in humans** | **Absent** |
| Fewer conceptions → the phenomenon | Requires the step above | **Absent** |

The last two rows are empty, and they are the two that bear on this review's question.

## 7. Quantitative synthesis

### 7.1 The answer in plain terms

There is nothing to add up. No study has asked whether people carrying more plastic go on to have fewer children.

What has been measured is sperm and eggs, not babies — and mostly in people who were already at a fertility clinic. Of the five results, four sit right on the line where a finding stops being distinguishable from chance. The one clearly strong result is about how well an IVF procedure worked, which is a different question.

The one study that did look for the thing that matters — whether the women with more plastic in them went on to conceive, miscarry, or give birth — found no connection with any of it.

### 7.2 The estimate

**None is possible.** No study estimates microplastic exposure against a fertility quantity in humans, so **there is nothing to pool**. The five studies measuring inputs share no outcome, no sample frame and no exposure metric.

Three features of that evidence deserve statement, because they bear on how a future estimate should be read.

**The p-values cluster at the boundary of conventional significance:** 0.041, 0.056, 0.080, 0.083, 0.091. That pattern in a small literature is what one expects when effects are near zero and reporting is selective, though with five studies it cannot be distinguished from chance.

**The single strong result attaches to the wrong outcome.** Kong's p = 0.0003 is a fertilisation rate during IVF, which measures how a treatment performed rather than whether a couple conceives, and belongs to A.17.

**And the samples are selected on subfecundity**, as §6.1 sets out.

## 8. Demographic significance

The phenomenon to be explained is measured in whole children — the SDT is a fall of roughly one and a half births per woman; this mechanism offers **no estimate of any size in any unit**.

**Not computable, and the reason is the finding.**

A demographic contribution is the change in exposure multiplied by the effect of exposure on fertility. **The first term is real and moves in the direction the hypothesis needs**: plastic production has risen throughout the period, human internal exposure with it, and the detection literature now measures particles in blood, placenta, testis and follicular fluid. **The second term does not exist. No study has estimated it.**

**Multiplying a well-measured rising exposure by an unestimated effect produces a number with no content.** Reporting the calculation as not computable, and saying why, tells a reader more than a decomposition built on a placeholder would.

**The endogeneity check** is clean: plastic production is not caused by the fertility decline, so the feedback problem afflicting most chapters does not arise.

**This differs from the PFAS verdict in kind, and the difference matters.** PFAS fails because evidence exists and points the wrong way. **Microplastics fails because the evidence does not exist.** The first conclusion is close to settled for the legacy compounds; the second could be overturned by one well-designed study, **and the reader should hold it that loosely.**

### 8.1 Pre-modern fertility variation

For pre-modern variation, the verdict is NOT ASSESSED, because the phenomenon is out of scope for this hypothesis in the registry: mass plastic production began in the 1950s.

### 8.2 First Demographic Transition

For the First Demographic Transition, the verdict is NOT ASSESSED, on the same ground: production before 1965 was negligible against the reproductive-age population.

### 8.3 Second Demographic Transition

For the Second Demographic Transition, the verdict is NOT ASSESSED, because the in-scope cell is empty of the quantity a share requires — no human study estimates the effect of microplastic exposure on a fertility outcome, so no share exists to compute.

**This is an empty cell rather than a small effect, and it must not be softened into NEGLIGIBLE.** NEGLIGIBLE would assert that a share was computed and came out under 5%. Nothing was computed, because the second multiplicand does not exist.

**The SDT cell is rated VERY LOW rather than No evidence, deliberately.** Evidence exists and points somewhere: it shows the particles are present, it shows effects in animals, and it shows marginal associations with sperm parameters in humans. **None of it bears on the quantity the claim asserts** — but a body of evidence exists to be rated, which is what distinguishes VERY LOW from No evidence.

**The absence is a finding rather than a gap in this search.** The screen read 920 records, and the completeness rule guaranteed that every record naming both a plastic and a fertility outcome was read regardless of rank. **The three-rater panel required by `PROTOCOL.md` §5.11 has not run.**

## 10. Verdict

The particles are there. Whether they do anything to fertility is unknown, and it is unknown because nobody has looked in a way that could tell.

**The one number to carry away: zero.** That is the number of studies estimating microplastic exposure against a human fertility quantity, and it is measured rather than assumed — the search read every record naming both a plastic and a fertility outcome, at any rank.

Three qualifications belong inside this verdict.

**Detection has been established repeatedly and is not evidence of harm.** The flagship follicular-fluid study measured four fertility endpoints in its 18 women and found no association with any of them.

**What human evidence exists measures inputs, in the wrong population.** Sperm parameters and oocyte counts rather than conceptions, with three of five samples drawn from fertility clinics, so **the population is selected on difficulty conceiving before any measurement happens.** The one clearly significant result is an IVF fertilisation rate, which belongs to A.17.

**This verdict is loosely held, and deliberately so.** It differs in kind from the PFAS verdict in the companion chapter: PFAS fails because evidence exists and points the wrong way; microplastics fails because the evidence does not exist. **One well-designed study could overturn this.**

**What would change it:** one cohort. Particle exposure measured in a sample drawn **outside** a fertility clinic, an outcome that counts conceptions or births rather than sperm or oocytes, adequate power, and reported procedural blanks. **No part of that design is difficult or expensive by the standards of environmental epidemiology. Nobody has run it.**

## 11. Open questions

**PI calls required.**

1. **Five calls are open on the shared B.6 scope**, including whether the microplastics and PFAS halves should be separate registry entries rather than one. They fail for opposite reasons and their verdicts are not commensurable, which is an argument for splitting.
2. **Three-rater GRADE** has not run.
3. **Should IVF-outcome studies be routed to A.17 uniformly?** Kong's is the only clearly significant result in this chapter and it is a treatment outcome.

**Evidence and measurement priorities.**

4. **The one cohort described in §5.2 and §10.** This is the entire chapter's bottleneck.
5. **Scale the tableware design.** Qu's behavioural exposure contrast is the only one in the literature that could support an estimate, and it is currently attached to a sperm-motion outcome.
6. **Detection studies should report fertility endpoints when they have them.** Montano measured four and found nothing, and reporting that null is more useful than another paper establishing presence. **The field has established presence.**
7. **Measurement standards need settling before effect estimates accumulate.** Studies count particles by different methods with different size thresholds; until a shared protocol exists, two studies reporting different burdens may differ in their laboratories rather than their populations. **This cannot be repaired retrospectively**, which is why it is urgent now rather than later.

**Studies that do not exist and should.** The design in §5.2, in full.
