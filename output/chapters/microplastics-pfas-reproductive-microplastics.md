# Chapter: Microplastics and Nanoplastics in Reproductive Tissue

**Category:** Biological
**Primary mechanism:** Plastic particles small enough to cross biological barriers reach the ovary, testis and placenta, where they are proposed to damage the cells that produce eggs and sperm.
**Cross-references:** Companion chapter on PFAS, which shares this hypothesis entry (HYPOTHESES-v5.md §B.6) and must be read with it; B.2 (legacy endocrine disruptors); A.17 (assisted reproduction); A.16 (paternal age and sperm quality).

## 1. The claim

Microplastic and nanoplastic particles now appear inside the human reproductive system, measured in follicular fluid, semen, testis and placenta rather than inferred from environmental levels. Exposure is close to universal and rising with plastic production. If the particles impair how ovaries and testes work, they lower realised fertility across the whole population, and the fertility decline after 1965 has a biological component that grows over time.

## 2. Theoretical mechanism

Two steps carry the argument, and the first is now well documented. Ragusa and colleagues (2020) found microplastics in human placenta. Leslie and colleagues (2022) found plastic particles in blood, which shows the particles travel through the body rather than staying in the gut. Zhao and colleagues (2023) found them in testis and semen. A 2025 study found them in ovarian follicular fluid. The particles reach the tissues where eggs and sperm develop, and this review treats that as established.

The second step proposes that presence causes harm. Laboratory work supplies a candidate route: particles trigger oxidative stress, damage the barrier that protects developing sperm, and disturb the granulosa cells that support a growing egg. This review screened 174 studies making that case in animals and 46 in cultured cells. Almost all of it uses mice, rats, fish, oysters or cell lines, and this review counts none of it as human evidence, because dose, particle type and species all differ from human exposure in ways nobody has calibrated.

The gap between the two steps is the whole difficulty. Finding a substance in a tissue establishes that the tissue is exposed. It does not establish that the substance does anything, and the flagship detection study says so itself. Montano and colleagues (2025), reporting the first microplastics found in human follicular fluid, measured four fertility endpoints in their 18 women and found no association with any of them: anti-Müllerian hormone, fertilisation outcomes, miscarriage, or live birth. This review therefore separates detection from effect and gives detection no weight in the causal rating.

## 3. Search strategy

This chapter and the PFAS chapter come from one search, run once over both chemical families, because they share every boundary rule and separating them would have doubled the screening cost. Records split at the extraction stage on which compound each study measured. Section 3 of the PFAS chapter describes the method; the counts below are shared.

Two boundary rules did most of the work here. Species had to be checked on every record rather than inferred, because studies of marine and rodent reproduction occupy the exact vocabulary this hypothesis uses, and the most-cited papers matching "microplastics" with "fecundity" study copepods and oysters. Environmental occurrence had to be separated from human exposure, since 28,000 records concern where particles are found in soil and water.

We added one rule for this half specifically. Because we expected to find no human effect estimates, we screened every record carrying both a plastic term and a fertility term at any rank, not only those inside the budget. An empty cell and an unread cell produce identical evidence and mean opposite things, and we wanted to be able to tell them apart.

## 5. Included studies

The screen routed 10 microplastics records into a cell for studies estimating a fertility quantity. We retrieved five of them. All five are reviews or opinion pieces, carrying titles such as *Microplastics: A Threat for Male Fertility* and *Microplastics May Be a Significant Cause of Male Infertility*. None reports an original estimate.

The five microplastics studies that do report original human estimates all measure inputs to fertility rather than fertility itself.

| Study | Design | Sample frame | Outcome | Result | Risk of bias |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Zhang 2024 | Cross-sectional | Multi-site, China | Sperm concentration | p = 0.041; other endpoints p = 0.083, 0.091 | Contamination control unresolved |
| Guo 2025 | Cross-sectional | Not established from text | Semen quality, immotile sperm | p = 0.056 and 0.080, both null | Small |

| Kong 2025 | Cross-sectional | IVF patients | Fertilisation rate | p = 0.0003 | Treatment outcome, routes to A.17 |
| Ferraz 2026 | Cross-sectional | Assisted reproduction | Retrieved oocytes | p = 0.008, negative | Selected on subfecundity; preprint |

Two cautions attach to this table. Ferraz and colleagues appear as a Research Square preprint and have not passed peer review. Several point estimates could not be recovered because decimal points split when we converted the documents to text, and we recorded those as missing rather than reconstructing them, since a wrongly transcribed number would do more damage here than an absent one.

## 6. Quantitative synthesis

None is possible. No study estimates microplastic exposure against a fertility quantity in humans, so there is nothing to pool. The five studies that measure inputs share no outcome, no sample frame and no exposure metric.

Three features of that evidence deserve statement, because they bear on how a future estimate should be read. The p-values cluster at the boundary of conventional significance: 0.041, 0.056, 0.080, 0.083, 0.091. The single strong result, Kong's p = 0.0003, attaches to fertilisation rate during IVF, which measures how a treatment performed rather than whether a couple conceives, and belongs to A.17. Three of the five samples come from fertility clinics, where follicular fluid is obtainable because women are undergoing egg retrieval, so the sample is selected on difficulty conceiving before any measurement happens.

Contamination control is the unresolved measurement question. Plastic labware and airborne synthetic fibres deposit particles into samples, so a detection study without procedural blanks reports an unknown mixture of biology and laboratory. We could not establish from the retrieved text whether the studies above ran them.

## 7. Demographic significance

### 7.1 Pre-modern
No cell. Mass plastic production began in the 1950s.

### 7.2 First Demographic Transition
No cell. Production before 1965 was negligible against the reproductive-age population.

### 7.3 Second Demographic Transition

Not computable, and the reason is the finding.

A demographic contribution is the change in exposure multiplied by the effect of exposure on fertility. The first term is real and moves in the direction the hypothesis needs: plastic production has risen throughout the period, human internal exposure with it, and the detection literature now measures particles in blood, placenta, testis and follicular fluid. The second term does not exist. No study has estimated it.

Multiplying a well-measured rising exposure by an unestimated effect produces a number with no content. We report the calculation as not computable and state why, which tells a reader more than a decomposition built on a placeholder would.

**Slope sufficiency: not assessable.** No decomposition share and no R² are reported, for the same reason.

This differs from the PFAS verdict in kind, and the difference matters. PFAS fails because evidence exists and points the wrong way. Microplastics fails because the evidence does not exist. The first conclusion is close to settled for the legacy compounds. The second could be overturned by one well-designed study, and the reader should hold it that loosely.

## 8. GRADE rating

**Very low.** PROTOCOL §4.1 assigns this level to correlational evidence with a speculative mechanism. The evidence here is weaker than that description usually implies, since no correlational evidence bears on a fertility quantity at all, only on inputs to fertility, drawn mostly from fertility clinics.

We rate the claim Very low rather than recording insufficient data because evidence does exist and does point somewhere. It shows the particles are present, it shows effects in animals, and it shows marginal associations with sperm parameters in humans. None of it bears on the quantity the claim asserts.

The absence is a finding rather than a gap in this search. The screen read 920 records, and the completeness rule guaranteed that every record naming both a plastic and a fertility outcome was read regardless of its rank. Full reasoning is in `literature/search-logs/microplastics-pfas-reproductive-grade.md`. The three-rater panel required by PROTOCOL §5.11 has not run.

## 10. Open questions and recommended studies

**One cohort would change this rating.** It needs particle exposure measured in a sample drawn outside a fertility clinic, an outcome that counts conceptions or births rather than sperm or oocytes, adequate power, and reported procedural blanks. No part of that design is difficult or expensive by the standards of environmental epidemiology. Nobody has run it.

**The tableware study points at the design that would work.** Qu and colleagues (2025) tied microplastic burden to how often participants used plastic tableware, which is the only exposure contrast in this literature that comes from behaviour rather than from a blood draw. Behaviour can differ between people for reasons unrelated to their fertility, which is what an estimate needs. A larger study on that design, with a conception outcome, would produce the first interpretable number here.

**Detection studies should report fertility endpoints when they have them.** Montano and colleagues measured four and found nothing, and reporting that null is more useful than another paper establishing presence. The field has established presence.

**Measurement standards need settling before effect estimates accumulate.** Studies count particles by different methods with different size thresholds, and until a shared protocol exists, two studies reporting different burdens may differ in their laboratories rather than their populations.
