**Parent hypothesis:** Tempo Effects and Birth Postponement  
**Placement:** Temple/tempo effect, first postponement mechanism  
**Category:** Demographic  
**Primary mechanism:** Compulsory schooling changes a prospective mother's own education and keeps
her enrolled during adolescence, reducing teenage pregnancy or births and postponing first birth.  
**Target phenomena:** FDT and SDT  
**Draft status:** Complete nested-driver draft; protocol stage-13 readability review deferred to
TICK-039 at Alexandra's request. This file is the PI voice rewrite of that draft, prepared for
side-by-side comparison; findings, estimates, and verdicts are unchanged.

## 1. The claim

Compulsory schooling postpones motherhood by extending the prospective mother's own education.
Raising the leaving age can reduce teenage pregnancy or birth while the mandate is binding, delay
marriage and sexual activity, or create longer-run changes in knowledge, partners, and employment
opportunities. Each of these channels governs when women have children, and none of them implies
that completed family size falls by the same amount.

The claim runs through the mother, which is what separates it from the child-economic-value
hypothesis. That hypothesis asks whether parents have fewer children because schooling prevents
those future children from working. The present stream asks instead what happens to a girl or young
woman whose own education is extended.

## 2. Theoretical mechanism

Four channels connect compulsory schooling to birth timing, and they differ in whether they operate
inside or outside what we call the covered-age window — the ages a reform newly makes compulsory.
The distinction organizes the rest of the chapter. A channel confined to the window can only move
births in time; a channel that survives the window need not stop at timing.

First, **incapacitation** operates while the mandate is binding. Attendance changes time use,
supervision, peers, and opportunities for pregnancy, and the most direct test asks whether
pregnancies fall during school terms but not during holidays.

Second, schooling can postpone **marriage or stable partnership formation**. Where marriage is the
usual gateway to childbearing, later marriage mechanically delays first birth.

Third, additional education can change **human capital, knowledge, and preferences**. Better
contraceptive knowledge, different partners, greater bargaining power, and higher labor-market
returns can each extend the effect beyond the compulsory ages.

Fourth, an early decline can be followed by **catch-up**. If births prevented at ages 16--18 occur
at ages 19--21, period fertility falls temporarily while completed cohort fertility remains nearly
unchanged. Catch-up is central to the Temple/tempo interpretation: a temporary shortage of births
can depress period TFR without a commensurate change in lifetime births.

## 3. Search strategy and routing

The evidence comes from the shared compulsory-education GACS search rather than from a
hypothesis-specific database query. Seven independently sourced anchors generated a
forward/backward citation frame of 1,255 DOI/title-deduplicated records. A blinded title/abstract
screen classified 233 records Relevant, 128 Uncertain, and 894 Not relevant. The focused exception
sheet contained 42 records, and the strict policy/outcome gate retained 28 retrieval candidates
before version collapse and final mechanism routing.

Alexandra's relevance review produced 10 distinct papers for the compulsory-schooling
teenage-pregnancy/birth stream, and we retrieved and extracted all 10 published or best-available
versions. Cummins is not one of those 10 tempo-set records. Alexandra's later full-text mechanism
review routed Cummins to decreased teenage pregnancy, so we use that paper as adjacent
completed-fertility context and do not count it as an eleventh approved tempo study.

We report these counts as GACS citation-frame accounting rather than as the protocol's eventual
Phase 1 keyword search plus Phase 2 snowball PRISMA flow. The two are different objects, and
relabeling the first as the second would credit the stream with a search it has not yet run.

## 5. Included studies

Each approved study is reported below with its design, its first-stage schooling or attendance
result, its fertility-timing result, and the age profile of that effect.

| Study | Setting and design | Maternal-own-education or attendance result | Fertility-timing result | Persistence and channel |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Black, Devereux, and Salvanes | United States laws and Norwegian reform | Mandatory attainment rises at covered dropout ages | Teen births fall 4.7--8.8% in US comparisons and about 3.5% in Norway | Effects concentrate at covered ages |
| Silles | Great Britain and Northern Ireland; compulsory-law IV | Leaving-age changes add about 0.25 years of schooling in the later period | Preferred IV estimates imply birth by 17 falls 1.0 pp and birth by 20 falls 4.0 pp per schooling year | Attendance and longer-run education channels are not separated |
| Wilson | United Kingdom; sharp RD | 1972 leaving-age reform extends compulsory enrollment | Motherhood at 16 falls 0.40 pp; cumulative effect by 18 falls 0.81 pp | Non-monotonic profile; effect dissipates after age 20 |
| Kırdar, Dayıoğlu, and Koç | Turkey; cohort policy discontinuity | Compulsory schooling rises from five to eight years | Marriage by 16 falls 44%; first birth by 17 falls 36% | Delayed marriage precedes delayed birth; effects later disappear |
| Geruso and Royer | United Kingdom; RD | 1972 reform raises women's schooling by about 0.31 year | Age-17 fertility falls 3.1--3.4 births per 1,000 | No effects at 18--19 or on completed family size by 45 |
| DeCicca and Krashinsky | Canada; compulsory-law IV | Laws instrument years of education | Teen birth probability falls about 2--3 pp | Effects concentrate at 17--18 and then disappear |
| Adamecz-Völgyi and Scharle | Hungary, Roma women; RD | Reform extends compulsory enrollment | Teenage motherhood falls 13.4--26.0%; motherhood is delayed about two years | Pregnancy falls during school terms, not summer or Christmas breaks |
| Arendt, Christensen, and Hjorth-Trolle | Denmark; fuzzy RD | Reform raises maternal education | Age at first birth rises about half a year | No effect on having children or number of children by age 39 |
| Chaijaroen and Panda | Thailand; donut-hole RD | Compulsory-schooling reform increases education | Ever birth falls about 4--5 pp at ages 14--15 and remains lower through 17 | Fertility catches up and is significantly higher at age 20 |
| Bhattacharjee | India; IV staggered DiD | Reform exposure increases schooling | Sexual activity by 18 falls 7.2 pp; marriage by 21 falls 3.9 pp; first birth by 21 falls 2.6 pp | Effects strengthen into early adulthood; knowledge and human capital dominate incapacitation |

Cummins uses the 1947 and 1972 English school-leaving reforms and finds preferred completed
marital-fertility effects near zero. That adjacent result helps define the boundary of the timing
claim and is not counted in the 10-study tempo set.

## 6. Quantitative synthesis and why we do not pool

We do not pool the 17 estimates that the effect-level extraction draws from the 10 approved
studies, because they do not estimate the same quantity. The direction is consistent at newly
covered ages: compulsory schooling reduces teenage births or postpones first birth. The estimates
behind that consistent direction mix age-specific and cumulative outcomes, probability points and
relative percentages, reduced forms and IV effects, different school-law contrasts, and different
age windows.

The prespecified pooling audit sorts the estimates into four outcome families: age at first birth,
age-specific birth probability, cumulative first-birth probability, and cumulative teen motherhood.
No family has three independent studies with both a comparable estimate and a standard error. A
forest plot or a pooled coefficient would therefore report a precision the evidence does not have.
Our preferred synthesis is a structured comparison of effect direction, magnitude, and age profile.

The age profiles carry more information than any pooled average would. Geruso and Royer find
effects at 16--17 but not at 18--19, DeCicca and Krashinsky find concentration at 17--18, and
Wilson's effect fades after age 20; Thailand shows explicit post-school catch-up. Read against the
covered-age window, these profiles trace postponement and do not trace a uniform permanent
reduction in births.

## 7. Which maternal-education channel the evidence identifies

No single maternal-education channel carries the literature, and each of the three substantive
channels is identified in some settings and not in others.

The strongest direct evidence for incapacitation comes from Adamecz-Völgyi and Scharle, who find
that pregnancy falls during the school year but not during summer or Christmas breaks. Geruso and
Royer point the same way: conceptions, rather than abortions, explain the British decline in
teenage births. Both results sit inside the covered-age window, which is where incapacitation
should bite if it operates at all.

Turkey provides the clearest marriage pathway, since the reform first reduces teenage marriage and
the birth effect follows marriage timing. India points in another direction. There, reductions in
sexual activity, marriage, and first birth persist or strengthen beyond the compulsory ages, and
Bhattacharjee emphasizes knowledge, contraception, and human capital rather than simple physical
attendance.

The literature therefore supports a channel mix rather than one universal maternal-education
channel. Direct attendance is persuasive for short-run effects at legally covered ages, delayed
marriage mediates some settings, and longer-run human-capital and knowledge effects matter
elsewhere.

## 8. Completed fertility and demographic significance

The evidence does not license treating fewer teenage births as equally fewer lifetime births.
Geruso and Royer estimate a precisely determined zero effect on completed family size by age 45,
and Arendt and coauthors find no effect on having children or on the number of children by age 39.
Thailand documents catch-up after school directly, and Cummins' adjacent reform evidence also rules
out a large completed marital-fertility effect.

The compulsory-schooling stream is thus demographically meaningful for teenage-birth timing and for
age at first birth, while its aggregate contribution to the FDT or the SDT cannot be classified as
significant under the protocol. A period-TFR decomposition requires a change in the age-specific
fertility rate at each affected age, combined across the shares of the population actually exposed,
with uncertainty attached. The extracted studies do not supply that common change in age-specific
fertility rates, nor do they supply a common treatment scale for a valid slope-sufficiency
calculation. Aggregate demographic significance remains Insufficient data.

## 9. Risk of bias and GRADE

The strongest studies in this stream use regression discontinuities around birth-date or cohort
cutoffs, verify schooling first stages, and measure births in administrative records. Remaining
risks include endogenous state-law adoption, exclusion restrictions when laws instrument education,
birth-date heaping and donut-hole choices, staggered-treatment comparisons, and survey-based
fertility measurement.

**GRADE: Moderate (unanimous three-rater panel).** Multiple quasi-experiments across the United
Kingdom, North America, Turkey, Hungary, Thailand, India, and Denmark converge on delayed teenage
births, and the direct term-time and conception evidence strengthens the causal interpretation.
Different channels and age windows, residual bias in some designs, and later catch-up prevent a
High rating.

## 10. Verdict

| Phenomenon | Causal credibility | Demographic significance | Bottom line |
|---|---|---|---|
| FDT | Moderate for teenage-birth timing | Insufficient data for aggregate significance | Maternal own-schooling credibly postpones early births, but its contribution to the total FDT decline is not scaled. |
| SDT | Moderate for teenage-birth timing | Insufficient data for aggregate significance | The driver explains some postponement, not an equivalent decline in completed fertility. |

Compulsory schooling is a credible cause of later first birth. The evidence is strongest for births
delayed during newly mandatory school years and weaker for any permanent reduction in family size.

## 11. Open questions and recommended studies

The most useful next analysis would harmonize the reforms onto age-specific fertility rates and
combine them with the share of women actually bound by each reform. That step would permit a
period-TFR tempo decomposition without treating IV effects per year of schooling as population-wide
policy effects.

Future studies should report conception, marriage, first birth, later catch-up, and completed
fertility for the same cohorts. Term-versus-holiday contrasts and administrative birth records are
especially valuable for separating incapacitation from human-capital channels. Until that
harmonized analysis exists, this stream should be cited as evidence on the timing of first birth and
not as evidence on the level of completed fertility.
