## 1. The claim

Compulsory schooling delays the age at which a girl has her first child. A law raising the
school-leaving age from, say, 14 to 16 puts her in a classroom for two years she would otherwise have
spent at home, at a job, or married, and those two years are supposed to move her first birth later
and cut the number of births to teenagers.

Delaying a birth is not preventing one. A woman who first gives birth at 21 instead of 17 may still
finish her childbearing years with as many children as she would have had otherwise. This chapter
keeps the two questions apart throughout: when a woman gives birth, and how many children she has
when she is done.

Two hypotheses in this review turn on school-leaving laws, and they count different children. The
child-economic-value hypothesis counts a schooled child's siblings, since parents who valued children
partly for what they earned want fewer of them once a law takes children out of paid work. This
chapter counts the schooled girl's own children, and asks how much one or two more years of her
education change the age at which she has the first one.

## 2. Theoretical mechanism

Take a girl who turns 14 the year her country raises the school-leaving age from 14 to 16, so that
she spends two years in a classroom she would otherwise have spent at home, at a job, or married.
School can push her first birth later in three ways, and the three last for different lengths of
time.

The first is the school day itself. Her hours go to a classroom, under adult supervision, among
classmates, and she has fewer occasions to conceive than she would have had at home or at work.
Demographers call this incapacitation, and it has a sharp signature: it works only while she is
actually attending, so it should weaken over the summer and at Christmas and stop the day the law
releases her.

The second is a later marriage. In several of the countries studied here a first birth follows a
wedding by a year or two, and a girl still enrolled at 15 is not being married at 15. Move the
wedding back two years and the first birth moves back with it and stays back, because she does not
marry twice as fast afterward to make up the difference. Incapacitation removes a chance to conceive
this month and returns it the next; a delayed marriage shifts the whole schedule of her adult life.

Data separate those two less easily than theory does. A study that finds fewer births at 16 usually
cannot say whether the girl was in a classroom during the month she would otherwise have conceived or
simply not yet married, because the reform moved attendance and marriage at the same time and both
show up in the same age-16 birth rate. Two kinds of evidence do separate them: a comparison of term
time against school holidays, which only incapacitation predicts, and the marriage rate itself, which
several studies measure directly.

The third is the change school makes in the woman herself. Two more years alter what she knows about
contraception, whom she meets and marries, what she can earn, and how much say she has at home, and
all of that outlasts the classroom. A woman who learned how contraception works at 15 still knows it
at 25, so this mechanism can move a birth that would have happened long after any law required her to
attend anything. Each mechanism therefore predicts a different age profile: an effect confined to the
newly covered ages, a delay that persists into her twenties, or effects that grow as she ages.

### A delayed birth is not a prevented birth

Postponement lowers the birth count in a given year without lowering the number of children anyone
ends up having. Suppose a reform stops births at 16, 17, and 18, and the same women give birth at 19,
20, and 21 instead. Count births in any single calendar year during the switch and you find fewer of
them, because the young mothers are missing while the older mothers have not arrived yet. Count the
children each woman has by the time she turns 45 and you may find no change at all.

The two counts have names. The period total fertility rate, or period TFR, is the number of children
a woman would have if she lived her whole life through the birth rates observed in one calendar year.
Completed cohort fertility is the number of children an actual woman actually had. Postponement
pushes the first down for a while and leaves the second alone, and catch-up is why. Catch-up is a
caution rather than a mechanism, since it delays no birth of its own. This chapter reports the two
counts separately and never treats evidence about one as evidence about the other.

## 3. Search strategy

One search supplies both this chapter and the chapter on the economic value of children, because both
hypotheses turn on the same school-leaving laws. The search began from seven papers, each found
independently, and followed citations in both directions: every paper those seven cited, and every
paper that cited them. Removing duplicates by DOI and title left 1,255 records.

A blinded screen of titles and abstracts sorted those records into 233 Relevant, 128 Uncertain, and
894 Not relevant. A focused review of exceptions covered 42 records the first screen had set aside. A
strict test then asked of each remaining paper whether it studied an actual schooling law and
reported a fertility outcome, and 28 passed. Some of the 28 were two versions of one study, a working
paper and its published article, and some belonged to the other hypothesis.

Ten distinct papers on compulsory schooling and the timing of teenage pregnancy and birth remain, and
we obtained and extracted all 10, using the published version where one exists. Cummins (2025) is not
among them. A later review of full texts routed that paper to a different hypothesis, decreased
teenage pregnancy, so it enters here only as adjacent evidence on completed fertility in Section 8
and is not an eleventh study.

## 6. Quantitative synthesis

We cannot pool the 17 estimates in these 10 studies, because they measure different outcomes and an
average across them would produce a number that answers no question anyone asked. One study reports
the chance a woman has given birth by age 17, another reports births per 1,000 women at exactly age
17, and another reports how much later her first birth arrives, in years. Some report a change in
percentage points and others a percentage change. Some report what the law did, and others report what
one more year of schooling does, using the law only as the instrument that moved schooling. The age
windows do not line up either.

The estimates agree on direction even where they disagree on units. Wherever a reform newly requires
a girl to be in school, teenage births fall or the first birth arrives later.

Sorted by what each one measures, the 17 estimates fall into four groups: age at first birth; the
chance of a birth at one specific age; the chance of having given birth by a given age; and the
chance of having become a mother at any point in the teenage years. No group holds three independent
studies reporting both a comparable estimate and its standard error, which is the minimum this review
requires before it averages anything. We compare the studies instead, on direction, on size, and on
the ages at which effects appear.

The ages carry more than an average would. Geruso and Royer (2018) find the effect at 16 and 17 and
nothing at 18 or 19. DeCicca and Krashinsky (2019) find theirs concentrated at 17 and 18. Wilson's
(2017) fades once women pass 20. In Thailand, fertility at 20 comes back significantly higher
(Chaijaroen and Panda 2023). Set those age profiles against the ages each law newly covered and they
describe postponement rather than a permanent loss of births.

## 7. Which mechanism does the work

No one mechanism explains every study, and which one dominates depends on the country.

Hungary gives the cleanest evidence for incapacitation. Adamecz-Völgyi and Scharle (2020) find that
pregnancies among Roma women fall during school terms and do not fall over the summer or the
Christmas break. A girl less likely to conceive in October but no less likely in July was kept from
conceiving by the school day itself rather than by anything she learned during it. Geruso and Royer
(2018) supply a second piece from Britain, where the drop in teenage births traces to fewer
conceptions and not to more abortions.

Turkey shows the marriage mechanism most plainly. Kırdar, Dayıoğlu, and Koç (2018) study a reform
that raised compulsory schooling from five years to eight; marriage by 16 fell 44 percent and first
birth by 17 fell 36 percent. Marriage moved first and births followed on the schedule marriage sets.

India runs the other way. Bhattacharjee (2025) finds that sexual activity by 18 falls 7.2 percentage
points, marriage by 21 falls 3.9 points, and first birth by 21 falls 2.6 points. The last two are
measured at 21, well past any age the reform required a girl to attend school, and an effect still
visible at 21 cannot be a girl sitting in a classroom. Bhattacharjee reads it as knowledge,
contraception, and human capital instead.

All three mechanisms survive the comparison. Attendance is convincing for the short-run effects that
show up at the ages a law newly covers, a delayed marriage carries the effect in countries where
marriage is the usual gateway to childbearing, and learning and earning carry it where the effect is
still visible in a woman's twenties.

## 8. Completed fertility and demographic significance

A fall in births to teenagers does not mean fewer children per woman, and the studies that follow
women long enough to check say so directly. Geruso and Royer (2018) find no effect on completed
family size by age 45, estimated precisely enough to rule out a large one. Arendt, Christensen, and
Hjorth-Trolle (2021) find no effect on whether a Danish woman has children, or on how many she has,
by age 39. Chaijaroen and Panda (2023) watch the catch-up happen in Thailand: births fall about 4 to
5 percentage points at ages 14 and 15 and stay lower through 17, and then fertility at 20 comes back
significantly higher. Cummins (2025), studying the English reforms of 1947 and 1972, likewise rules
out a large effect on completed marital fertility.

The hypothesis therefore matters demographically for the timing of teenage births and for age at
first birth. No study reports what share of the FDT or of the SDT this hypothesis accounts for, and
the estimates in hand cannot be assembled into one. Assembling one would take every reform's effect
expressed as the same quantity, namely the change in the birth rate at each age; the share of women
each law actually bound, since a law that touches only the girls who would have left at 14 barely
moves a national birth rate; and those numbers, with their uncertainty attached, carried into a
decomposition of the period TFR. The alternative check, which asks whether an effect of the estimated
size applied over the range the policy moved could produce the observed decline, needs a common
measure of how much treatment each reform delivered, and the extracted studies report none. Aggregate
demographic significance is Insufficient data.

## 9. Risk of bias and GRADE

The strongest studies here compare women born days or months apart on either side of a cutoff date,
confirm that schooling really did change at the cutoff, and count births in administrative registers
rather than asking women to remember.

Risks survive even in those designs. American state legislatures did not raise their leaving ages at
random, and a state that acted in a decade when teenage births were already falling makes its law
look more effective than it was. A law used as an instrument for years of schooling has to affect
births only by changing schooling: a reform that also put sex education into the curriculum would cut
births among girls whose attainment never moved, and the estimate would charge that fall to the extra
year of school. Reported birth dates pile up on round dates, so the women just above a cutoff are not
quite comparable to those just below; studies drop observations near the cutoff to handle it, and the
results can move with how wide a donut hole the authors choose. Where different places adopted
reforms in different years, a study can end up using already-treated places as the comparison group,
and those places' births are still moving in response to their own earlier reform, so the comparison
charges that movement to the later law. Some fertility histories also come from surveys in which
women report births from decades earlier.

**GRADE: Moderate (unanimous three-rater panel).** GRADE runs from Very low through Low and Moderate
to High, and three raters scored this hypothesis independently at Moderate. Quasi-experiments in the
United Kingdom, North America, Turkey, Hungary, Thailand, India, and Denmark all point the same way
on delayed teenage births, and the term-time and conception results strengthen the causal reading. We
stop short of High because the dominant mechanism differs across settings, the ages at which effects
appear differ too, some designs carry real bias, and the births come back later.

## 10. Verdict

| Phenomenon | Causal credibility | Demographic significance | Bottom line |
|---|---|---|---|
| FDT | Moderate for teenage-birth timing | Insufficient data for aggregate significance | Maternal own-schooling credibly postpones early births, but its contribution to the total FDT decline is not scaled. |
| SDT | Moderate for teenage-birth timing | Insufficient data for aggregate significance | The driver explains some postponement, not an equivalent decline in completed fertility. |

A school-leaving reform is a credible cause of a later first birth. The evidence is strongest for
births pushed out of the years a reform newly made compulsory, and thinnest for any lasting reduction
in the number of children a woman ends up with.

## 11. Open questions and recommended studies

The most useful next analysis would put every reform on a common footing. It would convert each
reform's effect into a change in the birth rate at each age, weight each reform by the share of women
it actually bound, and then run a tempo decomposition of the period TFR. That route also avoids the
step we most want to skip. These reforms raised schooling by around a third of a year on average, and
only among the girls who would otherwise have left early, so the estimated return to a full year of
schooling is not the effect of the policy on a whole population.

Studies of individual reforms would help most by reporting conception, marriage, first birth, later
catch-up, and completed fertility for a single set of cohorts rather than for several. Comparisons of
term time against school holidays, and birth counts drawn from administrative registers, do the most
to separate the school day from the things school teaches.
