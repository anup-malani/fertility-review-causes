# Despair and Accelerated Childbearing

**Category:** Cultural (psychological / existential)
**Primary mechanism:** Where economic opportunity is foreclosed, young women do not postpone childbearing, because postponement buys them little — so the same bleakness that defers a birth elsewhere brings it forward here.

## 1. The claim

This chapter explores the effect of foreclosed economic opportunity on the timing of first birth.

### 1.1 In plain terms

In plain terms: a young woman in a place where the good jobs have gone can look at waiting to have a child and ask what the waiting is *for*. Delay is supposed to buy something — training, a career, a better partner, a steadier life. Where none of that is on offer, delay buys nothing, and the reason to put motherhood off disappears. Meanwhile having a child is one thing she can do that is unmistakably worth doing, that makes her someone, and that is available now.

So the prediction is that she has a child **earlier**, and more often outside marriage.

This is the same starting point as the companion chapter and the opposite ending. There, hopelessness stops a birth; here it brings one forward. **That is not a contradiction to be resolved by averaging, and this review does not average it.** The difference is what the hopelessness is *about*: whether a person doubts they could provide for a child, or doubts that waiting would improve anything.

### 1.2 The claim precisely

The parameter this chapter estimates is the change in the probability of a first birth before a given age, and in the probability that it occurs outside marriage, caused by a reduction in the perceived return to delaying childbearing, signed so that a positive value means earlier and more nonmarital births.

The chapter is scoped to the **SDT** (the Second Demographic Transition, roughly 1965 onward). There is no **PM** or **FDT** cell.

**The claim is about the timing and marital context of first birth — teen, adolescent and nonmarital childbearing — and its predicted direction is upward.** The companion chapter's claim is about completed quantum and points down.

**What separates the two chapters is what the despair is about, not when it points.** Both mechanisms are forward-looking. **The companion's agent doubts the capacity to provide for a child; this chapter's agent doubts the return to waiting.** A present-versus-future distinction would misdescribe both — the hypothesis list's own wording for the deferral claim is that "the subjective sense of having a viable future disappears."

**This is a behavioural parameter with no identity arm.**

**Margin.** Timing and marital context of the *first* birth, as against the companion's completed count. **The two are never pooled** (§7).

**What would make the hypothesis wrong.** It is wrong if foreclosed opportunity leaves the timing of first birth unmoved; if the association between lower-tail inequality and early nonmarital birth is confounded by the level of disadvantage rather than by the return to delay; or if the observed acceleration is compositional rather than behavioural.

## 2. Theoretical mechanism

In the reader's own vocabulary: this is an **opportunity-cost** argument, and it is the cleanest one in the D.3.c pair. The cost of an early birth is the discounted value of what delay would have purchased. Where the trajectory delay buys is unavailable, **that opportunity cost falls toward zero and the optimal age at first birth falls with it.** No preference has changed and no budget constraint has moved; the return to an alternative use of time has vanished.

**Two accounts converge, one economic and one ethnographic.**

**The economic account is Kearney and Levine (2014)**, who find that low-SES young women are more likely to have a nonmarital birth where lower-tail income inequality is greater, and propose a model of adolescent decision-making to interpret it. The mechanism is a judgement about the return to delay.

**The ethnographic account is Edin and Kefalas (2005)**, who document that poor women in American cities place motherhood before marriage deliberately, treating it as the one achievable source of meaning, identity and adult standing when the alternative life course has been foreclosed. Wilson (1996) supplies the structural antecedent for both.

**The two models of a child are what generate the opposite signs.** Modelled as an **investment good**, a child is deferred when the future is bleak. Modelled as a **meaning good available now**, the same bleakness brings the child forward. **Which model applies is an empirical question about a population, not a theoretical question to be settled in advance** — and §11 argues the aggregate sign may not be a well-posed quantity at all.

## 5. The ideal design

Written before the literature was screened, which here is literal.

### 5.1 The ideal estimand

The change in the probability of a first birth before age 20, and in the probability that it occurs outside marriage, caused by an exogenous reduction in the **return to delaying childbearing** — the earnings and stability a young woman could expect to gain by waiting — **holding the current level of disadvantage fixed.**

The holding-fixed clause is the specification and it is what the existing canon lacks. **Lower-tail inequality, this literature's usual treatment, moves both the return to delay and the level of disadvantage at once.** An estimate that does not separate them is consistent with the mechanism in §2 and with a simple income effect, which is C.1.a's.

**And the ideal design estimates both margins in one population** — completed quantum *and* timing of first birth, under one identification strategy — because §11 argues the review's central question is whether the two chapters describe two populations or one population's two responses.

### 5.2 The design that would identify it

**Source of variation.** A shock to the **return to delay** that does not change current household resources: a change in the local returns to schooling; the arrival or withdrawal of an employer offering a career ladder rather than a wage; a training-programme rollout; or a change in college access that alters the *option* without transferring income.

**Comparison group.** Young women in comparable local labour markets with the same current disadvantage and an unchanged return to delay.

**Identifying assumption.** The shock moved the expected return to waiting and not current consumption possibilities. **Falsifiable: enter current household income and local wage levels alongside the treatment.** If the treatment's effect survives them, the mechanism is the return to delay; if not, it is an income effect.

**Estimating equation.** Hazard of first birth before 20 and probability of a nonmarital first birth, on the return-to-delay shock, with current resources entered — **and, on the same sample, completed fertility at 45**, so the two margins are estimated together rather than in different traditions with different data.

**Data required.** Individual panel from adolescence to completed fertility, linked to local labour-market conditions. **This is the same data requirement as the companion chapter's, which is why one study could serve both.**

**Sample size.** Ordinary; the outcomes are common in the affected population.

**What the ideal design excludes.** Cross-sectional associations between inequality and teen birth rates, which cannot separate the return to delay from the level of disadvantage. And single-margin designs, which cannot address the sign question in §11.

### 5.3 Distance from the ideal — an advance assessment

No study can be scored, because none has been screened. Two things are nonetheless already established.

**This chapter's canon estimates its mechanism against its outcome directly, which the companion's does not.** Kearney and Levine measure a treatment and a fertility-timing outcome in one design and interpret it through an explicit model of the return to delay. That is a real distance from §5.1 — inequality is a proxy for the return to delay, not a measure of it, and the level of disadvantage is not held fixed — but **it is a proxy for the right thing, estimated against the right outcome.** The companion chapter has nothing of that kind.

**Nobody estimates both margins in one population.** The literature estimates them separately, in different traditions, with different data. **That is the single most consequential gap for the hypothesis as registered**, because without it the review cannot say whether the two chapters describe two distinct populations or one population's two responses — and if it is the latter, the aggregate sign is not a well-posed quantity.

## 8. Demographic significance

The phenomenon to be explained is measured in whole children — the SDT is a fall of roughly one and a half births per woman; this mechanism currently offers no estimate in any unit, and **points the other way.**

**BLOCKED.** Two features of this chapter's computation differ from the companion's and are fixed now to prevent an error at synthesis. Both are `PROTOCOL.md` §4.2.1 questions.

**The denominator is teen and nonmarital births, not total births.** A ceiling computed against total births would understate this mechanism's reach within the population it concerns. §4.2.1 requires the denominator to be the phenomenon's own change in matching units, and for a timing-and-marital-context mechanism the relevant phenomenon is the change in early and nonmarital childbearing, not the change in completed fertility.

**The sign is positive, and the two chapters net rather than add.** **An accelerating mechanism works *against* the fertility decline this review is explaining.** A significance verdict that summed the two chapters' magnitudes would double-count in the wrong direction. ## 10. Verdict

**No verdict.** Nothing has been screened.

**The one number to carry away: ten.** That is roughly the factor by which this chapter's literature exceeds its companion's — 4,800 records against 474 on the open index. **The two halves of one registry entry are not two halves of one evidence base**, and the asymmetry has been visible since reconnaissance.

Three things are established and worth carrying.

**This chapter's canon measures the right outcome with a proxy for the right mechanism.** Kearney and Levine estimate a treatment against fertility timing in one design, interpreted through an explicit model of the return to delay. The proxy is imperfect — lower-tail inequality moves the return to delay *and* the level of disadvantage — but it is a proxy for the right thing.

**Nobody estimates both margins in one population.** The literature splits them across traditions and datasets, which is exactly the split the hypothesis needs closed.

**The pre-2000 American literature is out of scope by decision, not by evidence**, and it is this chapter's direct antecedent. A reader should assume no coverage of it.

**What would produce a verdict:** an API credential and the shared two-stage screen. **What would produce a good verdict** is the design in §5.2 — and specifically its both-margins requirement, which would settle the sign question in §11 for the whole hypothesis rather than for this chapter alone.

## 11. Open questions

**PI calls required.**

1. **The sign question is the review's, not the field's.** Both mechanisms can hold at once, in one community, of different women — one deferring because she cannot provide, another accelerating because waiting buys nothing. **If that is what is happening, the aggregate sign of "despair on fertility" is not a well-posed quantity at all**, and the review's contribution is to say so precisely rather than to report a pooled estimate that describes neither.
2. **Should D.3.c be two registry entries?** §8.3 shows the shared entry is an active hazard: the two chapters carry opposite signs and net rather than add, and a reader summing them would double-count in the wrong direction.
