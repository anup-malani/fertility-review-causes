## 1. The claim

Compulsory schooling delays the age at which a girl has her first child. That is the claim this
chapter evaluates. A law raising the school-leaving age from, say, 14 to 16 puts a girl in a
classroom for two years she would otherwise have spent at home, at a job, or married, and the claim
is that those two years move her first birth later and cut the number of births to teenagers.

Delaying a birth is not the same as preventing one. A woman who first gives birth at 21 instead of
17 may still finish her childbearing years with exactly as many children as she would have had
otherwise. This chapter therefore keeps two questions apart from beginning to end: when a woman
gives birth, and how many children she has when she is done.

Two hypotheses in this review turn on schooling, and they differ in whose schooling matters. The
child-economic-value hypothesis is about the children. A law that sends children to school takes
them out of paid work, so parents who wanted children partly for the money they brought in want
fewer of them. This chapter is about the mother herself. The girl kept in school is the woman whose
births we are counting, and the question is how much one or two more years of her own education
change the age at which she first gives birth.

## 2. Theoretical mechanism

Three different things about an extra year or two of school could push a girl's first birth later.
Take a girl who turns 14 the year her country raises the school-leaving age from 14 to 16, so that
she sits in a classroom for two years she would otherwise have spent at home, at a job, or married.
The three mechanisms are worth separating, because they last for different lengths of time.

The first mechanism is the school day itself. She spends her hours in a classroom, under adult
supervision, among classmates. She has fewer occasions to conceive than she would have had at home
or at work. Demographers call this incapacitation, and it has a sharp signature: it works only while
she is actually attending. It should weaken during the summer and at Christmas, and it should stop
altogether the day the law releases her.

The second mechanism is a later marriage. In several of the countries studied here, a first birth
follows a wedding by a year or two, and a girl still enrolled at 15 is not being married at 15. When
the wedding moves back two years, the first birth moves back with it and stays back, because she
does not marry twice as fast afterward to make up the difference. The two mechanisms differ in how
long they last. Incapacitation removes a chance to conceive this month and returns it next month. A
delayed marriage shifts the whole schedule of her adult life, and the shift outlives the classroom.

Telling the two apart in the data is harder than telling them apart in theory. A study that finds
fewer births at 16 often cannot say whether the girl was sitting in a classroom during the month she
would otherwise have conceived, or was simply not yet married. Two kinds of evidence do separate
them, and both appear below: a comparison of term time against school holidays, which only
incapacitation predicts, and the marriage rate itself, which several studies measure directly.

The third mechanism is the change school makes in the woman herself. Two more years alter what she
knows about contraception, whom she meets and marries, what she can earn, and how much say she has
at home. All of these outlast the classroom. A woman who learned how contraception works at 15 still
knows it at 25, which is why this mechanism can move a birth that would have happened long after any
law required her to attend anything.

The three mechanisms disagree about when the effect should fade. Incapacitation predicts an effect
confined to the ages the new law covers, and nothing after. A later marriage predicts a delay that
persists into her twenties. Learning and earning predict effects that grow as she gets older. The
studies below can be read against those three predictions, and Section 7 does exactly that.

### A delayed birth is not a prevented birth

Postponement lowers the birth count in a given year without lowering the number of children anyone
ends up having. Suppose a reform stops births at 16, 17, and 18, and the same women give birth at
19, 20, and 21 instead. Count births in any single calendar year during the switch and you will find
fewer of them, because the young mothers are missing while the older mothers have not arrived yet.
Count the children each woman has by the time she turns 45 and you may find no change at all.

The two counts have names, and the difference between them is the heart of this chapter. The first
is the period total fertility rate, or period TFR: the number of children a woman would have if she
lived her whole life through the birth rates observed in one calendar year. The second is completed
cohort fertility: the number of children an actual woman actually had. Postponement pushes the first
down for a while and leaves the second alone. Catch-up is the reason, and it is a caution rather
than a mechanism, since it does not delay any birth. This chapter reports the two counts separately
and never treats evidence about one as evidence about the other.

## 3. Search strategy

One search supplies both this chapter and the chapter on the economic value of children, because
both hypotheses turn on the same school-leaving laws. The search began from seven papers, each found
independently, and then followed citations in both directions: every paper those seven cited, and
every paper that cited them. Removing duplicates by DOI and title left 1,255 records.

A blinded screen of titles and abstracts sorted the 1,255 records into three piles: 233 Relevant,
128 Uncertain, and 894 Not relevant. A focused review of exceptions covered 42 records the first
screen had set aside. A strict test then asked of each remaining paper whether it studied an actual
schooling law and reported a fertility outcome, and 28 papers passed. Some of those 28 turned out to
be two versions of the same study, a working paper and its published article, and some belonged to
the other hypothesis rather than this one.

Our review left 10 distinct papers on compulsory schooling and the timing of teenage pregnancy and
birth. We obtained and extracted all 10, using the published version where one exists and the best
available version otherwise. Cummins is not among the 10. A later review of full texts routed that
paper to a different hypothesis, decreased teenage pregnancy, so it appears here as adjacent
evidence about completed fertility and is not counted as an eleventh study.

long the effect lasted.

| Silles | Great Britain and Northern Ireland; compulsory-law IV | Leaving-age changes add about 0.25 years of schooling in the later period | Preferred IV estimates imply birth by 17 falls 1.0 pp and birth by 20 falls 4.0 pp per schooling year | Attendance and longer-run education channels are not separated |

| Chaijaroen and Panda | Thailand; donut-hole RD | Compulsory-schooling reform increases education | Ever birth falls about 4--5 pp at ages 14--15 and remains lower through 17 | Fertility catches up and is significantly higher at age 20 |
| Bhattacharjee | India; IV staggered DiD | Reform exposure increases schooling | Sexual activity by 18 falls 7.2 pp; marriage by 21 falls 3.9 pp; first birth by 21 falls 2.6 pp | Effects strengthen into early adulthood; knowledge and human capital dominate incapacitation |

Three abbreviations recur in that table. "pp" means percentage points, so a fall of 1.0 pp takes a
rate of 10 percent down to 9. "RD" means regression discontinuity, a design that compares women born
just before a cutoff date with women born just after it, on the reasoning that the two groups differ
in the law that governed them and in little else. "IV" means instrumental variables, a design that
uses the law as a lever to move schooling and then reports the effect of a year of schooling rather
than the effect of the law.

Cummins studies the English school-leaving reforms of 1947 and 1972 and finds preferred effects on
completed marital fertility near zero. That result marks where the timing claim stops, and Cummins
stays outside the 10-study set.

## 6. Quantitative synthesis

We cannot pool the 17 estimates in these 10 studies, because they all look at different outcomes.
Pooling means averaging estimates into one number with one margin of error, the standard output of a
meta-analysis. Averaging only works when the numbers being averaged answer the same question.

These 17 numbers answer different questions. One study reports the chance a woman has given birth by
age 17. Another reports births per 1,000 women at exactly age 17. Another reports how much later her
first birth arrives, measured in years. Some report a change in percentage points and others a
percentage change. Some report what happened when the law changed, and others report what one more
year of schooling does, using the law only as the lever that moved schooling. The age ranges do not
line up either. An average taken across that mixture would not mean anything.

The estimates agree on direction even where they disagree on units. Wherever a reform newly requires
a girl to be in school, teenage births fall or the first birth arrives later.

We sorted the 17 estimates into four groups according to what each one measures: age at first birth;
the chance of a birth at one specific age; the chance of having given birth by a given age; and the
chance of having become a mother at any point in the teenage years. No group contains three
independent studies that report both a comparable estimate and its standard error, the number saying
how precisely the estimate was measured. Three is the minimum this review requires before it will
average anything. A pooled coefficient, or a forest plot lining up each study's estimate and error
bar against a pooled average, would put a decimal point on a quantity the underlying evidence does
not pin down. We compare the studies instead, on direction, on size, and on the ages at which
effects appear.

The ages tell us more than an average would. Geruso and Royer find the effect at 16 and 17 and
nothing at 18 or 19. DeCicca and Krashinsky find theirs concentrated at 17 and 18. Wilson's fades
once women pass 20. In Thailand, fertility at 20 comes back significantly higher. Set those age
profiles against the ages each law newly covered and they describe postponement rather than a
permanent loss of births.

## 7. Which mechanism does the work

No one mechanism explains every study, and which one dominates depends on the country.

Hungary gives the cleanest evidence for incapacitation. Adamecz-Völgyi and Scharle find that
pregnancies among Roma women fall during school terms and do not fall over the summer or the
Christmas break. A girl less likely to conceive in October but no less likely in July was kept from
conceiving by the school day itself rather than by anything she learned during it. Geruso and Royer
supply a second piece from Britain, where the drop in teenage births traces to fewer conceptions and
not to more abortions.

Turkey shows the marriage mechanism most plainly. The reform raised compulsory schooling from five
years to eight, marriage by 16 fell 44 percent, and first birth by 17 fell 36 percent. Marriage moved
first and births followed on the schedule marriage sets.

India runs the other way. Bhattacharjee finds that sexual activity by 18 falls 7.2 percentage points,
marriage by 21 falls 3.9 points, and first birth by 21 falls 2.6 points. The last two are measured at
21, well past any age the reform required a girl to attend school. An effect still visible at 21
cannot be a girl sitting in a classroom, and Bhattacharjee reads it as knowledge, contraception, and
human capital instead.

All three mechanisms survive this comparison. Attendance is convincing for the short-run effects that
show up at the ages a law newly covers. A delayed marriage carries the effect in countries where
marriage is the usual gateway to childbearing. Learning and earning carry it where the effect is
still visible in a woman's twenties.

## 8. Completed fertility and demographic significance

A fall in births to teenagers does not mean fewer children per woman, and the studies that follow
women long enough to check say so directly. Geruso and Royer find no effect on completed family size by
age 45, and their estimate is precise enough to rule out a large one. Arendt and coauthors find no
effect on whether a Danish woman has children, or on how many she has, by age 39. Chaijaroen and
Panda watch the catch-up happen in Thailand: births fall about 4 to 5 percentage points at ages 14
and 15 and stay lower through 17, and then fertility at 20 comes back significantly higher. Cummins,
studying the adjacent English reforms, likewise rules out a large effect on completed marital
fertility.

The hypothesis therefore matters demographically for the timing of teenage births and for age at
first birth, and its contribution to either fertility decline as a whole cannot be scored. Scoring
that contribution would need three ingredients this literature does not supply. It would need every
reform's effect expressed as the same quantity, namely the change in the birth rate at each age. It
would need to know what share of women each law actually bound, since a law touching only the girls
who would have left at 14 barely moves a national birth rate. And it would need those numbers,
with their uncertainty attached, carried into a decomposition of the period TFR. The review also runs
a second test, asking whether an effect of the estimated size, applied over the range the policy
actually moved, could produce the fertility decline we observe; that test needs a common measure of
how much treatment each reform delivered, and the extracted studies do not report one. Aggregate
demographic significance is therefore Insufficient data.

## 9. Risk of bias and GRADE

The strongest studies here compare women born days or months apart. A reform that binds everyone born
after a given date creates two groups of women alike in nearly everything except the schooling law
that governed them, so comparing the groups isolates the law. These studies also confirm that
schooling really did change at the cutoff, and they count births in administrative registers rather
than asking women to remember.

Five risks survive even in the better designs.

American state legislatures did not raise their leaving ages at random. A state that acted in a
decade when teenage births were already falling will make the law look more effective than it was.

Studies that use a law as a lever to estimate the return to a year of schooling need that law to
affect births only by changing schooling. That requirement is an assumption, not a finding, and a
reform that also changed, say, what teenagers were taught about contraception would violate it.

Reported birth dates pile up on round dates, which distorts comparisons right at a cutoff. Studies
handle the problem by dropping observations near the cutoff, a "donut hole," and the results can
move with how wide a hole the authors choose.

Where different places adopted reforms in different years, a study can end up comparing the later
adopters with places already treated, and that comparison biases the estimate.

Some fertility histories come from surveys in which women report births that happened years earlier.

**GRADE: Moderate (unanimous three-rater panel).** GRADE is the scale this review uses to record how
much confidence the evidence supports, running from Very low through Low and Moderate to High. Three
raters scored this hypothesis independently and all three said Moderate. Quasi-experiments in the
United Kingdom, North America, Turkey, Hungary, Thailand, India, and Denmark all point the same way
on delayed teenage births, and the term-time and conception results strengthen the causal reading. We
stop short of High because the dominant mechanism differs across settings, the ages at which effects
appear differ too, some designs carry real bias, and the births come back later.

## 10. Verdict

| Phenomenon | Causal credibility | Demographic significance | Bottom line |
|---|---|---|---|
| FDT | Moderate for teenage-birth timing | Insufficient data for aggregate significance | Maternal own-schooling credibly postpones early births, but its contribution to the total FDT decline is not scaled. |
| SDT | Moderate for teenage-birth timing | Insufficient data for aggregate significance | The driver explains some postponement, not an equivalent decline in completed fertility. |

A school-leaving reform is a credible cause of a later first birth. The evidence is strongest for
births pushed out of the years a reform newly made compulsory, and thinnest for any lasting
reduction in the number of children a woman ends up with.

## 11. Open questions and recommended studies

The most useful next analysis would put every reform on a common footing. It would convert each
reform's effect into a change in the birth rate at each age, weight each reform by the share of women
it actually bound, and then run a tempo decomposition of the period TFR. That route also keeps us
from the step we most want to skip: treating the estimated return to one year of schooling as though
it were the effect of a policy on a whole population.

Studies of individual reforms would help most by reporting conception, marriage, first birth, later
catch-up, and completed fertility for a single set of cohorts rather than for several. Comparisons of
term time against school holidays, and birth counts drawn from administrative registers, do the most
to separate the school day from the things school teaches.
