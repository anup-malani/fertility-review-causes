**Category:** Demographic  
**Primary mechanism:** Compulsory schooling changes a prospective mother's own education and keeps

**Target phenomena:** FDT and SDT  
**Draft status:** Complete nested-driver draft; protocol stage-13 readability review deferred to

## 1. The claim

The claim we evaluate is that a compulsory-schooling mandate postpones motherhood by acting on the
prospective mother's own years in school. A higher leaving age can cut pregnancies and births among
teenagers for as long as the mandate binds, push back marriage and the onset of sexual activity, or
work through slower changes in what a woman knows, whom she partners with, and what she can earn.
Each of these is a statement about when women bear children. None of them says that a woman finishes
her childbearing years with fewer children.

Whose schooling does the work is what separates this stream from the child-economic-value
hypothesis. That account turns on the children: parents want fewer of them once schooling keeps the
young out of paid work. Here the schooling belongs to the girl or young woman herself, and the
question is what an extra year or two of it does to the age at which she first gives birth.

## 2. Theoretical mechanism

Four channels run from a schooling mandate to the timing of a birth, and they divide on whether they
keep working once a girl ages out of the years a reform newly makes compulsory. Call those years the
covered-age window. A channel confined to the window can only rearrange births across ages; a
channel that outlives the window can in principle change how many births occur at all. The
distinction organizes the evidence that follows.

First, **incapacitation** works only while the mandate binds. A girl in a classroom spends her hours
differently, under adult supervision, among a different set of peers, with fewer occasions to
conceive. The sharpest available test of the channel sets term time against holidays: conceptions
should fall when school is in session and recover when it is out.

Second, staying in school can push back **marriage or stable partnership formation**. Where
childbearing follows marriage as a matter of course, a later wedding delays a first birth by
arithmetic alone.

Third, more years of education can alter **human capital, knowledge, and preferences**. A woman who
knows more about contraception, matches with a different sort of partner, bargains harder inside her
household, or faces a steeper wage profile carries the effect past the last covered age.

Fourth, whatever falls early may reappear later as **catch-up**. Suppose the births a reform prevents
at ages 16--18 arrive instead at ages 19--21. Period fertility then dips for a few years while
completed cohort fertility barely moves. That possibility is what puts catch-up at the center of the
Temple/tempo reading: a passing deficit of births drags period TFR down without a matching change in
lifetime births.

## 6. Quantitative synthesis

We decline to pool the 17 estimates that the effect-level extraction draws from the 10 approved
studies, because those estimates do not measure a common quantity. The sign is stable wherever a
reform newly covers an age: schooling either cuts teenage births or moves a first birth later.
Underneath that stable sign sit age-specific outcomes beside cumulative ones, probability points
beside relative percentages, reduced forms beside IV effects, one school-law contrast beside
another, and age windows that do not line up.

Our prespecified pooling audit sorts the estimates into four outcome families: age at first birth,
age-specific birth probability, cumulative first-birth probability, and cumulative teen motherhood.
Not one family holds three independent studies reporting both a comparable estimate and its standard
error. A forest plot or a pooled coefficient built on that base would advertise a precision the
underlying estimates cannot deliver. We synthesize instead by comparing direction, magnitude, and
age profile across studies.

Age profiles teach more than an average would. Geruso and Royer locate the effect at 16--17 and find
nothing at 18--19, DeCicca and Krashinsky find theirs concentrated at 17--18, Wilson's fades once
women pass 20, and in Thailand fertility visibly returns after school ends. Read against the
covered-age window, those profiles describe postponement rather than a uniform and permanent loss of
births.

## 7. Which maternal-education channel does the work

No single channel carries the whole literature; which one dominates depends on the setting.

Incapacitation has its cleanest support in Adamecz-Völgyi and Scharle, who find pregnancies falling
during the school year and holding over the summer and Christmas breaks. Geruso and Royer supply a
second piece from Britain, where the drop in teenage births traces to fewer conceptions and not to
more abortions. Both results sit inside the covered-age window, which is where a channel that runs
through attendance ought to appear if it operates at all.

Turkey shows the marriage pathway most plainly, because the reform lowers teenage marriage first and
births then move on the schedule that marriage sets. India runs the other way. There the reductions
in sexual activity, in marriage, and in first birth hold or grow once the compulsory ages have
passed, and Bhattacharjee reads them as evidence for knowledge, contraception, and human capital
rather than for physical attendance on its own.

Attendance itself is convincing for the short-run effects that show up at legally covered ages,
delayed marriage carries the effect in some countries, and the slower human-capital and knowledge
effects carry it in others.

## 8. Completed fertility and demographic significance

Fewer births to teenagers is not the same finding as fewer births per woman, and the studies say so
directly. Geruso and Royer estimate a precise zero for completed family size by age 45. Arendt and
coauthors find no effect on whether a woman has children, or on how many she has, by age 39.
Thailand records the catch-up itself. Cummins, from the adjacent English reforms, likewise rules out
a large effect on completed marital fertility.

The stream therefore matters demographically for the timing of teenage births and for age at first
birth, while its aggregate contribution to the FDT or the SDT cannot be classified as significant
under the protocol. Scoring aggregate significance would require a common change in age-specific
fertility rates, weighted by the share of women each reform actually bound, carried into a period-TFR
decomposition with uncertainty attached. The extracted studies supply no such common change, and
they supply no common treatment scale on which a slope-sufficiency calculation would be valid.
Aggregate demographic significance remains Insufficient data.

## 9. Risk of bias and GRADE

The best designs in this stream exploit a discontinuity in birth date or birth cohort, confirm that
schooling actually moved at the cutoff, and count births in administrative records. Risks that
survive include state legislatures adopting laws endogenously, the exclusion restrictions required
when a law instruments for years of education, heaping at birth dates and the donut holes chosen to
handle it, comparisons across staggered treatment timing, and fertility histories collected by
survey.

**GRADE: Moderate (unanimous three-rater panel).** Quasi-experiments in the United Kingdom, North
America, Turkey, Hungary, Thailand, India, and Denmark all point the same way on delayed teenage
births, and the direct term-time and conception results strengthen the causal reading. We stop short
of High because channels and age windows differ across settings, some designs retain bias, and
births come back later.

## 10. Verdict

| Phenomenon | Causal credibility | Demographic significance | Bottom line |
|---|---|---|---|
| FDT | Moderate for teenage-birth timing | Insufficient data for aggregate significance | Maternal own-schooling credibly postpones early births, but its contribution to the total FDT decline is not scaled. |
| SDT | Moderate for teenage-birth timing | Insufficient data for aggregate significance | The driver explains some postponement, not an equivalent decline in completed fertility. |

Our bottom line is that a school-leaving reform is a credible cause of a later first birth. Support
is strongest for births pushed out of the newly mandatory school years and thinnest for any lasting
reduction in the number of children a woman ends up with.

## 11. Open questions and recommended studies

The next analysis worth running would put every reform on common footing in age-specific fertility
rates and weight each by the share of women it actually bound. That harmonized analysis would make a
period-TFR tempo decomposition possible without the step we most want to avoid, which is treating an
IV effect per year of schooling as if it were the effect of a policy on a population.

Studies of individual reforms would help most by reporting conception, marriage, first birth, later
catch-up, and completed fertility for one set of cohorts rather than for several. Contrasts between
term and holiday, and birth counts drawn from administrative registers, do the most to separate
incapacitation from the human-capital channels.
