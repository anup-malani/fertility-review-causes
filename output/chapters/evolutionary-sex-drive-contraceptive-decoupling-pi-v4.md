**Category:** Biological  
**Primary mechanism:** Natural selection built a strong drive for sex and never built a comparable
drive for children, because sex reliably produced children on its own. High fertility came as a
by-product. Contraception breaks that arrangement: the drive keeps running and stops producing

**Fertility declines this chapter tests the hypothesis against:** variation in fertility among

Demographic Transition (1965 to the present). We abbreviate the three PM, FDT, and SDT. The pill era
of the second is the sharp case.

## 1. The verdict

The hypothesis makes two claims, and the evidence treats them very differently. The first: once
contraception is available, status and wealth stop predicting how many children a person has. A large
body of studies supports it. The second, the claim the hypothesis is named for: births fall because
contraception cuts the tie between sex and reproduction, and would fall even if nobody's desire for
children had changed. Almost no study can test the second.

No study in this evidence base measures fertility while holding the desire for children fixed, so the
effect of the severing cannot be separated from a genuine fall in desired family size. Studies of
contraceptive access measure the whole fall in births that follows when contraception arrives, and
that fall mixes the two together. Unidentified does not mean zero: the numbers these studies report
fit the decoupling account and fit a shrinking desire for children equally well, and nothing in their
designs chooses between them.

The shrinking desire for children belongs to two other chapters. Modern contraceptive technology
(A.2) treats contraception as the thing that closes the gap between the family size people want and
the family size they reach; shifting family preferences (D.1) treats the wanted family size itself as
the thing that shrank. Net of those two channels, the share of the fertility decline that belongs to
decoupling is not presently estimable and may be small.

That split sets the verdict. Where the hypothesis says something distinctive it rests on formal
models and one descriptive trend. It is most plausible for the Second Demographic Transition, when
the contraceptive pill severed sex from reproduction across whole populations, and least useful for
anything before 1960, when no technology could do the severing.

## 2. The claim

Natural selection built a strong drive for sex and never built a comparable drive for children.
Throughout human history the first drive delivered the second outcome on its own: people who had sex
had children, whether or not children were the point, so selection had no reason to build a separate
appetite for children on top of an appetite that already produced them.

Contraception breaks that arrangement. The drive for sex runs at full strength and yields no births,
nothing of comparable force stands behind children to make up the loss, and births fall. One
prediction separates this hypothesis from every account in which contraception merely helps people
reach a smaller family they had already settled on: births fall even when nobody's wish for children
has changed.

Biologists separate two kinds of cause, and the distinction carries the argument. The reason a trait
was selected is its ultimate cause, here reproductive success; the machinery inside the animal that
delivers the behavior is its proximate cause, here sexual desire. Selection cannot install a taste
for an outcome directly. It installs the equipment that produced the outcome under ancestral
conditions, and since sex reliably produced children, an appetite for sex sufficed and cost less than
a separate standing appetite for children would have. Dawkins (1976), Trivers (1972), and Symons
(1979) work out the general version: animals run the drives they were issued rather than calculating
their own reproductive success.

Barkow and Burley (1980) state the demographic consequence flatly. A continuous sex drive evolved to
serve reproduction, and once that drive can be satisfied without reproducing, fertility should fall.
Kingsley Davis (1986) treats low fertility itself as the puzzle evolutionary theory has to solve, and
its sharpest form is a comparison anyone can check: if people acted to maximize the number of
descendants they leave, the richest people in modern societies would have the largest families, and
they do not.

The theory predicts that severing sex from reproduction shuts off the mechanism that used to produce
high fertility without anyone intending it. It does not predict how far fertility then falls, which
depends on how much people actually want children once the mechanism is gone. A weak evolved wish for
children, or a strong wish supplied by culture, would put a floor under the decline, and measuring
the wish that remains separates this hypothesis from the account in which people simply came to want
fewer children. On why fertility was ever high the theory says something testable; on the size of the
fall it claims, where a demographic cause has to be strong, it says little.

## 3. How the evidence sorts

Only one of the three groups of studies below reaches the claim the hypothesis is named for.

| Group of studies | What it measures | Role in the chapter |
|---|---|---|
| Status and reproduction | Whether an evolutionary predictor of success, such as wealth or social rank, still predicts the number of children a person has, and how that changes once contraception is available. | The best-populated evidence. It tests whether the ancestral link between status and reproduction survives, which the hypothesis predicts it should not. |
| Desire for children as a distinct drive | Whether the wish for children is a measurable motivation, separate from sexual desire and weaker than a stated family-size target. | Bridge evidence. It bounds the residual demand for children that the whole hypothesis turns on. |
| Direct decoupling | Whether fertility falls because contraception severs sex from reproduction, with the desire for children held fixed. | The distinctive claim. It is the thinnest group and contains almost no identified estimate. |

A paper's topic does not decide which chapter it belongs to; the causal question it answers does. A
study finding that cheaper contraception helped people who had already decided on smaller families
get there measures the distance between the family size people want and the family size they reach,
the question of the modern-contraception chapter (A.2). This chapter keeps a paper only when the
paper speaks to the severing of sex from reproduction itself, or reports fertility falling while the
desire for children stays fixed.

The search started from independently chosen anchor studies covering the three groups and the

## 5. The studies

### 5.1 Status and the number of children

Wealth, social rank, and mate value stop predicting how many children a person has once contraception
is available, and that break is the best-populated finding in the chapter. Completed fertility in the
table is the number of children a woman ended up with by the end of childbearing; reproductive
success is the biologist's term for the same count seen from selection's point of view, the number of
surviving descendants a person leaves.

| Study | Where and when | What was tested as a predictor | Fertility outcome | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kaplan and Hill (1986) | Modern industrial societies | Social class | Reproductive success | Early statement of the puzzle that class stopped predicting reproduction in modern conditions. |
| Boone and Kessler (1999) | Cross-cultural | Social status | Fertility against long-term fitness | Status can trade against the number of children while still serving reproductive success over the long run. |
| Kohler, Rodgers, and Christensen (1999) | Danish twins | Genetic variation | Completed fertility | Once births became voluntary, differences in fertility began to track genetic variation, meaning selection is now acting on fertility itself. |
| Kanazawa (2003) | Contemporary United States | Predictors from evolutionary psychology | Reproductive behavior | Tests, and largely fails to confirm, the prediction that higher status brings more children today. |
| Fieder and Huber (2007) | Population data | Status, allowing for childlessness | Reproductive output | The status-reproduction link is present but sex-specific, running mainly through whether men have any children at all. |
| von Rueden and Jaeggi (2016) | 33 nonindustrial societies | Men's status | Reproductive success | Where contraception is absent, status still predicts reproduction, which fixes the pre-contraception baseline. |

| Hopcroft (2018) | United States | Status, for men and women | Number of children | Asks whether modern fertility is now working against reproductive success; the link survives for men, not women. |

Where contraception is absent, in the nonindustrial societies of von Rueden and Jaeggi (2016) and in
historical populations, higher status still brings more children. Where contraception is present, the
link weakens and splits by sex, surviving for men and vanishing or reversing for women, and it runs
increasingly through whether a person has any children at all rather than through how many. Kohler,
Rodgers, and Christensen (1999) add a second signature from Danish twins: once people control their
own fertility, differences in how many children they have begin to track genetic differences, so
natural selection has re-engaged on fertility.

Both findings fit decoupling and neither identifies it. A weakened link between status and family
size fits equally well a change in what people want that runs along status lines: the women with the
most education and the highest earnings face the steepest career cost per birth, and came to want
smaller families for that reason. That alternative is the subject of D.1, and nothing in these
studies chooses between it and decoupling. They are reported here because the hypothesis predicts the
break, and the rest of the chapter turns on the distinctive claim rather than on them.

### 5.2 Wanting a child as a drive of its own

The hypothesis's psychological premise is that wanting a child is a real motivation, weak, and
separate both from sexual desire and from whatever family size a person says they want. Warren Miller
and coauthors built and validated an instrument that measures childbearing motivation directly,
showed the measure carries some genetic loading, and showed it predicts realized fertility only
imperfectly (Miller and Pasta 1988; Miller 1995; Pasta and Miller 2000). Rotkirch (2007) documents a
felt longing for a child, in men as well as women, as a motivational state in its own right. The
findings cut both ways. A motive aimed at children exists and can be measured, which tells against
the hardest version of the claim that evolution left no desire for children; that same motive is
weaker and less decisive than a simple preference model assumes, which tells for the hypothesis. The
group bounds how much demand for children remains once the by-product mechanism is gone, and
estimates no decline.

### 5.3 Decoupling itself

The group the hypothesis is named for is nearly empty of identified evidence. Kennes and Knowles
(2013) and Burke and Pakaluk (2010) build economic models in which contraception arrives, a sexual
revolution follows, and fertility drops; the models state the mechanism and measure nothing. Léridon
(2006) estimates the demographic effect of introducing the pill in developed countries, Murphy (1993)
splits British fertility change between contraception and employment, and Myers (2017) estimates the
effect of abortion and contraception access on young women's outcomes. All three measure the total
effect of contraceptive access on births, which belongs to A.2, because none of them holds the desire
for children fixed.

Finer and Philbin (2014) come closest without reaching the distinctive claim, documenting sixty years
of American data in which the age of first sex and the age of first birth pull apart. The two ages
could have separated because contraception severed sex from reproduction, or because Americans came
to want their first child later, after more schooling and more time in an early career, and the trend
cannot say which. Eckstein, Keane, and Lifshitz (2019) is the one study coded to the
desire-held-fixed question, and it identifies fertility off the changing structure of careers, so any
fall it measures fits women wanting fewer children as the career cost of a birth rose just as well as
it fits decoupling. Models and proxies test the mechanism the hypothesis is named for; no identified
estimate does.

### 5.4 The theoretical literature

More has been written in evolutionary theory around this hypothesis than exists in its entire
empirical evidence base. The line runs from Darwin (1871) through the mid-century synthesis, taking
in Barkow and Burley (1980) on the demographic transition, Davis (1986) on low fertility as the
evolutionary puzzle, and Turke (1989) on the demand for children, and on into modern evolutionary
demography. That imbalance is the chapter's organizing finding rather than a hole to be filled.

## 6. Putting the numbers together

Only the status-and-children group can be pooled. The desire-for-children studies validate a
measuring instrument and share no fertility outcome. The decoupling studies hold no comparable
identified estimates, since most measure the total contraceptive effect that belongs to A.2 and the
rest are models or a descriptive trend. Reporting the total contraceptive effect as though it were
the decoupling effect would be the confusion this review was built to catch.

Lidborg, Cross, and Boothroyd (2020, 2022) combined dozens of samples on the link between men's
physical size and strength and their reproductive and mating success, and found a small positive
association, on the pre-decoupling side of the prediction. No published meta-analysis has yet
estimated the link between status and children with contraceptive availability entered as the thing
that changes it.

A first pool built for this project reverses sign between men and women. Among men, higher status
predicts more children, at a pooled correlation of about 0.08 (95% confidence interval 0.03 to 0.14,
five studies). Among women, higher status predicts fewer children, at about negative 0.13 (95% CI
negative 0.21 to negative 0.04, three studies). Pooled across both sexes the association is
statistically indistinguishable from zero (95% CI negative 0.06 to 0.07), because the two sexes
cancel. The headline result of the poolable group is the reversal itself.

That estimate tests whether the link breaks and does not identify the decoupling claim. The
prediction that separates this chapter from its neighbors concerns the link among men: strong where
contraception is absent, weaker where it is present. Among men the association runs about 0.19 where
contraception is absent, in von Rueden and Jaeggi's (2016) synthesis of thirty-three nonindustrial
societies, against about 0.07 where contraception is present (95% CI 0.01 to 0.13, four studies). The
association is weak in both settings, the direction matches the prediction, and the
contraception-absent value runs roughly two and a half times the size of the other.

Two things keep that comparison from testing the prediction. The contraception-absent side rests on a
single study, so there is no way to tell whether the gap reflects a real difference or sampling
noise; this review reports rather than pools any group holding fewer than three studies. And the two
sides differ in more than contraception. The contraception-absent estimate comes from nonindustrial
societies and the contraception-present estimates from modern administrative and survey samples,
which differ in mortality, in marriage systems, in subsistence, and in how status gets measured. In a
nonindustrial society a high-status man's larger family may reflect that more of his children
survived infancy and that his rank let him marry more than one wife; neither channel operates in a
modern administrative sample, so the smaller modern association need have nothing to do with
contraception. More studies of populations without contraception would sharpen the 0.19 figure and
would still compare nonindustrial societies against modern ones rather than contraception against its
absence. Identifying the effect of contraceptive availability needs variation in contraceptive access
inside a population otherwise similar to itself, which no study in this evidence base provides.

Read the pooled correlations as summaries of a consistent direction rather than as a precise common
value. Heterogeneity runs above ninety percent by the I-squared statistic, as a pool spanning samples
of a few hundred people alongside administrative datasets of several hundred thousand, and mixing
income, wealth, and education as the status measure, should, and the largest samples dominate the
pooled point, which therefore describes large modern administrative populations more than it
describes the smaller studies.

A second reader read every pooled effect back against the table it came from in the original paper,
and the check mattered. It removed two studies whose reported statistic was not a comparable
correlation: one reported an analysis-of-variance F test rather than a correlation between status and
fertility, and one turned out to be quoting a figure from a different study rather than estimating
its own. The five that remain were each confirmed against the printed table, and the sex reversal
survived.

## 7. How much of the fertility decline this explains

### 7.1 Differences among pre-modern populations

No technology could sever sex from reproduction before the modern era, so the mechanism can produce
no differences among pre-modern populations. Those populations differed in mortality, in marriage
systems, and in subsistence, and decoupling was missing from all of them alike. The hypothesis does
speak to the pre-modern level of fertility: it stood high because sex and reproduction were coupled
and the sex drive carried the load, and the contraception-absent evidence confirms the ancestral link
between status and reproduction that the theory needs as its baseline.

That baseline will not bear much weight. The contraception-absent evidence amounts to a single study,
von Rueden and Jaeggi's (2016) synthesis of thirty-three nonindustrial societies, which can establish
the association inside those populations and not variation across pre-modern ones. The study also
lands in the pre-modern group by fertility regime rather than by date, aggregating twentieth-century
fieldwork in societies that had not yet passed through the transition rather than observations of
populations before 1870. Reading those societies as pre-transitional is a defensible judgement and
remains a judgement rather than a measurement, which the target-period table flags. The pre-modern
verdict is insufficient for variation: the mechanism supplies a background reason fertility stood
high and no source of the differences among pre-modern populations.

### 7.2 The First Demographic Transition

Every study in the quantitative pool was dated to the years it observes. Four carry a datable window,
and not one of the four observes fertility inside the first transition, which this review places at
roughly 1870 to 1965. The earliest observation window in the entire pool opens in 1979, fourteen
years after the transition closed. Numbers gathered entirely after a decline has ended cannot
identify its causes, so this review credits the mechanism with no part of that decline.

Western fertility fell mainly through withdrawal, abstinence, and stopping childbearing early. Each
of those lowers births by bringing behavior into line with a wish for children that had already
shrunk, which makes them the mechanisms of A.2 and D.1 rather than of decoupling. The technology that
severs sex from reproduction across a whole population shows up only at the very end of the window,
and this chapter's own decoupling studies include nothing from the transition period. A channel that
exists only in models cannot generate a large fall in completed fertility that mostly predates the
severing technology. The verdict for the first transition is not significant, and the mechanism is
present but too late and unidentified in this window.

### 7.3 The Second Demographic Transition

The pill severs sex from reproduction at scale after 1965, as the theory requires, and the
status-and-children evidence shows the matching signatures: across rich post-1965 populations the
link between status and family size weakens and splits by sex, and fertility becomes heritable as it
becomes voluntary. Those signatures support the relationship the hypothesis describes. They do not
measure how large a decline decoupling caused, as against a genuine change in how many children
people want, which would need the fall recovered with the desire for children held fixed.

The break that has been measured is, on its own terms, demographically small. All four studies of
contraception-using populations observe fertility entirely after 1965, so they sit squarely inside
the phenomenon. Status accounts for under one percent of the variance in completed fertility among
men and under two percent among women. The opposing signs matter more than either figure.
Higher-status men have slightly more children and higher-status women slightly fewer, so in a
population with roughly equal numbers of each the two effects offset, and the association across both
sexes is statistically indistinguishable from zero. A gradient that vanishes in aggregate cannot by itself move aggregate
fertility; moving it would require the distribution of status to shift in a way that weighted the
sexes differently, and no study in this evidence base estimates that. Converting these correlations
into numbers of births would require the standard deviation of the fertility outcome in each study,
which the extraction does not hold, so no share of the decline is assigned here rather than estimated
loosely.

Decoupling may well have contributed something after 1965, and no study measures how much. Two other
chapters can produce the same decline without it: A.2 explains it as contraception closing a gap that
opened once people came to want fewer children, and D.1 explains it as the wish for children
shrinking on its own. Decoupling adds one thing to those accounts, the claim that fertility would
have fallen even with the desire for children held fixed, and no study identifies that claim. Net of
the contraception and preference channels, the share of the SDT decline that belongs to decoupling is
not presently estimable and may be small. The break the hypothesis predicts shows up in the data,
rests on many studies, and differs between men and women, and measured across a whole population it
is too small to carry the decline by itself. This review assigns the distinctive claim no share of
the transition rather than a small one, because assigning a share would mean scoring an estimate that
does not exist.

## 8. Where the findings travel

The status-and-children studies fall into a nonindustrial and historical group with little or no
contraception and a rich group with contraception everywhere, and comparing the two groups is the
test, so that split is a strength rather than a nuisance.

The limit falls on the distinctive claim. Severing is sharpest in rich countries after 1965, where
the pill is widespread and the desire for children is already low, and those same conditions make
decoupling hardest to tell apart from a genuine fall in the desire for children. Contraception is
largely absent from the nonindustrial societies in which the status-to-children baseline is clean, so
the decoupling claim admits of no test there. The settings that pin down the baseline and the
settings that would pin down the decoupling effect are different places.

## 9. Weaknesses in the studies

The status-and-children studies are observational associations between a status measure and completed
fertility, drawn from population samples and historical records, so confounding is the chief risk:
whatever raises a person's status also travels with education, location, and preferences that move
fertility on their own. Comparing contraceptive societies against non-contraceptive ones adds the
risk that societies of the two kinds differ along many dimensions besides contraception.

Measurement is the risk in the desire-for-children studies, which are validation studies for an
instrument: a scale that predicts fertility imperfectly may be picking up stated attitudes rather
than a stable underlying drive. The formal models in the decoupling group have internal validity and
no empirical anchoring, their conclusions following from their assumptions and nothing tying those
assumptions to measured data. The empirical papers there do identify a real effect of contraceptive
access, on the total number of births rather than on the decoupling channel with the desire for
children held fixed, which makes them credible evidence for another chapter's question.

The five studies in the quantitative pool were assessed across the seven standard domains for
observational work, from confounding and who ends up in the sample through how the outcome is
measured and whether the design can separate cause from correlation. Confounding dominates in all
five, since the traits that raise status also raise education and shift the preferences that move
fertility on their own. Four of the five are rated serious overall on that last domain, because
nobody assigns a person a social rank and the correlations these papers report are either unadjusted
or adjusted only lightly. von Rueden and Jaeggi's meta-analysis is rated moderate instead: its
authors restricted the sample to nonindustrial societies, and that choice holds down the contraception
confound in the one comparison this chapter most needs. Fieder and colleagues carry an extra serious
risk from who is in the sample, since a single employer's university workforce is not a general
population. The pool is strong evidence that the association between status and children exists and
differs by sex, and weak evidence that contraception caused its modern weakening.

## 11. Reading the hypothesis as an economist

An economist can read this hypothesis as a claim about the thing people were ever choosing in the
first place. The standard model treats children as a good that parents demand, and explains the
fertility decline as a fall in that demand or a fall in its price. This hypothesis says the demand
was largely indirect. People chose sex, children followed, and part of the demand for children that
shows up in the data was really a demand for something else casting a shadow. Once contraception let
people have the sex without the children, that shadow demand disappeared.

The distinction has a testable edge the evidence cannot yet reach. If the demand for children was
real and direct, cheap contraception should leave completed fertility close to the number people say
they want. If the demand was largely a by-product of the sex drive, cheap contraception should push
completed fertility below the number people say they want, because the mechanism that used to
overshoot their stated preference is gone. The discriminating test is the gap between wanted and
realized fertility, and specifically its sign: realized below wanted would favor this hypothesis,
realized at wanted would favor the account in which people simply came to want fewer children. No
study in the evidence base reports that gap in a form that settles the question, which is how the
chapter can rate the hypothesis plausible and still leave its distinctive contribution unmeasured.

## 12. Limits and what would settle the question

The chapter's main limit is built into the hypothesis rather than into the search: no design in the
evidence base builds the counterfactual the distinctive claim is about, the path fertility would have
taken had the desire for children stayed fixed while contraception spread. Every credible study here
measures one of two other things, whether the link between status and children survives, which is a
consistency check, or the total effect of contraceptive access, which belongs to A.2.

None of the three studies that would move the chapter exists. The first would estimate the fertility
decline while a documented and stable desire for children is held fixed across a contraception
rollout, and would report whether realized fertility fell below the stated target. The second would
link a validated childbearing-motivation measure to completed fertility inside a fully contracepting
group, to put a number on how much demand for children remains. The third would compare fertility
across differing contraceptive access within a population otherwise similar to itself, the design
Section 6 identifies as the missing one.

Some full texts could not be obtained, so this chapter runs on a thinner set of studies than the
screen produced. Retrieving more studies of populations without contraception stays worth doing,
because it would put a defensible interval on the contraception-absent side, and it is the nearest of
these improvements to hand. That retrieval would not identify contraceptive availability as the
source of the weakening, and it changes no verdict in this chapter. Of the three designs above, only
the first would rescue the distinctive claim.

## 13. How the review was built

The chapter uses the project's Gold-Anchored Clustered Search workflow. Twenty independently chosen
anchor studies produced a citation-based candidate pool of 4,900 records once duplicates were
removed. Each record was screened blind on title and abstract into the three groups of evidence, into
a neighboring chapter, or out. The screen produced 152 relevant records, 311 uncertain, and 4,437 not
relevant, and an empirical pooling set of 95 distinct studies alongside a separate reading list of
173 theoretical works. A retrieval-overlap test confirmed that the five search families reach
different papers rather than the same ones.

For the studies retrieved to date the work is complete: full-text retrieval, structured effect
extraction, a second-reader check of every pooled effect, risk-of-bias coding, the meta-analysis of
Section 6, and the demographic-significance and target-period pass.

Each pooled study's observation window was read out of its own full text rather than recalled, and is
recorded with the quoted source sentence in the target-period table, so a reader can check the timing
argument in Section 7.2 rather than take it on trust. The pass would normally cross-check each window
against total fertility measured in the same years, but the shared classification module needs a
United Nations fertility series held in a sibling repository not present on this machine, so the
cross-check did not run. Window classification here rests on study dates alone, and the script
records the cross-check as unavailable rather than passing over it in silence. Re-running the pass
where that data is available would add the check and would not change any window in the current
table, all of which are unambiguous.
