## 1. The claim

The hypothesis names a three-link chain, and every link has to hold. Compulsory attendance takes
hours away from children's work; the lost hours lower what a child is worth to their parents; and
parents, facing a lower return on an additional child, have fewer children. The outcome at issue is
completed fertility. A reform that only pushes teenage births to later ages leaves the claim
untouched.

Most quasi-experimental compulsory-schooling studies estimate the effect of the law on the wrong
generation. The reforms they exploit bind on a woman while she is a child, and what those reforms
change is her own years of schooling; the hypothesis needs a law that binds on her children, and
what it must change is the work those children would otherwise have done. An estimate of the first
kind identifies the reduced-form effect of a schooling law on the woman's own fertility. It does not
identify the expected work value of her future children. We call this the **wrong-generation
problem**, and it governs how we read every study below.

## 2. Theoretical mechanism

The formal literature agrees that child labor raises fertility and disagrees about what a
restriction on child labor does to it. In any model where children contribute labor or household
production, an additional child carries a return as well as a cost, and a binding attendance
requirement cuts into the return. Parents who can no longer put children to work may substitute
away from child quantity and toward schooling and other investments per child.

Hazan and Berdugo give the mechanism its most direct formal statement. Child labor raises fertility
in their development-trap model, and a temporary prohibition, or a compulsory-schooling
intervention, can accelerate an economy's movement toward a steady state with low child labor and
low fertility. Mookherjee, Prina, and Ray reach the same conclusion through a different model, showing
how stronger child-labor regulation moves education, occupational composition, and steady-state
fertility together. Fioroni derives the same sign from the cost side: child work lowers the cost of
a child and therefore raises fertility.

Takakura shows that the aggregate sign need not be negative at all. Banning child labor lowers
fertility among unskilled parents in his model and raises it among skilled parents, so the
population-level effect turns on which parents the ban binds. His result is the sharpest piece of
theoretical counterevidence in the set, and it is a warning about interpreting any single pooled
estimate as the effect the models predict.

Emerson and Knabb add an expectations channel, in which a temporary child-labor ban combined with
mandatory education moves households out of a high-fertility, incomplete-education equilibrium and
into a persistent low-fertility one. Baland and Robinson are more cautious. Child labor can be
inefficient in their setting and a ban welfare-improving, and the fertility effect remains
theoretically ambiguous. Cigno and Rosati's policy synthesis reaches a similar conclusion: compulsory
education or a partial work restriction can establish a self-enforcing higher-education
equilibrium, while a total ban may require complementary insurance and education policy alongside
it.

Greenwood, Seshadri, and Vandenbroucke formalize a closely related child-productivity mechanism and
then take the policy reading of it away. In their historical account, technological change and
rising skill demand explain the decline of child labor, and effective child-labor regulation
arrived after much of the withdrawal from work had already occurred. On that history,
compulsory-schooling and child-labor laws may be consequences or complements of the economic
transformation rather than its initiating cause. Their challenge is to the policy interpretation of
the mechanism rather than to the mechanism itself, and it bears directly on how much weight the
reduced-form estimates in §5 can carry.

## 3. Search and routing

This chapter rests on a GACS cold-start citation-frame audit rather than on the protocol's ordinary
keyword-search PRISMA flow, and its counts must not be presented as a conventional
database-keyword search. The audit identified and deduplicated 1,255 citation-frame records. A
blinded AI screen classified 233 as Relevant, 128 as Uncertain, and 894 as Not relevant. The
focused exception sheet contained 42 records, the strict policy/outcome gate retained 28 retrieval
candidates before publication versions were collapsed, and Alexandra's strict focused RA review,
later divided into mechanism-specific sets, produced a final routing of 16 distinct child-value
papers: 10 theory or mechanism papers and six empirical quantum papers. Shanan was added afterward
as a transparently labeled primary-study supplement.

None of the six empirical papers was routed as direct child-value evidence. Each entered the set
because it reports completed or desired fertility under a compulsory-schooling reform. Alexandra's
completed mechanism review classifies none of these reduced-form papers as direct child-value
evidence: Cummins routes to decreased teenage pregnancy; Fort, Chen-Guo, and Kan-Lee are not
explicit; and Lei et al. belongs to neither mechanism. Membership in the set preserves contextual
relevance and establishes nothing about the child-value mechanism.

## 5. Empirical evidence and the wrong-generation problem

All six approved empirical studies change the woman's own schooling, and none of them changes what
her children could have earned. Their estimates are informative about compulsory schooling and
uninformative about the mechanism this chapter is testing.

Fort, Schneeweis, and Winter-Ebmer pool compulsory-schooling reforms across England and Continental
Europe and recover the effect only in England. Additional schooling is associated with lower
completed fertility there; on the Continent the reforms neither reduce biological children nor
raise childlessness. The treatment is adults' own schooling in both settings, so children's
productive value is never isolated.

Geruso and Royer exploit the 1972 British school-leaving reform and find an effect that is entirely
about timing. The reform reduces births at ages 16 and 17, the fertility effects disappear after
the teenage years, and completed family size by age 45 is precisely unchanged. Their own mechanism
evidence points to conceptions prevented while girls are required to attend school rather than to
parents placing a lower value on future children. Section 1 already put timing outside the claim.

Cummins studies the 1947 and 1972 English reforms with linked historical registration records and
estimates effects on first-marriage fertility that are effectively zero; the analysis rules out a
substantial negative causal effect. The variation is once again the woman's own schooling rather
than the labor value of her future children.

Kan and Lee exploit Taiwan's 1968 extension of compulsory schooling using exact dates of birth and
whole-population censuses, and their regression-discontinuity estimates show that the reform raised
women's education without changing fertility, completed fertility included. Chen and Guo do find an
effect in China: an additional year of reform-induced female education reduces total live births by
0.238 (SE 0.059), with no significant childlessness effect. Their channel analysis credits delayed
marriage and birth, women's opportunity costs, assortative mating, and child mortality, and it does
not credit the work value of children. Lei and coauthors find that each year of exposure to China's
law reduces women's desired family size by 0.009 child, do not measure completed fertility, and
locate the mechanism in women's opportunity cost and intrahousehold contraceptive bargaining.

Shanan (2023) is the one study whose variation binds on the generation the hypothesis is about, and
it is the closest primary evidence to the claimed mechanism. Cigno and Rosati's evidence chapter
describes combined schooling and child-work restrictions as explaining about 10% of the observed US
fertility decline; the underlying primary study is Shanan, and Shanan's own back-of-envelope
estimate is 8%, not 10%. Exposure to any restriction at ages 20--30 predicts a 0.85 percentage-point
reduction in the annual probability of a birth (SE 0.11 percentage points) against a baseline of
14.3% (Table 7, column 2), and a one-year increase in the average school exit age predicts a 0.27
percentage-point reduction (SE 0.03; Table 8, column 2). The first stages sit in the same paper:
lower child employment and higher school attendance (Table 2). So do the completed-fertility
reduced forms. Exposure at ages 20--30 predicts 0.301 fewer children (SE 0.044; Table 4, column 2),
and a one-year higher exit age predicts 0.108 fewer children (SE 0.024; Table 5, column 2).

Taken together, the approved empirical studies neither test the original mechanism nor deliver a
consistent negative reduced-form effect on completed fertility. Shanan narrows the direct-evidence
gap materially, since policy exposure there predicts less child employment, greater attendance, and
lower completed fertility within one design. What is left is mechanism isolation. Shanan's
treatment bundles compulsory schooling with child-labor restrictions, so the estimate cannot
disentangle lost child earnings from higher expected child quality, and the study does not formally
estimate either mediated effect.

## 6. Quantitative synthesis

No mechanism-specific pooled estimate is justified, and we report none. Shanan is the only study
with a verified child-work first stage and a completed-fertility estimate, and the remaining
studies differ in country, reform, outcome, and estimand. Pooling own-schooling coefficients across
them would produce a number, and the number would answer a different question.

## 7. Demographic significance

Expanded restriction exposure accounts for 8% of the 1900--1930 decline in the annual birth
probability among 30-year-old native-born women, which falls below the protocol's 10%
demographic-significance threshold and supports a Partial verdict rather than a threshold-significant
one. Shanan's arithmetic is explicit: exposure rose by 48 percentage points, the estimated annual
effect is 0.85 percentage points, and the observed birth probability fell by five percentage
points, giving `0.48 * 0.85 / 5 = 0.08`.

We treat that 8% as a fragile number. The calculation is age-specific and back-of-envelope, and it
carries no bootstrap uncertainty interval. Propagating only Shanan's reported coefficient standard
error, while holding exposure and the five-point historical denominator fixed, yields an analytic
95% interval of 6.1% to 10.2%. The point estimate therefore remains below threshold while its upper
sampling bound narrowly exceeds it. This is a sensitivity calculation and not the protocol-preferred
bootstrap interval.

## 8. Provisional GRADE assessment

**FDT causal mechanism: Low.** The renewed three-rater panel was unanimous. Shanan supplies one
credible historical quasi-experiment connecting restrictions, child work and attendance, and
completed fertility. The rating does not reach Moderate because the full mechanism has no
independent replication, because policy adoption may retain residual endogeneity, and because lost
child economic value is inferred rather than directly measured as a mediator. Both this rating and
the verdict below are provisional.

## 9. Provisional verdict

| Phenomenon | Causal credibility | Demographic significance |
|---|---|---|
| FDT | Low | Partial; 8% decomposition is below the 10% threshold |

The expanded evidence supports a historically relevant relationship running from policy to child
work to completed fertility. How much of that relationship operates specifically through lost child
earnings, as against increased child quality or the other effects bundled into a schooling law,
remains uncertain on the evidence in hand.

## 10. Required next analysis

The decisive next study would do two things: replicate Shanan independently, and mediate the
fertility effect formally through reduced child earnings or another measure of children's economic
contribution. A population-wide TFR or completed-fertility decomposition reported with uncertainty
would improve the scaling in §7. Any replication will also have to confront Greenwood, Seshadri,
and Vandenbroucke, since a design identified only off the timing of legal adoption cannot separate
the law from the skill-demand shift that preceded it. No approved or supplementary full texts
remain missing, so the binding constraint on this chapter is new analysis rather than retrieval.
