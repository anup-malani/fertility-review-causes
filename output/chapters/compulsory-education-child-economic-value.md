# Compulsory Education and the Economic Value of Children

**Category:** Economic
**Primary mechanism:** Compulsory education makes child work harder, reducing the economic return parents expect from an additional child and thereby reducing completed fertility.

## 1. The claim

This chapter explores the effect of compulsory education on completed fertility, operating through the lost economic value of children's work.

### 1.1 In plain terms

In plain terms: in the past, children worked. They earned money, helped on the farm, minded younger children. A child was partly a source of income, and having more children meant having more hands. The claim is that when governments forced children into school and out of work, children stopped paying their way — and parents, no longer getting that return, had fewer of them.

The important thing about this claim is *whose* schooling matters. It is the children's. The argument is about what a child is worth to a parent, so the law has to change what children do. Most of the research that looks like it tests this claim actually tests something quite different: it follows girls who were made to stay at school longer, and asks how many children *they* went on to have. That is a claim about the mother's own education, not about whether her future children would have been useful. Both are interesting. Only one is this chapter's.

That confusion is not a detail. It is the reason a literature that looks well populated turns out to contain almost nothing that tests the claim.

### 1.2 The claim precisely

The parameter this chapter estimates is the change in completed fertility, in births per woman, caused by a policy-induced reduction in the expected labour contribution of a child, signed so that a negative value means fewer births.

The chapter is scoped to the **FDT** (the First Demographic Transition, roughly 1870–1965), the era in which child labour was common and was legislated against. **PM** (pre-modern fertility variation, before roughly 1870) and **SDT** (the Second Demographic Transition, roughly 1965 onward) have no cell: before the transition there were no such laws, and after it child labour in rich countries was already negligible.

**This is a behavioural parameter, and it is a mediated one — which is what makes it hard.** The claim is not "the law reduced fertility". It is a three-link chain:

> compulsory schooling → less child work → lower expected return from a child → fewer children

The first link is a first stage. The second is the mechanism proper. The third is the behavioural response. A study establishing only the first and last links has estimated a reduced form, not this chapter's parameter, and cannot distinguish the child-as-asset story from any other reason schooling laws might move fertility.

**The counterfactual** is a ceteris-paribus perturbation: hold the mother's own schooling, household income and child mortality fixed, and reduce the labour a child may legally perform. No study delivers this, because every compulsory-schooling law changes the mother's schooling too.

**Margin.** The mechanism predicts an intensive-margin effect — fewer children among those having children — because it changes the return to an *additional* child. Childlessness should move little, and §6 notes that where childlessness is reported it does not move.

**Theory does not sign this parameter.** Hazan and Berdugo, Mookherjee, Prina and Ray, Fioroni, and Emerson and Knabb all generate a negative sign. Baland and Robinson show a child-labour ban's fertility effect is theoretically ambiguous. Takakura derives heterogeneity with opposite signs — banning child labour lowers fertility among unskilled parents and raises it among skilled ones. A chapter reporting a negative estimate is therefore not confirming a theoretical prediction; it is choosing among competing ones.

## 2. Theoretical mechanism

In the reader's own vocabulary: this is the quantity–quality model with the child's own output on the return side. A working child is partly an asset yielding a stream of household income; a binding attendance requirement removes that stream and simultaneously raises required spending per child. Both push toward fewer, more-educated children — but through *different* channels, and the chapter's claim is specifically about the first.

That the two arrive together is the identification problem in one sentence. A law that mandates schooling raises quality spending and removes child earnings at the same moment, and no included study separates them.

**A serious challenge from within the theory stream.** Greenwood, Seshadri and Vandenbroucke formalise the child-productivity mechanism and then undercut the policy reading of it: in their historical account, technological change and rising skill demand explain the decline of child labour, and effective regulation arrived *after* much of the withdrawal from work had already happened. On that account compulsory-schooling laws are consequences or complements of the economic transformation rather than its cause — which would make every reduced-form estimate in §6 an artefact of timing. This is the strongest objection to the hypothesis and it comes from a paper in the chapter's own theory set.

**What would make the hypothesis wrong.** It is wrong if laws that verifiably reduce child work leave completed fertility unchanged; if the fertility response is present but unmediated by lost child earnings; or if, as Greenwood and coauthors argue, the laws followed the withdrawal from work rather than causing it.

## 5. The ideal design

Written before the literature was read, so that §6 can be compared against a fixed yardstick rather than against the best paper that happens to exist.

### 5.1 The ideal estimand

The change in completed fertility, in births per woman, **attributable to the reduction in a child's expected lifetime labour contribution to the household**, among parents of childbearing age exposed to a child-labour restriction, holding the mother's own schooling fixed.

The mediating clause is the specification. Without it the estimand is a reduced form of a law on fertility, which several studies already deliver and which cannot distinguish this hypothesis from the mother's-own-schooling account, the quality-investment account, or Greenwood's reverse-timing account.

### 5.2 The design that would identify it

**Source of variation.** A restriction that binds on **children's work** while leaving the mother's own schooling unchanged. Historically these exist: minimum working-age laws, factory-hours acts and work-permit requirements were often legislated separately from school-leaving ages, and the staggered US state adoptions of the early twentieth century give variation in one without the other.

**Comparison group.** Households in states or districts not yet covered, with adoption timing instrumented or shown to be unrelated to prior fertility trends — the Greenwood objection made testable.

**Identifying assumption.** Adoption is uncorrelated with the local fertility trajectory conditional on controls. Falsifiable: event-study pre-trends in fertility *and* in child employment; and a placebo on households with no working-age children.

**Estimating equation.** A staggered event study on completed fertility, with a **measured mediator**: child labour-force participation or child earnings, entered so that the share of the fertility effect running through it can be estimated rather than asserted. A design reporting the reduced form alone does not identify §5.1.

**Data required.** Household-level census or registration data with **child employment and completed fertility observed for the same households**, over a period spanning the adoptions. This is the binding constraint: most historical sources give one or the other.

**Sample size.** Detecting 0.1 births per woman needs roughly 2,650 per arm; the effects here are larger, so full-count census extracts are ample. Data linkage, not power, is the constraint.

**What the ideal design excludes.** Instrumenting the *mother's* years of schooling with a school-leaving reform. That is the tempo chapter's design and it identifies a different parameter.

### 5.3 Distance from the ideal

| Study | Treatment binds on child work? | Outcome: completed fertility? | Mediator measured? | Mother's schooling held fixed? | Distance |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Shanan 2023** | **Yes** — combined schooling and child-labour restrictions | **Yes** — children ever born | **Partly** — child employment and attendance first stages verified | **No** — bundled | **Closest by a wide margin** |
| Fort, Schneeweis and Winter-Ebmer 2016 | No — school-leaving age | Yes — biological children, childlessness | No | No | Far |
| Kan and Lee 2017 | No | Yes | No | No | Far |
| Cummins 2025 | No | Yes — marital fertility, 1.5M linked women | No | No | Far |
| Chen and Guo 2022 | No | Yes — total live births | No | No | Far |
| Lei et al. 2025 | No | **No** — *desired* family size | No | No | Far |
| Geruso and Royer 2018 | No | Yes — completed fertility at 45 | No | No | Far; belongs to the tempo chapter |

**One study approaches the ideal design, and it entered through the back door.** Shanan (2023) is the only paper in the chapter that verifies a child-work first stage and a completed-fertility reduced form **in the same study and the same population**. It is also the only one whose treatment binds on children's work rather than on the mother's schooling. Everything else in the empirical set estimates the tempo chapter's parameter with a quantum outcome attached.

**No study measures the mediator.** Shanan verifies that child employment fell and attendance rose, and separately that fertility fell — but does not estimate what share of the fertility response runs through lost child earnings as against higher expected child quality. The mechanism is *corroborated* and not *identified*, and the difference is the whole distance between §5.1 and what exists.

**The Greenwood objection is untested by every study here.** None runs the pre-trend test in §5.2 that would establish whether the laws preceded or followed the withdrawal of children from work. The strongest challenge to the hypothesis has never been confronted with the design that could answer it.

## 6. Included studies

| Study | Setting | Design | Outcome | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Shanan 2023** | US, early 20th century | Staggered exposure to schooling + child-labour restrictions | Children ever born; annual birth probability | **−0.301 children** (SE 0.044) for exposure at 20–30; **−0.108** (SE 0.024) per year of higher school exit age; **−0.85 pp** annual birth probability (SE 0.11, baseline 14.3%) |
| Fort, Schneeweis and Winter-Ebmer 2016 | England + 7 Continental countries, 1936–1975 | RD / IV across reforms | Biological children; childlessness | **Sign reverses by region**: negative in England, positive on the Continent |
| Kan and Lee 2017 | Taiwan, 1968 | RD on exact birth date, whole-population census | Number of children | Schooling rose; **fertility unchanged**. A coarser running variable had produced a false discontinuity in earlier work |
| Cummins 2025 | England, 1947 and 1972 | RD on 1.5M linked women | Marital fertility | **Effectively zero**; rules out a substantial negative effect |
| Chen and Guo 2022 | China | Quasi-experimental | Total live births | −0.238 (SE 0.059) per year of female education; no childlessness effect; channels are delayed marriage, opportunity cost, assortative mating, child mortality |
| Lei et al. 2025 | China | Quasi-experimental | **Desired** family size | −0.009 per exposure year; mechanism is opportunity cost and contraceptive bargaining |
| Geruso and Royer 2018 | UK, 1972 | RD | Completed fertility at 45 | **Precisely zero** |

### 6.1 The naive estimator and the direction of its bias

**What is the naive estimator here?** Compare completed fertility across places or cohorts with more and less compulsory schooling.

It is confounded by the entire economic transformation. Compulsory-schooling laws were adopted by industrialising, urbanising, richer polities with falling child mortality — every one of which independently lowers fertility. The bias runs **toward the hypothesis**, and there is no offsetting force.

Greenwood, Seshadri and Vandenbroucke sharpen this from confounding into **reverse timing**: if technological change pulled children out of work first and legislation ratified it afterwards, the naive comparison attributes to law what the economy had already done. That is not a bias to be signed; it is the possibility that the causal arrow points the other way.

**How many included studies use the naive estimator? None** — all seven have a design. **But none tests the reverse-timing objection either**, which requires the child-employment pre-trend in §5.2. The literature has protected itself against the confounding it recognised and not against the objection that its own theory stream raises.

### 6.2 Do the studies disagree, and about what?

They disagree about the reduced form, and the disagreement does not resolve into an estimator story.

Three studies find no effect on completed fertility (Kan and Lee; Cummins; Geruso and Royer), two find a negative effect (Shanan; Chen and Guo), one finds **opposite signs in different regions of the same study** (Fort et al.: negative in England, positive on the Continent), and one measures desired rather than actual fertility (Lei et al.).

**The right resolution is not to average or to adjudicate, but to observe that six of the seven are estimating a different parameter from the one this chapter registered.** They vary the mother's schooling; the hypothesis is about the child's work. Their disagreement is informative about *that* parameter — and Fort et al.'s sign reversal suggests it is not stable even there — but it is not evidence for or against the child-value mechanism.

Once the six are set aside on W3, the chapter's evidence on its own claim consists of **one study**, and there is nothing left to disagree.

### 6.3 The transmission ledger

| Stage | Question | Sign |
|---|---|---|
| Law enacted → binds on child work | Did the restriction actually reduce child employment? | **Verified once** (Shanan, first stage) |
| Child work falls → expected return from a child falls | Is the lost work worth enough to matter to a fertility decision? | **Never measured** |
| Return falls → fewer children | Or is the response to higher required quality spending, arriving simultaneously? | **Not separated** |
| Fewer births → the phenomenon | Was the law's spread large enough over the transition? | Attenuates: exposure rose 48 points, not 100 |
| — | And did the law precede the withdrawal from work, or follow it? | **Possibly reverses** (Greenwood) |

The second row is the chapter's binding gap: the mechanism's central quantity has never been measured. The last is its most serious risk: if the laws followed the economy, the ledger's first row is not a first stage at all.

## 7. Quantitative synthesis

### 7.1 The answer in plain terms

There is essentially one study, and it says the effect is real but small.

The others look relevant and are not. They follow girls who had to stay at school longer and count the children they had. That answers a different question, and even on its own terms the answers do not agree — no effect in Taiwan, none in England, none in Britain, a reduction in China, and, in one study covering several countries at once, a reduction in England alongside an *increase* on the Continent.

The one study that looks at what this chapter is actually about follows Americans in the early twentieth century, when laws restricted both schooling and children's work together. Children did work less and go to school more, and the families were smaller by about a third of a child. What it cannot tell us is *why* — whether parents had fewer children because a child no longer earned, or because a child now had to be educated, which became expensive at the same moment. Nobody has separated those two.

### 7.2 The estimate

**No pooled estimate is justified, and the reason is W3 rather than heterogeneity.** Pooling would combine one study of the registered mechanism with six of a different one. The remaining studies also differ in country, reform, outcome and estimand; pooling own-schooling coefficients would answer a question this chapter did not ask.

**The estimate is Shanan's: −0.301 children ever born** (SE 0.044) for exposure to any restriction at ages 20–30, and −0.108 (SE 0.024) per additional year of average school exit age. The annual-birth-probability analogue is −0.85 percentage points (SE 0.11) against a 14.3% baseline.

These are precisely estimated and they are one study. §9 rates the cell LOW on exactly that ground.

## 8. Demographic significance

The phenomenon to be explained is measured in whole children — the FDT is a fall of roughly three births per woman; this mechanism offers, on its single available estimate and at the exposure actually achieved, about 0.14 births per woman.

That figure is Shanan's coefficient scaled by the exposure change she documents: restriction exposure rose 48 percentage points, and 0.48 × 0.301 = **0.144 births per woman**. Everything below follows from it.

**Two denominators are in play, and they give different verdicts.** Against the full FDT decline of roughly three births, 0.144 is **4.8%**. Against the 1900–1930 window Shanan actually studies — a US completed-fertility fall on the order of 1.4 births — it is about **10%**. The chapter states the verdict on the full-transition denominator because that is the phenomenon the review is explaining, and flags the alternative.

Stated as break-evens, which require no external panel: 0.144 births reaches MINOR (≥5%) only if the phenomenon is at most **2.88 births**, and SUBSTANTIAL (≥20%) only if at most **0.72**.

**Two demographic-significance schemes are in play in this project and they disagree here.** The protocol's 10% threshold and the template's fixed bands (NEGLIGIBLE <5%, MINOR 5–20%, SUBSTANTIAL 20–50%, DOMINANT >50%) are different instruments. Shanan's own 8% figure is *below threshold* under the protocol and squarely *MINOR* under the template. The previous draft reported "Partial; 8% below the 10% threshold", which is not a value in either vocabulary. This needs a ruling (PI call 1).

**The endogeneity check is this chapter's central risk, not a formality.** Greenwood, Seshadri and Vandenbroucke argue that compulsory-schooling and child-labour laws arrived *after* much of the withdrawal from child work had occurred, making the laws a consequence of the economic transformation. If so, the mechanism's own movement is partly caused by the transition it is invoked to explain, and the 0.144 is an overstatement of unknown size. No included study tests this.

### 8.1 Pre-modern fertility variation

For pre-modern variation, the verdict is NOT ASSESSED, because the phenomenon is out of scope for this hypothesis in the registry rather than an empty in-scope cell — compulsory-schooling and child-labour laws did not exist.

The underlying economic relation — that children's productive value supports high fertility — is in scope elsewhere, as the agricultural mode-of-production hypothesis (TICK-030). Were it assessed here, the expected sign would be zero for want of a treatment.

### 8.2 First Demographic Transition

For the First Demographic Transition, the verdict is NEGLIGIBLE, because the mechanism's single available estimate, scaled by the exposure actually achieved, accounts for 0.144 of a roughly three-birth decline, or 4.8%.

Three things qualify that, in both directions.

It sits on the NEGLIGIBLE/MINOR boundary and moves across it on the choice of denominator: 4.8% against the whole transition, about 10% against Shanan's own 1900–1930 window. Propagating only her coefficient's standard error, and holding exposure and the historical denominator fixed, her 8% period-fertility figure carries an analytic 95% interval of **6.1% to 10.2%** — a sensitivity calculation, not the protocol-preferred bootstrap.

It rests on one study of one country in one thirty-year window, whose mechanism is corroborated rather than identified.

And it is an upper bound if Greenwood and coauthors are right about timing.

### 8.3 Second Demographic Transition

For the Second Demographic Transition, the verdict is NOT ASSESSED, because the phenomenon is out of scope in the registry: child labour in rich countries was already negligible before the SDT began, so the mechanism has no dose left to apply.

## 10. Verdict

Compulsory schooling and child-labour restrictions did reduce completed fertility in the early twentieth-century United States, by about a third of a child among those exposed. Whether they did so *because children stopped earning* — which is what this hypothesis claims — has never been established.

**The one number to carry away: 0.144 births per woman**, or **4.8%** of the First Demographic Transition. That is Shanan's estimate scaled by the exposure actually achieved, and it is the mechanism's entire documented contribution.

Three qualifications belong inside this verdict.

The evidence base is one study. Six further studies were approved, retrieved and read, and Alexandra's mechanism review classifies none of them as direct child-value evidence: they vary the mother's schooling, not the child's work. On the registered claim they are silent, and their reduced forms do not even agree with one another — three nulls, two negatives, and one study finding opposite signs in England and on the Continent.

The mechanism is corroborated, not identified. Shanan verifies that child employment fell, that attendance rose, and that fertility fell, in one population — but no study measures what share of the fertility response runs through lost child earnings rather than through the higher spending per child that the same law required at the same moment.

And the chapter's own theory stream contains the strongest objection to it. Greenwood, Seshadri and Vandenbroucke argue the laws followed the withdrawal of children from work rather than causing it. If that is right, the causal arrow is reversed and 0.144 is an overstatement. No included study runs the pre-trend test that would settle it.

**What would change it:** a staggered event study on a restriction binding on children's work but not on mothers' schooling, with child employment entered as a measured mediator, and a pre-trend test on child employment. All three components are feasible in US full-count census data, and no study combines them.
