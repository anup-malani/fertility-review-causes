# Old-Age Security and Pension Crowd-Out

> **Bottom line.** The hypothesis this chapter examines is the **old-age-security hypothesis**, and
> it claims two opposite things at once. A pension is a non-child source of old-age support, so it
> makes a child less necessary and fertility falls. The same pension retires a grandmother, whose
> time lowers what a child costs her adult daughter, so fertility rises.
>
> The evidence base is thirteen studies with extracted estimates and risk-of-bias ratings: nine on the
> retirement-asset channel, three on the grandparent-time channel, and one mechanism study whose
> outcome is insurance purchase rather than fertility. A fourteenth paper, a working draft from Ghana,
> is carried provisionally without an extraction or a rating. Seven of the thirteen are rated moderate
> risk of bias and six serious; none is rated low.
>
> Restricted to the settings the old-age-security hypothesis is about, where children still plausibly
> insure old age, more non-child old-age security lowers the annual probability of a birth by
> **0.007278** (SE 0.002901, 95% CI −0.012963 to −0.001593), pooling four studies. Danzer and Zyska (2023) record their
> own Brazilian estimate as 7.6% of the baseline probability that a woman in their sample gives birth
> in a year, which is the scale to read the pooled figure against.
>
> This chapter reports no pooled completed-fertility figure. Two claim-relevant studies report a
> completed-fertility effect with a usable standard error, below this review's threshold of three
> studies for pooling, so both are reported on their own: rural China at −0.169 (SE 0.079) and Namibia
> at −4.58 (SE 1.63).
>
> Causal credibility is **Very low** for the retirement-asset channel in the pre-modern period, **Low
> to moderate** for that channel during the First Demographic Transition, **Low** for it during the
> Second Demographic Transition, and **Low to moderate** for the grandparent-time channel there.
>
> State pensions arrive too late to have started the Western fertility decline, and the live pension
> channel in rich low-fertility countries runs through grandmothers rather than through retirement
> assets.

**Primary mechanism:** A non-child source of old-age support lowers the retirement return to an

## 1. The claim

The hypothesis this chapter examines is the **old-age-security hypothesis**: where a child is how a
person survives old age, that child is partly an investment asset, so any other retirement asset
lowers the return to having one more. A pension, a private annuity, or a bank that will hold savings
and pay them back all do the same job as a grown son who sends money home. The old-age-security
hypothesis predicts that fertility falls where such a substitute arrives and children were previously
the only claim on old age.

A retired grandmother supplies childcare, which lowers what a child costs her adult daughter, so a
retirement policy can raise the fertility of a woman it never touches. The treatment lands on the
grandmother and the outcome is counted a generation below her. A pension that lets a grandmother stop
working buys her daughter time she would otherwise have to purchase or forgo.

A pension expansion switches both channels on at once, so whether a pension raises or lowers fertility
is an empirical question rather than a theoretical one. The old-age-security hypothesis is about a
lifetime count of children, which matters because roughly half the corpus measures instead whether a
woman gave birth in a single year, and a policy can move the annual probability of a birth without
moving the lifetime count.

## 2. Theoretical mechanism

### 2.1 Children as a retirement asset

Neher (1971), Willis (1980), and Nugent (1985) state the founding proposition, that in a society
without pensions, insurance, or usable credit a child is the asset that funds old age, so the demand
for children falls once a substitute asset exists. Caldwell (1976) restates the same idea in
demographic vocabulary, tying fertility to the direction of net transfers between generations. None
of these statements is a model of a pension system; each is an account of why children were worth
having.

The pay-as-you-go pension literature reaches a fertility prediction from a formal model, and this
chapter reports only what each paper concludes. Ehrlich and Lui (1991), Nishimura and Zhang (1992),
Sinn (2004), and Boldrin, De Nardi, and Jones (2015) conclude that a public pension system displaces
the implicit contract under which children support parents, and that fertility falls as a result.
Sinn (2004) further concludes that such a system relieves parents of enforcing repayment by their own
children.

Theory does not sign the net effect of a pension on fertility, because the same literature also
produces models whose point is that pension systems leave households with too little private
incentive to bear children and therefore need correcting. A prediction of decline and a prescription
for pro-natal correction sit in the same body of work. The direction has to come from the data.

### 2.2 Pensions and grandmothers' time

Hirazawa and Yakita (2009) is the only formal theoretical source in this review's extraction file for
the grandparent-time channel, and its title states its subject: fertility, child care outside the
home, and pay-as-you-go social security. The project's theory file records it as the bridge from the
pension literature to the grandparent-time logic. This chapter makes no claim about what happens
inside the model, because no one on this project has opened the paper.

Every one of the 29 theory sources in this review's extraction file carries a retrieval status of
`not_retrieved`. Sections 2.1 and 2.2 therefore report what each paper concludes and say nothing
about how any model works. Section 13 records the same limitation as a deviation from protocol.

## 3. Empirical strategy

### 3.1 The treatments

The retirement-asset studies use six different instruments and this chapter declines to call them one
treatment: a newly created rural pension (Brazil and Namibia), individual participation in a rural
pension scheme (rural China), a cut to expected pension wealth (Italy), a long-term-care insurance
pilot (China), per-capita social-security spending (the United States after 1935), and the presence of
a bank in an American county in 1850. The two Bismarck-era studies use a seventh, the share of a
region's workers covered by compulsory social insurance. Each of these raises or lowers a household's
non-child claim on old age, which is why all of them appear in one chapter, and none is measured on
the same scale as any other, which is why no single coefficient summarises them.

Treating a bank as an instrument for old-age security is a substitution this chapter makes
deliberately, and the First Demographic Transition verdict in Section 8.2 depends on a reader
accepting that substitution. A household that can save and borrow has a non-child way to move
resources into old age, whether or not a state pension exists, so financial-market access belongs on
the same footing as a pension under the old-age-security hypothesis even though no legislature
enacted it as retirement policy.

The grandparent-time studies use three instruments, and the three are not interchangeable: assignment
by a pension reform (the Netherlands), actual retirement instrumented by eligibility (Germany), and
pension eligibility itself (Australia). A reform changes the rules facing a cohort, an instrumented
retirement measures the people who actually stopped working, and eligibility measures a legal
entitlement whether or not anyone acted on it.

Every estimate below is stated as the effect of *more* of the thing its channel names, which reverses
the sign on two studies whose treatments ran the other way. Billari and Galasso (2009) studied Italian
reforms that *cut* pension wealth, so their positive raw coefficients become negative once restated as
the effect of more non-child old-age security. Ilciukas (2023) studied a Dutch reform that *delayed*
mothers' retirement and so *reduced* grandparent availability, so his negative raw coefficients become
positive once restated as the effect of more availability. Without those two reversals the Italian and
Dutch results would appear to contradict the studies they in fact agree with.

### 3.2 The outcomes

The outcome the old-age-security hypothesis names is the number of children a woman ends up with,
and roughly half the corpus reports instead whether she gave birth in a given year. The two answer
different questions. A
policy that moves births earlier or later changes the annual probability for a while and leaves the
lifetime count untouched, so an estimate on the annual measure cannot establish the claim on its own.
This chapter therefore reports the two outcomes separately and never averages across them.

For the grandparent-time channel the person treated and the person counted are a generation apart:
the pension lands on the grandmother and the birth belongs to her daughter. The design therefore has
to link two people's records, and the estimate describes the daughter's childbearing rather than the
grandmother's.

### 3.3 The ideal experiment, twice

The ideal experiment for the retirement-asset channel takes otherwise identical households in a place
where children are the only old-age support, randomly gives some of them a non-child claim on
retirement income, and counts children at the end of childbearing. Household income, health, child
mortality, and the local labour market are held fixed, so the only thing varying across households is
whether old age is funded by a child or by something else.

The ideal experiment for the grandparent-time channel takes otherwise identical grandmothers, randomly
retires some of them, and counts their daughters' births. The daughter's own wage, the price of formal
childcare, and the grandmother's health are held fixed, so the only thing varying is how much of the
grandmother's time is available to her daughter.

The two experiments randomise different people, count different people, and hold different things
fixed, which is why the estimates from the two channels are never pooled in this chapter. Opposite
expected signs are the symptom of that difference rather than the reason for the rule.

### 3.4 The practical design for the retirement-asset channel

No government randomises a household's claim on retirement income, so this literature substitutes
pension reforms that legislatures enacted for their own reasons and compares people the reform covered
against people it did not. Three limitations follow from that substitution, and each is named here and
used by name below.

1. **Reform endogeneity: governments choose when to expand or cut a pension.** A country that
   extended pensions to rural workers in 1991 may differ from one that did not in ways the comparison
   cannot rule out, including in the fertility trend that was already under way. Reform endogeneity
   drives the risk-of-bias ratings in Section 6 and the causal ratings in Section 9.
2. **Treatment-scale incommensurability: the studies do not estimate the same treatment, so their
   coefficients are not measured on a common scale.** A binary exposure to a new rural pension, a
   continuous measure of pension value, and the presence of a bank are all changes in non-child
   old-age security, and a coefficient from one says nothing about the size of a coefficient from
   another. Section 7.1 takes up what this rules out.
3. **Bundled spending: a real programme delivers more than the thing being tested.** Per-capita
   social-security spending in the United States bundles old-age benefits with family allowances and
   broader relief, and long-term-care insurance bundles a retirement income with relief from nursing
   an elderly parent. Sections 7.4 and 9 both turn on this bundling.

### 3.5 The practical design for the grandparent-time channel

No government randomises a grandmother's retirement either, so this literature substitutes reform
assignment, instrumented retirement, and eligibility thresholds. Three further limitations follow, and
none of the three is shared with the retirement-asset designs.

4. **Opposite-signed reforms: the three studies do not push in the same direction.** The German and
   Australian treatments increase a grandmother's availability and the Dutch reform decreases it, so
   the three become comparable only after the reversal described in Section 3.1. Section 7.5 reports
   both the raw and the restated estimates so a reader can check the reversal.
5. **Eligibility is not retirement: a woman who becomes entitled to a pension may keep working.** An
   estimate built on eligibility measures the effect of an entitlement on a population that partly
   ignores it, which is a smaller and differently composed treatment than actual retirement. Sections
   7.5 and 9 both use this distinction.
6. **The selected sample: the cleanest Dutch estimate is computed on women chosen for their family
   circumstances.** Ilciukas's main sample holds only women who were childless and cohabiting before
   the reform, a group already close to a first birth, and his estimate roughly halves when that
   restriction is dropped. Section 7.5 reports both samples.

### 3.6 Limitations that would survive any experiment

Three more limitations concern which period a study describes or what its numbers are divided by,
rather than how treatment was assigned, so a perfectly executed experiment would leave all three in
place.

7. **The wrong clock: a study can identify its effect cleanly and still describe a period that is not
   the transition in question.** Italy's fertility *rises* across the 1998 to 2004 window Billari and
   Galasso study, and the historical settings have no in-window fertility series at all. Sections 7.3
   and 9 both use this limitation.
8. **Policy-constrained fertility: China's low fertility was produced partly by birth policy.** A
   Chinese fertility response to a pension therefore measures the birth policy alongside the pension,
   which this review requires be flagged wherever such a setting supplies evidence. Section 8.3 uses
   it.
9. **The mismatched denominator: a magnitude check divides one thing by another that is not its
   counterpart.** The screen behind the grandparent-time magnitude claim divides an effect on a
   one-year birth probability, or the effect of a one-year retirement delay, by a change in the total
   fertility rate measured over seventeen to twenty-one years. Section 8.4 uses it, and it decides
   that verdict.

## 4. Search strategy

The search combined two term blocks against OpenAlex with no restriction on publication date. The
fertility block ran from `fertilit*`, `birth rate`, `total fertility rate`, `completed fertility`,
`parity progression`, `childbear*`, and `number of children`, expanded with terms mined from a frozen
set of verified anchor papers. The old-age-security block ran from `pension*`, `social pension*`, `old
age security`, `social security`, `social insurance`, `provident fund`, `superannuation`, `annuities`,
`retire*`, `social grant`, `cash transfer*`, and neighbouring terms, expanded the same way. Crossref
resolved records that OpenAlex returned without a usable identifier.

The binding inclusion test asked which causal question a paper answers, not whether the paper is about
pensions. A pension study whose outcome is savings, schooling, or elderly health tests a link in the
mechanism without estimating a fertility effect. A study in which fertility is the cause and insurance
purchase the outcome is mechanism evidence running the other way. A study in which pension
eligibility changes a grandmother's childcare time answers this chapter's grandparent-time question
rather than its retirement-asset question.

The three grandparent-time studies were found by this search, ruled off the chapter's causal
question, and then put back by review, which bears on how much weight their evidence can take. Eibich
and Siedler (2020), Ilciukas (2023), and Akyol and Atalay (2025) ranked eighth, tenth, and nineteenth
in the screening pool and were coded as belonging to a different channel, with the reason "retired
parents reduce the cost of children", before review reinstated all three. They never returned through
the full-text screen or the extraction-verification step, and Section 13 records that as a deviation.

The search also found and dropped grandparent-availability papers whose outcome is not fertility,
including Bratti, Frattini, and Scervini on Italian pension reform and Duflo on South African
pensions, both of which report maternal labour supply or child outcomes rather than births. The
grandparent-time evidence base is three studies for that reason rather than because only three
exist.

## 7. Quantitative synthesis

### 7.1 Why there is no single pooled coefficient

A single number for "the effect of pensions on fertility" would require at least three independent
studies sharing a mechanism, an outcome, a unit, a treatment scale, and a usable standard error, and
this corpus does not supply that combination. Treatment-scale incommensurability is the binding
obstacle: a binary exposure to a new rural pension, a continuous measure of pension wealth, a
long-term-care pilot, per-capita social spending, and the presence of a bank are not one treatment
measured five ways. This chapter therefore pools within an outcome family, never across all pension
treatments, and reports 24 further effects narratively because their outcomes, scales, or uncertainty
measures do not permit pooling.

### 7.2 Birth probability, restricted to the settings the claim is about

More non-child old-age security lowers the annual probability of a birth by 0.007278 (SE 0.002901,
95% CI −0.012963 to −0.001593), pooling the four studies set in places where children still
plausibly insure old age: Brazil, Namibia, rural China, and the Chinese long-term-care pilot. Precision inside that
pool is concentrated in Brazil, whose standard error of 0.003 is an order of magnitude smaller than
Namibia's 0.043 or rural China's 0.044.

Restricting the pool to those four studies barely moved the summary, because the study removed sits
almost on top of the number it was removed from. The published five-study summary is −0.006954 (SE
0.002037, 95% CI −0.010946 to −0.002963), and Italy carried 50.7% of the weight in it. Italy's
own oriented estimate of −0.006640 (SE 0.002860) sits almost exactly on the restricted pool, so
dropping half the weight of the summary changed the point estimate by three ten-thousandths and
widened the interval. Concentration of weight was real here and turned out not to matter.

Danzer and Zyska record their own Brazilian estimate of −0.009 as a fall of 7.6% against the baseline
probability that a woman in their sample gives birth in a year, and the restricted pool is slightly
smaller than their estimate and therefore a slightly smaller share of that same baseline. Billari and
Galasso's Italian sample gives a second anchor: an annual probability of 0.0522 observed in the data,
against which their own oriented effect of −0.00664 is roughly an eighth.

### 7.3 Completed fertility, reported with no pooled number

This chapter reports no pooled completed-fertility figure, because two claim-relevant studies carry a
usable standard error on that outcome and this review's rule requires three. Rural China gives −0.169
(SE 0.079) and Namibia gives −4.58 (SE 1.63). Both point the same way, and the gap between them is
large enough that averaging two numbers so far apart would report a precision the evidence does not
have.

Italy's oriented completed-fertility estimate is −0.0529 (SE 0.0287), reported here on its own rather
than inside a pool. The previously published
completed-fertility summary of −0.067672 (SE 0.026971, 95% CI −0.120536 to −0.014808) was 88.3%
weighted on that Italian estimate, with rural China supplying 11.7% and Namibia 0.03%. The wrong clock
applies directly: Italy in 1998 to 2004 is a mature pension system in a below-replacement setting
whose total fertility rate rises across the study window, from 1.2099 to 1.3368, which is the case
this chapter says the mechanism does not fit. A summary that was arithmetically an Italian number
was, for that reason, evidence about the setting the argument excludes.

### 7.4 The two estimates that run the other way

Han and coauthors' long-term-care insurance estimate of +0.04 sits inside the birth-probability pool
running against the direction the pool is offered as evidence for. A long-term-care pilot is followed
by *more* births. Han's estimate carries 2.8% of the weight in the published summary, so it does not
drive the result, and bundled spending offers a reading of it: insurance that relieves a daughter of
nursing her mother frees the daughter's time in much the way a retired grandmother does, which would
make this estimate evidence on the grandparent-time channel rather than on the retirement-asset
channel. Section 11 records that reading as an open question rather than acting on it.

Galofré-Vilà's positive estimates are excluded from the completed-fertility pool for a defensible
reason that has not previously been stated in this chapter, and the exclusion is rating-determinative.
Bundled spending is the ground: per-capita social-security spending after 1935 bought old-age benefits
alongside family allowances and broader relief, so a positive fertility response cannot be read as a
response to old-age security alone. The GRADE panel recomputed both pools and found that reinstating
Galofré-Vilà's +0.098, which would take 74% of the weight, flips the completed-fertility summary from
−0.0677 to +0.0549. All three raters escalated on this point, because the same chapter retains Han's
equally bundled long-term-care estimate inside the other pool.

### 7.5 The grandparent-time estimates

All eight grandparent-time estimates point the same way once restated as the effect of more
availability, across three countries and three research designs. Table 2 gives them: Germany at +0.166
(SE 0.040) for fathers and +0.114 (SE 0.054) for mothers; the Netherlands at +0.056 (SE 0.021) and
+0.266 (SE 0.131) on the main sample and +0.029 (SE 0.012) and +0.145 (SE 0.072) on the unrestricted
sample; Australia at +0.046 (SE 0.008) and +0.067 (SE 0.023).

Eight agreeing estimates are worth less than the count suggests, because opposite-signed reforms, the
gap between eligibility and retirement, and the selected sample all bite on this channel.
Opposite-signed reforms mean the Dutch agreement is produced
by a reversal, and a reader who rejects the reversal loses two of the three countries. Eligibility is
not retirement, so the Australian estimates describe an entitlement acting on a population that partly
ignored it, while the German estimates describe women who actually stopped working. The selected
sample shows its effect within Ilciukas's own paper: his instrumented estimate falls from +0.266 to
+0.145 when the restriction to women childless and cohabiting before the reform is dropped, so half
the headline magnitude comes from studying women already close to a first birth.

### 7.6 What the protocol asks for and this chapter does not have

This review's quantitative-synthesis template asks for heterogeneity statistics, moderator analysis,
sensitivity analysis, and a publication-bias check, and none of the four was computed for this
hypothesis. Section 13 records the omission as a deviation.

## 8. Demographic significance

This chapter reaches a verdict for each of three phenomena: pre-modern fertility variation before
1870, the First Demographic Transition of roughly 1870 to 1965, and the Second Demographic Transition
from 1965 to the present. The Second Demographic Transition receives two verdicts, because the
retirement-asset and grandparent-time channels disagree about it.

### 8.1 Pre-modern fertility variation

Reliance on children for old-age support was close to universal before 1870, and a universal condition
explains why fertility was high rather than why it differed across populations. Pre-modern societies
varied in family systems, inheritance rules, communal care, and expectations about who supports an
older adult, and this corpus contains no estimate of how any of that variation moved fertility.

No study in this corpus estimates a causal effect in a setting without financial markets. The single
study whose data predate 1870, Basso, Bodenhorn, and Cuberes on American counties in 1850, is coded in this review's own
classification file as spanning both the pre-modern period and the First Demographic Transition, and
is placed in the First Demographic Transition below, because county banking in 1850 is part of the same financial deepening that runs through the
transition rather than a feature of a world before financial institutions existed. One cross-section
of counties cannot explain why fertility differed across pre-modern populations in any case.

**Verdict: insufficient data.**

### 8.2 First Demographic Transition

State pensions arrive too late to have started the Western fertility decline. That decline is under
way from the late nineteenth century, Bismarck's pension system dates to the late 1880s, and U.S.
Social Security begins in 1935. A pension arriving decades after a decline began can deepen or
redirect it and cannot have caused it, and that argument depends on no estimate in this chapter.

Financial development is the better-timed substitute, because a household that wants to stop relying
on a child needs a credible way to save and borrow rather than a state pension. Basso, Bodenhorn, and
Cuberes (2014) supply the only estimate on that margin, and its full description matters: 196
Northeastern American counties observed in a single year, 1850, rated SERIOUS for confounding and
SERIOUS for selection because a bank's location is chosen where development is already happening,
reporting −0.04 on the log child-woman ratio with a standard error of 0.02. A cross-section of that
design cannot separate the effect of a bank from the reason the bank was there.

Two of this review's own files classify the three historical studies differently, and this chapter
follows the documented reasoning rather than the automated rule. The rule based on fertility levels
returns no classification for Prussia, Imperial Germany, or 1850 America, because none has an
in-window fertility series. The file recording documented reasoning assigns Prussia and Imperial
Germany to the First Demographic Transition and 1850 America to both the pre-modern period and the
First Demographic Transition.

**Verdict: partial.** The mechanism is supported and the historical estimates operate at population
scale, while the timing rules out state pensions as the initiating cause and no share of the decline
has been computed.

### 8.3 Second Demographic Transition, retirement-asset channel

The retirement-asset argument predicts the wrong sign for the rich-country Second Demographic
Transition. Pension systems in rich countries have been retrenched since the 1980s, later eligibility
and lower replacement rates make a child *more* valuable as old-age support on this argument, and
fertility fell anyway. An explanation whose sign is backwards for the phenomenon it is offered to
explain does not become right by being applied more carefully.

Mature pension systems also leave the retirement-asset mechanism almost no room to work. By the time
the Second Demographic Transition begins, public pensions and private savings are near-universal in the settings
where it is best documented, so a new pension policy changes a household's non-child claim on old age
at the margin rather than creating one.

Policy-constrained fertility disqualifies half of the below-replacement evidence on this channel from
settling the question. The
Chinese studies, Han and coauthors and Shen and coauthors, observe fertility under a binding birth
policy, so a Chinese response to a pension measures the birth policy alongside the pension. The two
Chinese estimates also disagree with each other after orientation, +0.04 against −0.169.

This review's third route to demographic significance was computed for the old-age-security
hypothesis and explains almost none of the variation. Across six association records the largest
partial R² is 0.0424, with model R² values running from 0.007 to 0.345.

**Verdict: not significant.**

### 8.4 Second Demographic Transition, grandparent-time channel

Three quasi-experiments in three rich countries whose total fertility rate is below the replacement
level of 2.1 agree that more grandparent availability raises fertility, which makes this the live
pension channel for the Second Demographic Transition. Their agreed direction is the finding, and no
magnitude follows from it for a reason that is arithmetic rather than a matter of judgment.

The magnitude screen this chapter previously cited compares eight estimates against an observed
fertility decline and returns six shares: 29.8%, 45.8%, 57.6%, 66.7%, 149.0%, and 273.4%. The Dutch
shares divide effects of 0.029, 0.056, 0.145, and 0.266 by a fall in the Dutch total fertility rate from 1.7215 to 1.6242, or 0.0973,
over 2004 to 2021. The Australian shares divide effects of 0.046 and 0.067 by a fall from 1.7385 to
1.6380, or 0.1005, over 2001 to 2022. The German estimates are not scaled at all, because German
fertility *rises* over the 1984 to 2017 window, from 1.3889 to 1.5696, leaving no decline to divide
by.

Two of the six shares exceed 100%, and a share above 100% shows that the ratio does not mean anything
rather than that the effect is large. The mismatched denominator produces such a share: this review's own
method note records that the screen "divides effect magnitudes by observed TFR declines even when the
paper outcome is a birth probability rather than TFR", and directs that its labels be read as
"slope-sufficiency screening language, not as exact decomposition shares." The 273.4% divides the
fertility effect of one year of delayed maternal retirement by a national fertility change accumulated
over seventeen years, and it never multiplies by the share of women the reform actually exposed. No
share of any fertility decline can be attributed to this channel on the arithmetic in hand.

**Verdict: insufficient data**, pending the decomposition specified in Section 11.

## 9. GRADE rating

Three raters scored each of four channel-and-phenomenon pairs independently and returned the same
verdict on three of the four, with the plain reason for each given first below. GRADE is an
evidence-grading framework borrowed from clinical research that rates how much confidence a body of
evidence supports rather than how large an effect is; its levels here run Very low, Low, Low to
moderate, and Moderate.

**Pre-modern, retirement-asset channel: Very low, unanimous.** No extracted study estimates a
pre-modern causal effect, and the nearest evidence is a single serious-risk cross-section of 196
counties in 1850. Correlational evidence alone is this review's very-low pattern. No rater escalated.

**First Demographic Transition, retirement-asset channel: Low to moderate, unanimous.** Two
moderate-risk quasi-experiments converge, in Brazil from 1981 to 2014 and Namibia from 1990 to 2012,
which meets the replication clause, and both sit outside the 1870 to 1965 Western transition the
verdict is about, which is the wrong clock. Every era-matched historical study is rated serious, and
those three disagree with one another. All three raters escalated, on the ground that Galofré-Vilà's
wrong-way estimate was dropped from the completed-fertility pool as bundled spending while Han's
equally bundled estimate was retained in the other pool, and that reinstating Galofré-Vilà flips that
pool from −0.0677 to +0.0549.

**Second Demographic Transition, retirement-asset channel: Low, unanimous.** One credibly identified
rich-country estimate exists, Billari and Galasso's Italian study, and its two replication candidates
are serious-risk Chinese studies that disagree with each other, which is this review's low pattern of
one credible quasi-experiment without replication. All three raters escalated, on the ground that the
significance file already flags this row for human review and that whether policy-constrained China
counts as evidence about this transition is a scope judgment rather than a rating judgment.

**Second Demographic Transition, grandparent-time channel: Low to moderate, by two raters of three.**
Three moderate-risk quasi-experiments in three below-replacement countries agree in direction, which
reaches the pattern for Moderate, and two raters docked one level because none of the three studies
appears in the full-text screen file and their eight effect rows are the only extractions in this
review marked unverified. One rater scored Moderate, having verified that the reinstatement of all
three studies was complete and therefore carried no selection risk, and noting that Eibich and
Siedler's outcome is a birth in the following year rather than a completed count. All three raters
escalated, on the ground that this channel's studies are unscreened and unverified and that the
magnitude screen does not measure what the verdict cited it for.

The panel's grandparent-time verdict is a downgrade on both axes from this chapter's previous rating
of Moderate for causal credibility and partial demographic significance with slope-screening support.
Escalations stand on three of the four pairs.

## 10. Verdict

One pension reform moves fertility down through one channel and up through another, and the evidence
for the two channels is strongest in different eras.

| Phenomenon and channel | Causal credibility | Demographic significance |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-modern, retirement asset | Very low | Insufficient data |
| FDT, retirement asset | Low to moderate | Partial |
| SDT, retirement asset | Low | Not significant |
| SDT, grandparent time | Low to moderate | Insufficient data |

The timing of state pensions is resolved and the size of both channels is not. State pensions arrive
too late to have started the Western fertility decline, and if a non-child claim on old age did work
through that transition, financial development is the better-timed candidate on evidence too thin to
confirm it. The retirement-asset channel has a birth-probability estimate in the settings where
children still insure old age and no completed-fertility figure at all, and the grandparent-time
channel has three studies agreeing on direction and no defensible magnitude. Nothing here supports a
statement about how much of any fertility decline either channel explains.

## 11. Open questions and recommended studies

**A real slope-sufficiency calculation for the grandparent-time channel would replace the magnitude
screen the current verdict cannot use.** The design applies the Australian eligibility effect on a
daughter's number of children, +0.067 (SE 0.023), to the observed change in the share of Australian
grandmothers past pension age between 2001 and 2022, using pension-age rules and population age
structure to compute that share. The number to report is the implied change in the total fertility
rate against the observed fall of 0.1005 over the same window, which is a comparison in matched units.

**A completed-fertility estimate with a standard error from a developing-country pension expansion is
what stands between this chapter and a pooled figure for that outcome.** The setting is a country
where children still plausibly insure old age and a pension was newly extended; the design is a cohort
exposure comparison; the outcome is the number of children born to women old enough to have finished
childbearing. Recovering the standard error on Danzer and Zyska's Brazilian −1.3 would do the same job
without new fieldwork, as Section 7.3 records.

**One reform measured on both channels is the only way to learn the net effect of a pension on
fertility.** The design needs a single pension reform, records linking a grandmother to her adult
daughter, and both outcomes measured in the same population: the fertility of women whose own old-age
claim changed, and the fertility of daughters whose mothers became newly retired. The numbers to
report are the two effects and their sum.

**Whether long-term-care insurance belongs to the grandparent-time channel is an open classification
question with a consequence.** Insurance that relieves a daughter of nursing her mother frees the
daughter's time, so Han and coauthors' +0.04 may be a grandparent-time estimate sitting inside a
retirement-asset pool. Reclassifying it drops the published birth-probability pool from five studies
to four and the restricted pool from four to three, and both would need recomputing.

**For the pre-modern period, the missing evidence is variation in whether children could be relied on
for old-age support at all.** Pre-modern societies differed in family systems, inheritance rules, and
communal-care arrangements, and no estimate in this corpus measures how any of that variation moved
fertility. A design would need populations that differed in whether an old person could expect
support from someone other than a child, with fertility observed across that difference.
