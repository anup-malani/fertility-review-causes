# Compulsory Schooling, Maternal Education, and Birth Postponement

**Category:** Demographic
**Primary mechanism:** Raising the school-leaving age keeps a prospective mother enrolled through the ages at which she would otherwise have had a first birth, moving that birth later in her life.
**Cross-references:** Parent hypothesis is Tempo Effects and Birth Postponement (`tempo-effects-birth-postponement.md`), of which this is one driver; see PI call 2 on whether the parent chapter should survive. Distinct from `compulsory-education-child-economic-value.md`, which asks whether schooling reduces the *number* of children by making them costlier — that is a quantum claim about parents' demand, this is a timing claim about the mother's own adolescence. The two share a search and 25 RA-approved papers, overlapping by one (Geruso and Royer).
**Status:** TICK-039 (stage-13 readability review deferred at Alexandra's request). Rewritten against `docs/chapter-template.md` on 2026-08-29. Not PI-reviewed in this form. Written on 10 of 10 wanted full texts (100%).

## 1. The claim

This chapter explores the effect of compulsory schooling on the timing of a woman's first birth.

### 1.1 In plain terms

In plain terms: many countries have raised the age at which young people are allowed to leave school. A girl who is still at school at sixteen is less likely to have a baby that year than a girl who has left, and the claim is that the law is what makes the difference.

The important question is what happens afterwards. A girl who does not have a baby at sixteen may have one at twenty instead. If she does, she ends up with the same number of children as she would have had anyway — she simply has them later. If she does not, the law has changed how many children exist, not just when they arrive. Those are two different claims, and almost everything in this chapter turns on which one the evidence supports.

The whole of the review is about why people came to have fewer children. A change that only moves births later, without changing how many there are, does not answer that question — though for a while it can make the numbers look as though it does. Why that is so is the subject of §2.

### 1.2 The claim precisely

The parameter this chapter estimates is twofold, and the two halves must not be reported as one: **the change in a woman's age at first birth**, measured in years, and **the change in her completed fertility**, measured in births per woman — both caused by an increase in the compulsory school-leaving age, signed so that a positive value on the first means later, and a negative value on the second means fewer.

The chapter rates the claim against the review's three target phenomena, abbreviated throughout as **PM** (pre-modern fertility variation, before roughly 1870), **FDT** (the First Demographic Transition, roughly 1870–1965) and **SDT** (the Second Demographic Transition, roughly 1965 onward).

The counterfactual is a ceteris-paribus perturbation: hold family background, ability and labour-market conditions fixed, and raise the age below which a girl may not leave school. The included designs approximate this well — better than in most chapters in this review — because reform cutoffs assign the perturbation on birth date or cohort.

**This mechanism has an identity arm and a behavioural arm, and they need entirely different treatment.**

- **The identity arm needs no study and cannot be false.** Fertility can be counted two ways, and this chapter turns on the difference. A **period** measure counts the births occurring in one calendar year across all women alive that year; a **cohort** measure follows one group of women born in the same year and counts the children they each end up with. If the average age at which women bear children is rising, the number of births observed in a given calendar year is mechanically depressed while the rise is under way, because each cohort's births are being spread over a longer stretch of calendar time. The standard statement of this is the Bongaarts–Feeney relation, in which the tempo-adjusted period fertility rate equals the observed rate divided by (1 − *r*), where *r* is the annual rise in mean age at childbearing. This is arithmetic. It is also **temporary and self-reversing**: when postponement stops, the depression unwinds.
- **The behavioural arm is where the hypothesis can be wrong.** Does the reform actually raise the age at first birth, and does it change the total number of children a woman eventually has? Only the second question bears on the phenomena this review is about.

Failing to separate these is the characteristic error in this literature. A chapter that reports a large fall in teenage births as though it were a contribution to the fertility decline has measured the identity arm and reported it as the behavioural one.

**Margin.** The reform's documented effect is on the **timing** of the extensive margin — whether a first birth has occurred by a given age. The claim that would matter for this review is an effect on **quantum**, the completed count. §8 shows the evidence separates these sharply.

## 2. Theoretical mechanism

In the reader's own vocabulary: compulsory schooling raises the shadow price of a teenage birth by making the adolescent's time non-substitutable during school hours, and raises the return to deferring childbearing by increasing human capital. Four channels have been proposed, and they are empirically separable.

**Incapacitation** operates only while the mandate binds: attendance changes time use, supervision and peer exposure. Its sharpest test is whether conceptions fall during school terms but not during holidays.

**Marriage postponement** operates where marriage is the usual gateway to childbearing: the reform delays union formation and the birth follows.

**Human capital, knowledge and preferences** operate after the mandate ends: contraceptive knowledge, partner quality, bargaining power and labour-market returns.

**Catch-up** is not a channel but the *absence* of one. If births prevented at 16–18 occur at 19–21, the reform has moved births in time and changed nothing else.

**What would make the hypothesis wrong.** The timing claim is wrong if reforms that verifiably raise attainment leave age at first birth unmoved. The claim that would matter for this review — that the mechanism helps explain the fertility decline — is wrong if completed fertility is unchanged. As §8 shows, every study that looked for the second found nothing, so the hypothesis in its demographically relevant form is not merely unsupported but repeatedly falsified in its own literature.

## 5. The ideal design

Written before the literature was read, so that §6 can be compared against a fixed yardstick rather than against the best paper that happens to exist.

### 5.1 The ideal estimand

Two parameters, from **the same cohort of women**, reported together:

1. The change in **completed cohort fertility**, in births per woman observed at age 45, caused by a one-year increase in the compulsory school-leaving age, among girls bound by the reform.
2. The change in **mean age at first birth**, in years, for those same women.

The insistence on one cohort supplying both is the whole specification. A study reporting only the second measures postponement; a study reporting only teenage births measures postponement and can be mistaken for measuring quantum. Only a design that reports both from the same women can say whether the births prevented at sixteen were prevented or merely moved — which is the question this chapter exists to answer.

### 5.2 The design that would identify it

**Source of variation.** A school-leaving-age reform with a **sharp birth-date cutoff**: girls born a day either side face different legal minimum leaving ages.

**Comparison group.** Cohorts born immediately either side of that cutoff.

**Identifying assumption.** No other policy discontinuity falls at the same cutoff, and the running variable is not manipulable. Falsifiable three ways: a density test at the threshold; placebo outcomes not plausibly affected by schooling; and pre-reform cohorts showing no discontinuity at the same calendar position.

**Estimating equation.** A sharp regression discontinuity in birth date, estimated twice on the same sample — once with completed fertility at 45 as the outcome and once with age at first birth — reported alongside the first stage in years of schooling. The two outcomes must come from one specification and one bandwidth, or the timing and quantum results are not commensurable.

**Data required.** An administrative birth registry linked to education records. The horizon is the binding constraint: from age 15 at the reform to observation at 45 is **30 years** of linked data.

**Sample size.** To detect 0.05 births per woman at 80% power requires about 10,600 per arm in a simple comparison; local-linear RD inflates that roughly 2.7-fold, to about **28,700 per side within the bandwidth**. This is why the credible studies in this literature are all built on administrative registers.

**A note on what the ideal design deliberately excludes.** Instrumenting years of schooling with the reform answers a different question — the effect of a year of schooling on compliers — and is *not* the ideal design here, because the registered claim is about the policy. §6.2 shows the literature divides on exactly this axis.

**The ideal design exists, and it is Geruso and Royer (2018).** UK 1972 reform, sharp birth-date discontinuity, both outcomes from the same cohort, followed to age 45. It matches §5.1 and §5.2 on every dimension. **Its completed-fertility result is zero.**

This is the most important sentence in the chapter, and the previous draft did not contain it. The chapter's verdict does not rest on an absence of evidence, a weak pooled number, or an inference across incommensurable designs. It rests on the one study that asked the exact question in the exact way it should be asked, and got a null. Arendt and coauthors, at a horizon nine years shorter, agree. Chaijaroen and Panda observe the mechanism of the null directly — the births reappearing at 20.

**The one thing the anchor study lacks is an extraction row.** Geruso and Royer's completed-fertility null is carried in this chapter on narrative, with no point estimate, no standard error and no locator (§4, §7.2). The chapter's single best piece of evidence is the piece that was never extracted, which is why §11 item 5 is the highest-value task on the list and why §9 rates the completed-fertility row LOW rather than MODERATE.

### 6.1 The naive estimator, and a literature that already fixed it

**What is the naive estimator here?** Compare the fertility of more-educated women with that of less-educated women.

It is badly confounded, and unusually, the confounding runs *backwards through the outcome*: a teenage birth is itself a leading cause of leaving school. Regressing early fertility on schooling therefore puts a consequence of the outcome on the right-hand side, and the resulting association overstates how protective schooling is. Selection on family background and ability pushes the same way.

**How many included studies use it? Zero.** All ten exploit reform-induced variation — regression discontinuities on birth-date or cohort cutoffs, or laws instrumenting attainment. This literature identified its naive estimator and built its designs around it, which is why the *timing* finding in this chapter is the most credible causal result across the three chapters rewritten so far. It is worth saying plainly, because the rest of this chapter is critical: the direction and existence of the timing effect is not in doubt.

### 6.2 Where the estimates genuinely disagree: two estimand classes, not one

The disagreement in this literature is not about sign. It is about what the coefficients are coefficients *of*, and the previous draft's pooling audit did not test for it.

Six of the 17 estimates are **2SLS effects per additional year of schooling**, identified off compliers whose behaviour the law changed, with first stages of roughly 0.25 years (Silles) to 0.31 years (Geruso and Royer). Eleven are **reduced-form intention-to-treat effects of the policy itself**. These are different parameters. An IV estimate of −4.0 pp *per year of schooling* is not comparable to a reduced-form −0.81 pp *from the reform*, and multiplying the former by a population-wide change in schooling treats a complier-specific local average as a population parameter — precisely the error the previous draft flagged in its open questions and did not act on.

The previous draft's pooling audit stratified on outcome family alone and concluded `DO_NOT_POOL` for all four families, on the ground that no family had three independent studies with a comparable estimate and standard error. Cross-tabulating those families against estimand class shows the conclusion is right and the stated reason is wrong:

| Pooling family | IV-per-year studies | Reduced-form studies | Mixed? |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Age-specific birth probability | 0 | **3** | No |
| Cumulative first-birth probability | **3** | 2 | **Yes** |
| Cumulative teen motherhood | 1 | 2 | **Yes** |
| Age at first birth | 1 | 0 | No |

Two families do contain three independent same-class studies — three reduced-form studies of age-specific birth probability (Black, Geruso and Royer, Wilson), and three IV-per-year studies of cumulative first-birth probability (Bhattacharjee, DeCicca and Krashinsky, Silles). The ≥3 threshold is met, twice. What blocks pooling is not the number of studies but the **six of 17 rows that carry a standard error**, and two families that mix estimand classes and would have to be split before anyone counted.

This changes what should happen next. "No family has three independent studies" reads as a fact about the literature and closes the question. "Three families would qualify once the missing standard errors are extracted and the mixed families are split by estimand class" is a task, and it is on the open-questions list at §11.

### 6.3 The transmission ledger

| Stage | Question | Sign |
|---|---|---|
| Reform enacted → binding on the girl | Who is actually bound? First stages are ~0.25–0.31 years of additional schooling. | Attenuates |
| Bound → conception averted | Does enrolment prevent conception? Adamecz-Völgyi and Scharle: falls in term time, not in holidays. | Holds |
| Conception averted → birth averted at that age | Robust across all ten settings. | Holds |
| Averted at 16–18 → averted for life | **Catch-up.** Thailand's cohorts are *significantly higher* at 20. Wilson's effect dissipates after 20. Kırdar's effects later disappear. | **Reverses** |
| Cohort quantum → period fertility rate | The tempo distortion depresses the period rate while postponement is under way, then unwinds. | **Reverses** |

**This ledger does not behave like the others in this review.** §2.3 of the template anticipates entries that all run one way, in which case the headline is biased in a known direction. Here the first three entries attenuate and the last two *reverse*: they do not shrink the effect, they return it. A mechanism whose ledger reverses is not a small cause of the phenomenon; it is a cause of a temporary appearance of the phenomenon. That distinction is the chapter's result, and the template has no vocabulary for it (PI call 3).

## 7. Quantitative synthesis

### 7.1 The answer in plain terms

The laws work, and they work in the direction everyone expected: where a country made girls stay at school longer, fewer of them became mothers at sixteen or seventeen, and the ones who did become mothers did so about half a year later on average. Ten separate studies in ten different countries agree, and the best of them can show the effect appearing during school terms and not during the holidays, which is about as close to a clean demonstration as this kind of evidence gets.

What the laws do not do is change how many children a woman has in the end. Three studies followed the women far enough to find out — into their late thirties and forties — and all three found the same thing: the same number of children as before. One country's data shows it directly, with the women who had fewer babies at fifteen having more of them by twenty.

So the answer is that these laws move births later without removing any. For a while that makes a country's birth statistics look as though fertility is falling, because the births that would have happened this year happen a few years from now instead. But they do happen. The apparent fall is real in the statistics and absent in the families.

### 7.2 The estimate

**The timing effect.** Consistent in direction across all ten studies. Reduced-form policy effects on the probability of a birth at newly covered ages run from roughly −0.4 to −0.9 percentage points (Wilson, UK) to −4 to −5 percentage points (Chaijaroen and Panda, Thailand), with relative reductions of 13–36% where reported in relative terms (Hungary, Turkey). The single extracted estimate of age at first birth is **+0.5 years** (Arendt et al., Denmark).

**The quantum effect.** Zero, in every study that measured it. This is the chapter's decisive quantity, and it is the one with no extraction rows (§4). On the previous draft's narrative reporting: Geruso and Royer find no effect on completed family size at 45; Arendt and coauthors none on having children or number of children at 39; Cummins finds preferred completed marital-fertility effects near zero; Chaijaroen and Panda find fertility significantly *higher* at 20 after being lower at 14–15.

**No pooled estimate is reported, and none should be.** Four pooling families, all `DO_NOT_POOL` — for the corrected reason given in §6.2, not the one recorded in the audit. The informative synthesis here is not an average but an **age profile**, and it is unanimous: effects concentrate at the legally covered ages and disappear afterwards. Geruso and Royer find effects at 16–17 and none at 18–19. DeCicca and Krashinsky find concentration at 17–18. Wilson's fades after 20. Thailand's reverses by 20. An average over ages would destroy exactly the structure that identifies the mechanism.

## 8. Demographic significance

The phenomenon to be explained is measured in whole children of completed cohort fertility — the FDT is a fall of roughly three births per woman and the SDT roughly one and a half; this mechanism offers a delay of about half a year in age at first birth and a completed-fertility effect that every study measuring it estimates as zero.

That comparison settles the chapter. A mechanism whose effect on the phenomenon's own unit is estimated at zero, repeatedly and precisely, does not require a decomposition share. What remains is to say why the mechanism nonetheless looks large in period statistics, and to be honest that the answer depends on a definition the review has not fixed.

**The definitional conflict, stated plainly.** If the target phenomena are defined on **period** total fertility rates, a pure tempo effect *does* contribute to them, mechanically and by the identity in §1.2, and this mechanism's contribution is real though temporary. If they are defined on **completed cohort** fertility, this mechanism contributes exactly nothing. The review's protocol does not say which, and the two readings give opposite verdicts for this chapter and for the whole tempo hypothesis. Everything below is stated on the cohort definition, because that is the unit the template's S4 assumes and the unit in which "people came to have fewer children" is a true statement. **This is PI call 1 and it is not a technicality.**

**The endogeneity check.** Runs the other way here, and against the mechanism. Compulsory-schooling reforms are legislated by states already undergoing fertility decline, urbanisation and rising female labour-force participation. The reforms are in part a *consequence* of the transition they are being asked to explain. Because the included designs identify off within-reform cutoffs rather than off which countries reformed, this does not threaten their internal validity — but it does mean the mechanism's own historical movement cannot be treated as exogenous when scaling to an aggregate.

### 8.1 Pre-modern fertility variation

For pre-modern variation, the verdict is NOT ASSESSED, because compulsory schooling did not exist before the modern era and the phenomenon is therefore out of scope for this hypothesis in the registry rather than an empty in-scope cell.

Had it been assessable, the expected sign would be zero: there is no treatment to vary.

### 8.2 First Demographic Transition

For the First Demographic Transition, the verdict is NOT ASSESSED, because the in-scope cell is empty — eight of the ten reforms postdate 1965 and none of the ten estimates a completed-fertility effect within the FDT window.

The two candidates are England's 1947 reform (Cummins, adjacent, near-zero on completed marital fertility) and Black, Devereux and Salvanes' early-twentieth-century US state laws, which report teenage-birth effects only. Neither supplies a completed-fertility estimate inside the transition. This is an empty cell, not a weak literature: the studies were never done, rather than done badly. Were it assessed, the sign implied by the post-1965 evidence would be zero.

### 8.3 Second Demographic Transition

For the Second Demographic Transition, the verdict is NEGLIGIBLE, because the mechanism's estimated effect on completed cohort fertility is zero in all three studies that measured it, and a mechanism that moves no births out of a cohort accounts for none of a decline measured in births per cohort.

Three points support this and one qualifies it.

The reforms are correctly timed for the SDT — 1972 in the UK, 1997 in Turkey, and later in Thailand and India — so unlike §8.2 this is a real evidence base rather than an empty cell. The mechanism had its chance to matter and did not.

The postponement it produces is genuine but small against the phenomenon's own postponement. Mean age at first birth rose by roughly four to five years across the SDT; the largest documented reform effect is about half a year, and that from a first stage of a third of a year of schooling.

The catch-up is directly observed rather than inferred. Thailand's treated cohorts are significantly *higher* at 20 having been lower at 14–15. This is the single most useful observation in the chapter, because it converts "we found no long-run effect" — which can be a power problem — into "we found the births, later" — which cannot.

The qualification is the definitional one above. On a reading based on the period **total fertility rate** (TFR: the number of children a woman would bear if she experienced current age-specific birth rates throughout her reproductive life) this verdict would change, and a reader who holds that reading should treat §8.3 as answering a different question from the one they are asking.

## 9. GRADE rating

**GRADE** is the standard scheme for rating how much certainty a body of evidence supports, from HIGH down to VERY LOW, with every downgrade attributed to a named defect.

| Phenomenon and claim | GRADE | Downgrades | Demographic significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| PM | **No evidence** | No body of evidence to rate; the treatment did not exist. Out of scope in the registry rather than an empty in-scope cell. | NOT ASSESSED |
| FDT, completed fertility | **No evidence** | No body of evidence to rate: no included study estimates a completed-fertility effect inside the FDT window. To earn a rating this cell would need a pre-1965 reform followed to cohort completion. VERY LOW would wrongly imply the question had been studied badly. | NOT ASSESSED |
| SDT, **timing** of first birth | **MODERATE** | Unanimous three-rater panel. Ten quasi-experiments across seven countries converge; term-versus-holiday and conception-versus-abortion evidence strengthens the causal reading. | SDT, **completed** fertility | **LOW** | *Imprecision and indirectness*: the three nulls that carry this row are **not extracted** — no effect rows, no standard errors, no risk-of-bias linkage (§4). The split between the last two rows is the chapter's structure in miniature: high confidence about a quantity that does not bear on the review's question, and lower confidence about the quantity that does — driven not by weak studies but by an extraction gap.

## 10. Verdict

Compulsory schooling reliably postpones first births and reliably fails to reduce completed families. Ten quasi-experiments in seven countries agree that raising the leaving age lowers births at the newly covered ages; every study that followed those women to the end of their childbearing years found the births made up later.

**The one number to carry away: zero.** That is the estimated effect on completed fertility at ages 39 and 45 in the two studies that measured it, corroborated by a third country's cohorts being significantly *higher* at 20 after being lower at 14–15. The timing effect is real and is about half a year of delay in age at first birth.

Three qualifications belong inside this verdict.

That zero is the least well-documented number in the chapter. It appears in the previous draft as narrative assertion and in the extraction table not at all; all 17 extracted effects are timing outcomes. The verdict rests on it, and it should be extracted before anyone relies on this chapter.

The verdict is stated on completed cohort fertility. On a period-TFR definition of the target phenomena the answer would differ, because a pure tempo effect depresses period rates by arithmetic while postponement is under way. The review has not ruled which definition governs, and this chapter cannot settle it.

The timing finding is not diminished by any of this. It is the most credible causal result in the three chapters rewritten so far, and it belongs in a chapter about adolescent outcomes rather than about the fertility decline.

**What would change it:** a reform cohort followed to completion with an extracted, standard-error-bearing null — or, decisively, any reform found to lower completed fertility. Thailand's catch-up is the observation that would have to be overturned.

## Provenance and standing caveats

This chapter is written on 10 of 10 wanted full texts (100%).

Unlike the other chapters rewritten in this pass, that figure is meaningful: every stage from 1,255 citation-frame records to 10 approved studies has a recorded count, and every approved full text was obtained. **The finding that would survive full retrieval is the age profile** — effects concentrated at legally covered ages and absent afterwards — which is unanimous across ten independent settings and does not depend on any unread material. **The finding that might not is the completed-fertility zero**, not because more studies would overturn it but because it has never been extracted: without standard errors it cannot be distinguished from an underpowered null, and the whole verdict rests on it.

**Objection over which this chapter was written.** None recorded from the PI. The stage-13 readability review was deferred at Alexandra's request to TICK-039 and remains outstanding. This one separates them — NOT ASSESSED for the FDT because the cell is empty, NEGLIGIBLE for the SDT because the relevant quantity was measured and came out zero.
