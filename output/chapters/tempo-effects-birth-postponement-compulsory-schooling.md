# Compulsory Schooling, Maternal Education, and Birth Postponement

**Parent hypothesis:** Tempo Effects and Birth Postponement  
**Placement:** Temple/tempo effect, first postponement mechanism  
**Category:** Demographic  
**Primary mechanism:** Compulsory schooling changes a prospective mother's own education and keeps
her enrolled during adolescence, reducing teenage pregnancy or births and postponing first birth.  
**Target phenomena:** FDT and SDT  
**Draft status:** Complete nested-driver draft; protocol stage-13 readability review deferred to
TICK-039 at Alexandra's request.

## 1. The claim

The claim is that compulsory schooling postpones motherhood by changing the prospective mother's
own schooling. Raising the leaving age can reduce teenage pregnancy or birth while the mandate is
binding, delay marriage and sexual activity, or create longer-run changes in knowledge, partners,
and employment opportunities. These channels concern when women have children. They do not imply
that completed family size falls by the same amount.

This is distinct from the child-economic-value hypothesis. That hypothesis asks whether parents
have fewer children because schooling prevents those future children from working. The present
stream instead asks what happens to a girl or young woman whose own education is extended.

## 2. Theoretical mechanism

Four channels can connect compulsory schooling to birth timing.

First, **incapacitation** operates while the mandate is binding. Attendance changes time use,
supervision, peers, and opportunities for pregnancy. A particularly direct test asks whether
pregnancies fall during school terms but not holidays.

Second, schooling can postpone **marriage or stable partnership formation**. Where marriage is the
usual gateway to childbearing, later marriage mechanically delays first birth.

Third, additional education can change **human capital, knowledge, and preferences**. Better
contraceptive knowledge, different partners, greater bargaining power, and higher labor-market
returns can extend the effect beyond the compulsory ages.

Fourth, an early decline can be followed by **catch-up**. If births prevented at ages 16--18 occur
at ages 19--21, period fertility falls temporarily while completed cohort fertility remains nearly
unchanged. Catch-up is central to the Temple/tempo interpretation: a temporary shortage of births
can depress period TFR without a commensurate change in lifetime births.

## 3. Search strategy and routing

The evidence comes from the shared compulsory-education GACS search. Seven independently sourced
anchors generated a forward/backward citation frame of 1,255 DOI/title-deduplicated records. A
blinded title/abstract screen classified 233 records Relevant, 128 Uncertain, and 894 Not relevant.
The focused exception sheet contained 42 records, and the strict policy/outcome gate retained 28
retrieval candidates before version collapse and final mechanism routing.

Alexandra's relevance review produced 10 distinct papers for the compulsory-schooling teenage-
pregnancy/birth stream. All 10 published or best-available versions were retrieved and extracted.
Cummins is not one of those 10 tempo-set records, but Alexandra's later full-text mechanism review
routed it to decreased teenage pregnancy; it is therefore used as adjacent completed-fertility
context and is not counted as an eleventh approved tempo study.

This is GACS citation-frame accounting, not the protocol's eventual Phase 1 keyword-search plus
Phase 2 snowball PRISMA flow. The counts are reported transparently and are not relabeled as a
conventional database search.

## 6. Quantitative synthesis

The effect-level extraction contains 17 estimates from the 10 approved studies. The direction is
consistent at newly covered ages: compulsory schooling reduces teenage births or postpones first
birth. The effects cannot be coefficient-pooled responsibly. The estimates mix age-specific and
cumulative outcomes, probability points and relative percentages, reduced forms and IV effects,
different school-law contrasts, and different age windows.

The prespecified pooling audit groups the estimates into age at first birth, age-specific birth
probability, cumulative first-birth probability, and cumulative teen motherhood. No family has
three independent studies with both a comparable estimate and standard error. A forest plot or
pooled coefficient would therefore create false precision. The correct synthesis is a structured
comparison of effect direction, magnitude, and age profile.

The age profiles are more informative than a pooled average. Geruso and Royer find effects at
16--17 but not 18--19. DeCicca and Krashinsky find concentration at 17--18. Wilson's effect fades
after age 20. Thailand shows explicit post-school catch-up. These patterns support postponement,
not a uniform permanent reduction in births.

## 7. Which maternal-education mechanism is supported?

The strongest direct evidence for incapacitation comes from Adamecz-Völgyi and Scharle: pregnancy
falls during the school year but not during summer or Christmas breaks. Geruso and Royer similarly
find that conceptions, rather than abortions, explain the British decline in teenage births.

Turkey provides the clearest marriage pathway. The reform first reduces teenage marriage, and the
birth effect follows marriage timing. India points in another direction: reductions in sexual
activity, marriage, and first birth persist or strengthen beyond the compulsory ages, and the paper
emphasizes knowledge, contraception, and human capital rather than simple physical attendance.

The literature therefore does not support a single universal maternal-education channel. Direct
attendance is persuasive for short-run effects at legally covered ages; delayed marriage mediates
some settings; longer-run human-capital and knowledge effects matter elsewhere.

## 8. Completed fertility and demographic significance

The evidence does not justify treating fewer teenage births as equally fewer lifetime births.
Geruso and Royer find precisely no completed-family-size effect by age 45. Arendt and coauthors find
no effect on having children or the number of children by age 39. Thailand directly documents
catch-up after school. Cummins' adjacent reform evidence also rules out a large completed marital-
fertility effect.

The compulsory-schooling stream is thus demographically meaningful for teenage-birth timing and
age at first birth. Its aggregate contribution to the FDT or SDT cannot be classified as significant
under the protocol. The extracted studies do not supply a common change in age-specific fertility
rates that can be combined across exposed population shares and mapped into a period-TFR
decomposition with uncertainty. Nor is there a common treatment scale for a valid slope-
sufficiency calculation. Aggregate demographic significance remains Insufficient data.

## 9. Risk of bias and GRADE

The strongest studies use regression discontinuities around birth-date or cohort cutoffs, verify
schooling first stages, and measure births in administrative records. Remaining risks include
endogenous state-law adoption, exclusion restrictions when laws instrument education, birth-date
heaping and donut-hole choices, staggered-treatment comparisons, and survey-based fertility
measurement.

**GRADE: Moderate (unanimous three-rater panel).** Multiple quasi-experiments across the United
Kingdom, North America, Turkey, Hungary, Thailand, India, and Denmark converge on delayed teenage
births. Direct term-time and conception evidence strengthens the causal interpretation. Different
channels and age windows, residual bias in some designs, and later catch-up prevent a High rating.

## 10. Verdict

| Phenomenon | Causal credibility | Demographic significance | Bottom line |
|---|---|---|---|
| FDT | Moderate for teenage-birth timing | Insufficient data for aggregate significance | Maternal own-schooling credibly postpones early births, but its contribution to the total FDT decline is not scaled. |
| SDT | Moderate for teenage-birth timing | Insufficient data for aggregate significance | The driver explains some postponement, not an equivalent decline in completed fertility. |

The concise conclusion is that compulsory schooling is a credible cause of later first birth. The
evidence is strongest for births delayed during newly mandatory school years and weaker for any
permanent reduction in family size.

## 11. Open questions and recommended studies

The most useful next analysis would harmonize reforms onto age-specific fertility rates and combine
them with the share of women actually bound by each reform. That would permit a period-TFR tempo
decomposition without treating IV effects per year of schooling as population-wide policy effects.

Future studies should report conception, marriage, first birth, later catch-up, and completed
fertility for the same cohorts. Term-versus-holiday contrasts and administrative birth records are
especially valuable for separating incapacitation from human-capital channels.
