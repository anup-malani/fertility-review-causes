# A.24 — Dating Apps and Union-Formation Friction

**Category:** Proximate cause (A) · **Registry:** HYPOTHESES-v5.md §A.24 · **Slug:** `dating-apps-union-formation-friction`

**Primary mechanism.** Online dating platforms enlarge the pool of potential partners while lowering
the cost of leaving any particular one, so people search longer, commit less, and form fewer stable
partnerships — and because births in rich countries occur overwhelmingly inside stable partnerships,
fewer partnerships mean fewer children.

**Cross-references.** C.7.a (marriage market and assortative mating) owns friction that comes from the
structure of the market — unequal numbers of men and women, gaps in education. This chapter owns
friction created by the technology's own design. C.2.h (digital leisure substitution) owns the
smartphone's effect on how time and attention are spent; A.14 (coital frequency) owns how often people
have sex; A.7 (age at marriage) owns the step from partnership to birth, which this chapter imports
rather than estimates.

**Status.** TICK-071 · drafted 2026-08-24 · scope frozen 2026-08-24 with all five PI calls ruled ·
**not yet PI-reviewed** · **written on 33 of 152 wanted full texts (22%)**.

## 1. The claim

### 1.1 In plain terms

This chapter explores the effect of dating apps on the number of children people have.

In plain terms: before the apps, a person looking for a partner might meet a few dozen plausible
candidates in a lifetime — through work, friends, a neighbourhood, a church. Now they can see
thousands, and a new one whenever they like.

That could cut either way, and the two directions are the whole argument. Being able to meet far more
people should mean more couples form, because the search is easier. But it could also mean fewer,
because when thousands of alternatives are visible, the person in front of you looks less like the
best available option and it starts to make sense to keep looking. **The claim in this chapter is that
the second effect wins** — that the apps have made people harder to settle down with, so fewer stable
couples form and fewer children are born.

There is a problem with almost all the evidence, and it is worth seeing before any of it is described.
Most studies of online dating survey people who are *in couples* and ask how they met. But the claim
is about people who never got to a couple at all. Someone who spent three years on the apps, had many
matches and many dates, and formed no lasting relationship, does not appear in a survey of couples.
**They are exactly the person the claim is about, and they are exactly the person the evidence cannot
see.**

### 1.2 The claim precisely

The registry states that online dating platforms "increase the volume of potential contacts but reduce
conversion to committed partnerships — via match-abundance effects, strategic delay ('why settle?'),
and the commodification of potential partners — lowering pair-bond formation rates and downstream
fertility." The registered target phenomenon is the Second Demographic Transition (the fertility
decline in rich countries from roughly 1965 to the present), and only that one.

The claim is a chain of three links, and they are in very different empirical states.

| Link | Statement | State of the evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | app adoption changes partner-search behaviour | measured, mostly in laboratories and on platform logs |
| 2 | that behaviour changes the rate at which stable partnerships form | measured, and the field estimates run in both directions |
| 3 | fewer partnerships mean fewer births | owned by A.7; this chapter imports it |

Under PI Call 1, ruled on 2026-08-24, this chapter grades link 2 and imports link 3 with its
uncertainty stated. It does not grade the composition of all three, because nobody has estimated it.

**The parameter this chapter estimates is the change in the probability that a person forms a stable
co-residential partnership caused by exposure to app-mediated partner search, measured in percentage
points of partnership formation.** The counterfactual is a person with the same characteristics, in the
same partner market, searching without app mediation. The sign convention: a negative number means
apps reduce partnership formation, which is what the registry asserts.

Two further scoping rulings shape what follows. Pre-app online dating, from the mid-1990s to 2012, is
in scope (PI Call 3), because excluding it would delete every quasi-experimental estimate that exists;
the app-era subset is therefore reported separately rather than pooled across a technological break.
And the broadband and internet-access studies are shared with C.2.h rather than routed away from this
chapter (PI Call 2), because they are the only identified evidence either chapter has.

## 2. Theoretical mechanism

The hypothesis is a claim about a matching market, and it is worth stating in the terms an economist
already owns, because doing so shows immediately where it can be wrong.

App mediation does two things at once. It **thickens the market**: the number of potential partners a
person can evaluate per unit of search time rises by orders of magnitude. And it **lowers the cost of
rejection to near zero**: declining a candidate takes one thumb movement, produces no social
consequence, and is invisible to everyone except the algorithm.

Standard search theory says the first effect raises match formation. The registry's claim is that the
second effect dominates, through three sub-mechanisms it names — abundance raising the reservation
standard, strategic delay when better options are believed to be one swipe away, and the treatment of
people as substitutable goods. All three are versions of one economic statement: **app mediation raises
the option value of continued search relative to the value of the offer in hand.**

That framing makes the falsification conditions explicit. The hypothesis is wrong if match formation
rises with app mediation and conversion to partnership does not fall; it is wrong if the reservation
standard rises but people partner anyway; and it is wrong if the people who search longest are the
people who most want a partner, because then the correlation between app use and singlehood is
selection rather than effect.

This chapter has no accounting identity in it. That distinguishes it from mechanisms like twinning
rates, where arithmetic guarantees part of the answer. Here every link is behavioural, which means
every link can be wrong, and no part of the claim is true by construction.

## 3. Search strategy

The search was built around nine walls, frozen before any screening, and their full statement is in
`literature/search-logs/dating-apps-union-formation-friction-search-scope.md`. Three matter to a
reader of the chapter.

**Two of the walls exclude words rather than ideas.** "Dating" in geochronology means assigning an age
to a rock, and that literature is roughly 64,000 records — larger than everything else in the frame
combined. "Fertility" in agronomy means the productive capacity of soil. Both were excluded, and both
exclusions were verified by counting rather than sampling: across the entire citation neighbourhood of
each, the share of records carrying a human demographic outcome was 0.0% and 0.1% respectively.

**Dating violence is not a word problem and needed a different wall.** Research on abuse in dating
relationships uses "dating" in exactly the sense this chapter means, and it is a literature of some
44,000 records. Only the outcome separates it, so the exclusion was made on outcome and adjudicated
paper by paper.

**One wall was declared unenforceable in advance, and that declaration turned out to be the most
consequential decision in the chapter.** The identified estimates in this area are published under the
vocabulary of technology diffusion — broadband rollout, internet access, cellular coverage — and never
mention dating apps in a title or abstract. Measured on this chapter's own citation frame, of the
records reachable from a technology-diffusion source, 7.6% carry a partnership or fertility outcome
with no app vocabulary at all, against 2.5% carrying app vocabulary. A screen built on the exposure
the registry names cannot see the studies that identify it. The search therefore reached those records
through their citation provenance rather than through their words, and §6 shows why that mattered.

## 5. The ideal design

Written before the literature was read, so §6 can be compared against a fixed yardstick rather than against the best paper that happens to exist.

### 5.1 The ideal estimand

The change in the probability that a **searching** person forms a stable co-residential partnership within a defined window, caused by the availability of app-mediated partner search — estimated on **everyone who was searching**, not on everyone who ended up partnered.

The population clause is the entire specification, and it is what the literature fails almost universally. **The registry's claim is about the rate at which searches convert.** A design that samples on conversion having occurred cannot estimate a conversion rate, whatever it finds about the partnerships that resulted. **The sampling frame must be defined by search, not by outcome.**

The exposure clause matters almost as much. The treatment is **app availability**, a population-level condition — not "met online", which is an individual's realised channel and is a *consequence* of the search process rather than a treatment applied to it.

### 5.2 The design that would identify it

**Source of variation.** Population-level, staggered exposure to app availability: **differential timing of platform entry** across countries or markets, or **differential smartphone or broadband penetration** interacted with the platform-entry date. The unit of treatment is the market, not the person.

**Comparison group.** Comparable markets not yet exposed, or the same market before entry, with the searching population defined **before** exposure.

**Identifying assumption.** Platform entry timing is unrelated to local partnership trends. Falsifiable: pre-trends in partnership formation by market; a placebo on cohorts past the age of partner search; and a check that broadband penetration is not itself proxying for local economic conditions — which is where this chapter's exposure meets **C.2.h's**, and why PI Call 2 exists.

**Estimating equation.** Partnership-formation hazard among the age-eligible unpartnered population, on market-level app availability, with the searching denominator observed. The **fertility** step is imported from A.7 with its uncertainty stated, per PI Call 1 — this chapter grades link 2 and does not compose all three.

**Data required.** A panel of the unpartnered — which is the hard part. Population registers give partnership formation; what they do not give is who was searching. A survey panel that follows single people prospectively, regardless of whether they ever partner, is the missing instrument.

**Sample size.** Ordinary, if the frame is right. **The binding constraint here is the sampling frame, not the sample size**, and no amount of additional data on couples relieves it.

**What the ideal design excludes.** Every venue-comparison design — comparing couples who met online with couples who met offline. It is **silent on the registry's claim by construction**, and it is most of the literature. Also excluded: platform-log studies, which observe behaviour within the app and cannot see whether a partnership formed outside it.

### 5.3 Distance from the ideal

| Design family | Population is searchers, not couples? | Exposure is availability, not "met online"? | Outcome is partnership formation? | Distance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Population-level app-availability designs | **Yes** | **Yes** | Yes | **The ideal — and the cell is empty** |
| Venue-comparison studies (met-online vs met-offline) | **No — conditions on the outcome** | No | Partly | **Silent by construction; most of the literature** |
| Platform-log and laboratory studies | Yes | Partly | **No — behaviour within the app** | Measures link 1 only |
| Broadband-penetration designs | Yes | Partly | Yes | **Closest identified variation — and it is C.2.h's** |

**No study implements the ideal design, and the reason is a sampling frame rather than a shortage of studies.** The literature on online dating is large and much of it is careful. Almost all of it samples couples and asks how they met, which answers a different question from the registered one.

**The one design family with credible identified variation belongs to another chapter.** Broadband and smartphone penetration supply exogenous, staggered, population-level exposure — and under the boundary ruling that variation is **C.2.h's**, because what it identifies is the effect of internet access rather than of app-mediated search specifically. This chapter cannot claim it without claiming C.2.h's estimand.

**So the empty cell here is not "nobody has looked".** It is that the designs that could answer the question either sample on the outcome, or identify a neighbouring treatment.

### 6.1 The naive estimator, and the reason it cannot answer the question

Every literature has a comparison an author would reach for without thinking hard. In this one it is
**people who met their partner online against people who met their partner offline**, and it appears in
the German panel study, the matchmaking study, and — among the works still unread — the best-known
papers in the area.

That comparison conditions on the outcome. To be in either group, a person must have a partner. The
people whose app use produced matches, conversations, dates and no partnership are in neither group;
they are not in the sample at all. **The design asks how successful searches succeeded, and the claim
is about how often searches succeed.**

The direction of the resulting bias is not "one way or the other" — it is that the comparison is
**silent on the registry's claim by construction**. The claim concerns the rate at which searches
convert to partnerships. A design conditioned on conversion having occurred cannot estimate that rate,
whatever it finds about the partnerships that resulted. This cuts against the hypothesis's defenders
and its critics equally: the meeting-venue literature cannot show that apps reduce partnering, and it
cannot show that they do not.

**Which studies escape it?** Only those whose exposure is measured in the whole population rather than
among the partnered — the internet-access and broadband studies. Kolk and Billari observe every young
adult in two nationally representative samples, whether partnered or not, and can therefore ask whether
access changes the probability of partnering at all. The Swedish register study observes every enrolled
woman. **The evidence that survives this problem is exactly the evidence the unenforceable wall was
built to reach**, which is the strongest argument available for having declared that wall unenforceable
rather than screening on the registry's own vocabulary.

Following the review's standing rule, this disagreement is resolved rather than averaged. The
meeting-venue studies and the technology-access studies do not use the same estimator, do not sample
the same population, and do not estimate the same parameter. Pooling them would manufacture a middle
value for a quantity only one group of them measures.

## 7. Quantitative synthesis

### 7.1 The answer in plain terms

Nobody has estimated the effect of dating apps on the number of children people have. One study comes
close, and what it finds is the opposite of the hypothesis: among Germans who met their partners
online, first births were no less likely in the youngest group and **more** likely in the oldest, and
intentions to have a first child were higher, not lower.

The same study then does something unusually candid. Its authors ask whether the arrow might run the
other way — whether wanting a child sends people to look for a partner online, rather than online
partnering producing children. It does, and the backwards relationship is about three times the size of
the forwards one in the same data. People who want children go looking.

The wider evidence about internet access and partnership does not support the hypothesis either,
though it is more interesting than a flat contradiction. Internet access appears to **delay**
partnership formation among people in their late teens and early twenties and to **raise** it from the
mid-twenties onward. On a platform in Japan that handles one marriage in thirty, the efficiency with
which searches turn into engagements roughly tripled over eleven years.

Against all of this stand a genuine mechanism literature showing that abundant choice makes people
pickier and more prone to reject, and one survey finding that exposure to negative online material
about parenthood predicts substantially lower intentions to have children. The mechanism is real. What
has not been shown is that it reaches births.

### 7.2 The estimate

There is no pooled estimate, and constructing one would misrepresent the evidence. No two extracted
studies share an exposure construct, an outcome, or an estimator. What follows is the estimate for each
link the evidence can reach.

**Link 2, technology to partnership.** The direction depends on age and is not summarisable by a single
number. Kolk and Billari find internet access associated with delayed partnership formation at younger
ages and with raised partnership propensity from the mid-to-late twenties, in two independent data
sources and for both same-sex and different-sex partnerships. A summary that reported either sign alone
would be false. The Japanese platform study finds matching efficiency tripling between 2014 and 2025,
which is the most direct measurement of the conversion quantity the registry claims falls — though its
users pay for costly screening and have already decided to marry, so it describes the committed rather
than the casual searcher.

**Link 3, apps to births.** One study. Among the youngest German cohort, the group most exposed to
apps, meeting online had no association with a first birth (coefficient −0.02, p = 0.439, fully
adjusted). Among the oldest cohort the association was positive (+0.11, p = 0.006). Intentions to have
a first child were higher among those who met online (+0.07, p = 0.017). The one result pointing the
registry's way is separation, which was more likely among those who met online (+0.06, p = 0.012) —
and that result loses significance in one of the study's four specifications.

**The reverse relationship, which is the finding this chapter is built around.** In the same data,
higher intentions to have a child predicted meeting a new partner online in the following wave
(β = 0.22, p = 0.001, 95% CI 0.092–0.343). The forward coefficient in that study is 0.07. **The
backwards relationship is roughly three times the forwards one.**

**Wider technology evidence.** Across OECD countries between 2000 and 2021, broadband penetration is
robustly associated with **higher** realised fertility, though at country-year level almost everything
that trends with broadband sits in the residual. In Swedish register data, being enrolled at university
cuts the first-birth conception hazard by 70% for campus students but only 43% for distance students,
so the online mode attenuates the postponement that education causes.

**Mechanism evidence.** Choice abundance produces measurable rejection: experimental work finds more
options lead to more searching and worse choices, and a rejection mindset that cumulates. None of it
reaches a demographic outcome. The one survey linking online content to fertility intentions finds
negative content strongly predictive (β = −0.749) and positive content weakly so (β = +0.058), from a
cross-sectional sample of 314 students who reported both their reading and their intentions at the same
moment.

### 7.3 Quality of the evidence

Of thirteen extracted studies, **seven carry critical risk of bias and five serious. None is rated low
or moderate.** Two features of that assessment are specific to this chapter and are reported as their
own domains.

The first is reverse causality, which was tested in one study of the thirteen and found there. The
second is the distance between the exposure a study measures and the exposure the registry names.
**Not one readable study measures dating-app use.** Four measure internet access, four measure media or
content exposure, two measure platform intermediation, one measures the venue where a couple met, and
one measures the delivery mode of a university course. The trade is systematic rather than accidental:
the better identified a study is, the further its exposure sits from the hypothesis, because
identification comes from broadband rollout, which is upstream of the technology in question. A body of
evidence can be individually sound and collectively off-target.

## 8. Demographic significance

**The phenomenon to be explained is measured in whole children per woman; this mechanism offers a
change in the probability of forming a partnership, which reaches children only through an elasticity
this chapter does not estimate.**

That statement settles more than it appears to. Even granting the registry's mechanism in full, its
contribution to fertility is the product of three terms — the share of the fertility decline running
through partnership rather than through births within partnerships, the share of the change in
partnership attributable to app mediation, and the elasticity of births with respect to partnership.
**No term in that product is estimated anywhere in the readable evidence.** What follows therefore
bounds the mechanism rather than measuring it.

**The denominator, per `PROTOCOL.md` §4.2.1.** Any future share is computed against the *fall* in
completed fertility across the SDT — roughly one and a half births per woman, a conventional magnitude
not computed here — and not against the fertility level, and not against the change over the years
since the apps appeared. **The last of those is the live hazard for this chapter**: the apps are a
late-arriving exposure, so a share taken against the post-2012 change would use a much smaller
denominator and flatter the mechanism, in exactly the way B.7's timing wall describes.

### 8.1 Pre-modern variation

For pre-modern fertility variation, the verdict is **NOT ASSESSED**, because the registry restricts
this hypothesis to the Second Demographic Transition and the technology did not exist.

### 8.2 First Demographic Transition

For the First Demographic Transition, the verdict is **NOT ASSESSED**, for the same reason.

### 8.3 Second Demographic Transition

For the Second Demographic Transition, the verdict is **NOT ASSESSED — insufficient evidence**, because
the parameter required to compute a contribution has not been estimated by any study this chapter could
read, and the one study reporting both an app-mediated exposure and a birth outcome finds no
association in the cohort most exposed and a positive association in the least exposed.

Two bounds are worth stating even so, because they constrain what any future estimate could show.

**The timing bound.** Tinder launched in September 2012 and swipe-based applications diffused over
roughly 2013 to 2016. The United States total fertility rate — the number of children a woman would
bear if she experienced current age-specific birth rates throughout her reproductive life — turned down
in 2007, from about 2.12, and reached roughly 1.62 by 2023. Slightly under half of that decline had
already occurred before swipe-based apps existed at all. A mechanism that begins in 2013 cannot explain
a decline that began in 2007, and its maximum possible share is the post-2012 remainder, before any
question of effect size arises. **These figures are taken from published national vital-statistics
series and have not yet been recomputed from source data in this repository; that recomputation is on
the residual list and the bound should be treated as provisional.**

**The adoption bound.** App adoption is neither universal nor uniform, and it does not track fertility
decline across countries. South Korea, which has the lowest fertility in the world and adopts consumer
technology unusually fast, uses dating applications at a comparatively low rate. Any account in which
app diffusion drives fertility decline has to explain why the country with the most extreme decline is
not among the heaviest users.

### 8.4 Is the mechanism itself caused by the phenomenon?

Partly, and it matters. People adopt dating applications because partnership formation has already
become harder and later — because the age at first marriage has risen, because more young adults live
alone or with parents, and because the workplace and neighbourhood have weakened as places to meet. To
the extent that app adoption is a **response** to delayed partnership rather than a cause of it, the
correlation between app use and singlehood is a feedback of the phenomenon rather than evidence about
its cause. The German panel study's reverse coefficient is the same point measured at the individual
level.

## 9. GRADE rating

**GRADE** (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations) is the standard scheme for rating how much confidence a body of evidence supports. It starts from the designs available and moves down one level for each named defect.

| Phenomenon | Link | Starting level | Downgrades | Rating |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pre-modern | — | — | out of registry scope | Not applicable |
| First Demographic Transition | — | — | out of registry scope | Not applicable |
| Second Demographic Transition | Link 2: technology → partnership | Low (observational) | risk of bias (7 critical, 5 serious); indirectness (no study measures the registered exposure); inconsistency (direction reverses with age) | **VERY LOW** |
| Second Demographic Transition | Link 3: app exposure → births | — | one study; critical risk of bias on reverse causality, demonstrated by its own authors | **INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE** |

Under PI Call 4, an empty or near-empty primary cell earns *insufficient evidence* rather than a graded
finding of no effect. The distinction is not a formality. "No evidence that dating apps reduce
fertility" and "evidence that dating apps do not reduce fertility" are different statements, and only
the first is supported here.

## 10. Verdict

**No study has estimated the effect of dating apps on fertility.** The single study that reports both
an app-mediated exposure and a birth outcome — a German panel of 8,177 people followed from 2009 to
2016 — finds no association with first births in the cohort most exposed to apps, a positive
association in the oldest cohort, and higher intentions to have a first child among those who met their
partners online. Its authors test whether the causation runs backwards and find that it does: wanting a
child predicts subsequent online partner-seeking about three times as strongly as online partnering
predicts a child.

**The number to carry away is the one that is missing.** Of 10,739 records assembled from the citation
neighbourhood of this literature, **two report both an app-mediated exposure and a fertility outcome,
and they are one study and its working paper.**

The wider evidence on internet access and partnership does not support the hypothesis and is more
informative than a simple contradiction. Access appears to delay partnership formation in the late
teens and early twenties and to raise it from the mid-twenties, so the honest summary is that the
technology moves the **timing** of partnership rather than its eventual level — which is a claim about
tempo, not about how many children are ever born. On the largest structured matching platform in
Japan, the efficiency of converting search into engagement tripled between 2014 and 2025.

The mechanism the registry names is real. Abundant choice does make people pickier, rejection on these
platforms is cheap and frequent, and the psychological literature measuring this is substantial and
competent. What has not been shown is that any of it reaches births — and roughly half of the fertility
decline this hypothesis is offered to explain had already happened before swipe-based applications
existed.

**What would change this verdict.** A study measuring app exposure at the population level rather than
among the already-partnered, with exogenous variation in adoption and a birth outcome. Nothing in the
readable evidence has that shape. Four unread studies might: an event-study of Tinder diffusion against
United States marriage outcomes, a working paper on digital technology and the post-2007 fertility
decline, an estimate of broadband diffusion on teenage fertility, and an estimate of adolescent
internet exposure on age at first marriage. All four are on the procurement list, and the second could
reverse the direction reported here for the technology-diffusion evidence.

## 11. Open questions

**Retrieval.** The chapter's first priority is the fifteen records in the top procurement band, four of
which are named in the verdict. The chapter cannot be finalised on 22% coverage; the finding about the
empty cell can be, because that finding is about the frame rather than about the studies.

**One PI sub-question remains.** PI Call 2 put the broadband and internet-access studies in both this
chapter's evidence base and C.2.h's. It did not say whether both chapters may report the same
magnitude in their demographic-significance sections. The working rule adopted is a shared evidence
base with a single claimant on magnitude, named at synthesis; if that is wrong, the place to change it
is the scope document's Call 2 paragraph.

**Studies that do not exist and should.** No study measures app adoption in a population sample and
follows partnership formation among users and non-users. No study exploits the staggered entry of
platforms into national markets. And no study separates the two forces this chapter opened with — the
fall in search costs and the rise in the reservation standard — although platform data could identify
both, since both are recorded in the logs.

**A template question raised by this chapter.** The demographic-significance vocabulary offers
*negligible*, *minor*, *substantial*, *dominant* and *not assessed*, and defines the last as
out-of-registry-scope. This chapter needed a sixth case: in scope, but with no estimated parameter to
compute a share from. It has been recorded as *not assessed — insufficient evidence*, and the template
should decide whether that is a distinct verdict or a variant of the fifth.
