**Category:** Economic  
**Main idea:** Where children are how a person survives old age, giving people another way to fund
retirement makes an extra child less necessary, so fertility falls.  
**Second idea, running the other way:** A pension also decides when grandparents stop working, and a
retired grandmother is available childcare, which makes children cheaper for her adult daughter and
can raise fertility.  
**Fertility patterns this chapter tests the hypothesis against:** pre-modern fertility variation, the
First Demographic Transition (roughly 1870 to 1965), and the Second Demographic Transition (1965 to
the present). We abbreviate them PM, FDT, and SDT.

## 1. Verdict

A pension moves fertility in two opposite directions at once. Treating "pensions" as a single
treatment makes this literature look self-contradictory, and the contradiction dissolves as soon as
the two directions are separated.

A couple in a village with no pension system, no insurer worth the name, and no bank they would trust
for thirty years has exactly one retirement plan: their children. A grown son sends money home, and a
grown daughter takes them in once they can no longer farm. A child is partly an investment. Give that
couple a pension, and the pension does the job the child was going to do, so one reason to have
another child disappears and fertility falls. Economists call this crowd-out: the new asset crowds
children out of the job of supporting a person in old age. This chapter calls it the
**retirement-asset channel**, because it turns on children being an asset held for retirement.

The same pension also decides when a grandmother stops working. A grandmother still at her job cannot
mind her daughter's baby on a Tuesday afternoon; a retired grandmother can. Childcare supplied free by
a grandparent lowers what a child costs the adult daughter, and cheaper children mean more of them.
This chapter calls that the **grandparent-time channel**, because the pension changes how a
grandparent spends her days. Expand pension coverage and the first channel pushes fertility down while
the second pushes it up, so this chapter never averages the two together.

The evidence supports a narrow version of the retirement-asset argument. Fertility falls where
children still plausibly insure old age and a credible substitute for them arrives. Settings outside
that condition do not line up: long-term-care insurance in China, social insurance in Bismarck's
Prussia, and U.S. Social Security during the baby boom do not all show the same negative fertility
response. The response depends on which kind of old-age security changes and on the demographic
setting in which it changes.

State pensions did not cause the First Demographic Transition, because most of them arrived after
that decline had already begun. The better-timed version of the argument runs through financial
development. Banks and savings instruments gave households a way to carry money from their working
years into old age without a child in the middle, and those institutions spread early enough to
matter (Basso, Bodenhorn, and Cuberes 2014).

The Second Demographic Transition poses a different question: whether pension policy still moves
fertility once public pensions and private savings are already everywhere. Rich low-fertility
countries leave the retirement-asset channel little room, because the substitute for children is
already in place. The stronger evidence in those settings supports the grandparent-time channel. More
grandparent availability raises fertility, and later retirement lowers it.

Confidence in the causal claim is rated on GRADE, the four-step scale this review uses throughout:
Very low, Low, Moderate, High, with in-between ratings such as Low-to-moderate where the evidence
splits. How much of the observed fertility change a hypothesis could account for is a separate
judgment, and a well-identified effect can be entirely real and still too small, or too narrow in
reach, to move a national birth rate.

|---|---|---|---|

## 2. The claim and the two channels

The old-age-security argument starts from one fact about households without pensions: an old person
who can no longer work has to be supported by somebody. Children are the somebody. A couple in such a
place, deciding whether to have a fourth child, is deciding in part how well they will eat at seventy.
Give them a public pension, a private insurance policy, or a bank that will still be there in thirty
years, and the retirement return to a fourth child falls. Fertility should fall with it.

The grandparent-time channel starts from the same policy and reaches the opposite prediction.
Retirement changes how much free time an older adult has, and a retired grandparent who supplies
childcare lowers the price of a child to that grandparent's own adult children. One pension reform can
therefore raise fertility in the same country where it lowers fertility through the retirement-asset
channel.

### 2.1 Children as a retirement plan

The older demographic literature treats children as part of a transfer system between generations
rather than only as something parents enjoy having. Neher (1971), Willis (1980), and Nugent (1985)
state that view most directly, and Caldwell (1976) and Cigno (1993) connect it to broader theory about
transfers between generations. The fertility decision then turns on the expected old-age return to a
child, counting cash support, personal care, and insurance against bad luck.

Caldwell's (1976) wealth-flow argument frames the demographic transition by asking which way money
runs between generations. High fertility can pay for itself when the net flow runs from children to
parents. When the flow reverses, or when a non-child asset takes over the retirement job, the private
demand for another birth should fall. Testing the channel therefore means comparing families that gain
a non-child source of old-age support with otherwise similar families that do not, and the prediction
is strongest where formal pensions, private savings, and insurance markets are thin.

### 2.2 What the formal models add

Formal fertility models supply the policy lever. Becker (1960), Becker and Barro (1988), and Barro and
Becker (1989) set the background framework, in which parents choose how many children to have given
their resources, their preferences, and what a child costs. Ehrlich and Lui (1991), Nishimura and
Zhang (1992), and Sinn (2004) then model how public pensions and private family transfers interact
with those choices, and Boldrin, De Nardi, and Jones (2015) tie the mechanisms to social-security
systems more directly. A "pay-as-you-go" pension system in this literature is one in which today's
workers' taxes pay today's retirees' benefits, rather than one in which each worker funds an account
of their own.

These models show why a public pension can move fertility even among households that plan ahead
carefully. A pay-as-you-go system replaces the implicit family contract under which children support
parents, and it changes who has to be trusted: a child no longer has to repay their parents directly
once the state taxes the next generation and mails the check itself. The transfer between generations
does not disappear in any of these models. It moves from a household contract to a state contract, and
fertility falls because an additional child no longer delivers the same private retirement return to
the parent.

## 3. Sorting studies by the question they answer

A paper can be about pensions and about fertility and still answer the wrong causal question. The
question this chapter asks is narrow: what happens to fertility when a household's non-child old-age
security rises for reasons outside that household's control. A pension study whose outcome is savings,
schooling, or elderly health tests a link in the mechanism without measuring the fertility effect. A
study in which fertility causes pension take-up or insurance purchase runs the arrow backwards. A
study in which pension eligibility changes grandparents' childcare time belongs to the
grandparent-time channel, which predicts the opposite sign and cannot be averaged with the first.

| Group of studies | What it measures | Role in the chapter |
|---|---|---|
| Children as old-age support | Non-child old-age security affects fertility by reducing the retirement value of children. | Direct evidence on whether pensions, insurance, or financial markets reduce fertility by replacing children as old-age support. |
| Mechanism evidence | Children affect private insurance, assets, or old-age support. | Evidence on whether children behave like old-age-security assets. It informs the mechanism but is not pooled with fertility effects. |
| Grandparental childcare | Pension or retirement policy changes childcare help and fertility. | Evidence on whether retirement policy raises fertility by increasing grandparents' childcare availability. |
| Indirect links | Pensions affect savings, schooling, migration, coresidence, health, or other links short of fertility. | Background evidence on the causal chain. It updates mechanism plausibility without estimating fertility effects. |

## 4. How the studies were found and kept

The search began broad, covering old-age security, pensions, and fertility, and one inclusion rule did
most of the screening: a paper enters the review only if it identifies one of the four questions in
the table above. Studies testing whether children behave like a retirement asset are kept and held out
of the fertility pool. Historical and aggregate studies are kept for what they say about timing and
about whether results carry to other settings, and their higher risk of confounding is carried into
the bias assessment rather than into the pooled numbers. Section 13 lists the search logs, extraction
sheets, and files behind every number here.

## 5. The studies

### 5.1 Studies on replacing children as a retirement plan

this channel. The tenth, a Ghana working paper retrieved late, enters provisionally and stays out of
the pooled summaries below.

LTCI in that table is long-term-care insurance, which pays for nursing and personal care in old age
rather than for cash income. CMBR is the crude marital birth rate, births per married woman in a year.

Each study contributes its own preferred estimate where the paper names one, in the paper's own units
and with the paper's own measure of uncertainty. Every estimate is oriented so that a negative number
means lower fertility after an increase in non-child old-age security. One limit applies to all of
them: a pension expansion raises household income at the same time as it replaces children as
retirement insurance, and higher income can raise fertility on its own, so each estimate is the net of
two forces the design does not separate.

### 5.2 Evidence set aside from the fertility question

Ci (2024) asks whether older parents with more children buy less private insurance, less private
pension coverage, and less private health insurance. The outcome is insurance purchase rather than
fertility, so the study speaks to whether children and purchased old-age security are substitutes and
not to whether old-age security changes births. Ruthbah (2022), from Bangladesh, is a candidate
addition to that mechanism evidence rather than part of the fertility synthesis. The same treatment
applies to the indirect evidence on what pensions do to household savings, to children's schooling,
and to whether the generations live under one roof: those studies establish that intermediate links
exist and measure no fertility effect.

### 5.3 Studies on grandparent time

Three studies anchor the grandparent-time channel, and the SDT case rests on them: Eibich and Siedler
(2020) in Germany, Ilciukas (2023) in the Netherlands, and Akyol and Atalay (2025) in Australia. None
of the three asks whether parents use children as pensions. Each asks whether pension or retirement
rules change how much childcare a grandparent can supply, and whether that change then shows up in the
adult child's births.

Comparing the three requires first turning them all the same way around. Two of the reforms made
grandparents more available: Eibich and Siedler study German parents reaching retirement, and Akyol
and Atalay study Australian grandmothers becoming eligible for a pension. The Dutch reform Ilciukas
studies did the opposite. It pushed the retirement age up, so mothers stayed at work and were less
available to mind a grandchild. Left as its authors reported it, the Dutch estimate answers the
question "what happens when grandparents become less available," while the other two answer "what
happens when they become more available." Three studies pointed at two opposite questions would look
like disagreement even if all three described identical behavior. Flipping the sign on the Dutch
estimate points all three at one question. A finding that less grandparent availability lowers
fertility becomes, after the flip, a finding that more grandparent availability raises it. The flip
changes the direction the comparison is stated in and leaves the estimate's size and precision
untouched. With all three pointed the same way, all three imply that more grandparent availability
raises fertility.

A retirement-age reform changes more than a grandmother's schedule. Working longer also raises her own
earnings and her eventual pension, and a mother who transfers money to her daughter instead of time
can move that daughter's fertility in either direction. None of the three designs separates the hours
a grandmother no longer supplies from the income she gains by supplying them elsewhere.

All three settings are rich countries whose fertility sits below replacement, meaning below about 2.1
births per woman, the level at which one generation roughly replaces itself. That classification comes
from the total fertility rate, or TFR, which is the number of children a woman would have if she lived
her whole life through the birth rates observed in a single calendar year.

Set against the fertility declines their own countries recorded over the same years, Ilciukas's
Netherlands estimates and Akyol and Atalay's Australia estimates are large. Eibich and Siedler's
Germany estimates support the same direction and are left unscaled, because German TFR rises slightly
across their 1984-2017 German Socio-Economic Panel window, so there is no decline to divide into.
Neither comparison delivers a share of any country's fertility decline, which would require knowing
how many grandmothers each reform actually bound and how their individual responses add up nationally.

## 6. Putting the numbers together

The synthesis keeps every estimate close to the way its own paper reported it, comparing effects only
after preserving each paper's outcome wording, treatment contrast, uncertainty, and follow-up window.
Unit conversions are mechanical: percentage-point effects are re-expressed as probabilities, and
completed-fertility estimates stay in births per woman.

### 6.1 The two pooled summaries

Two pooled summaries are reported for the retirement-asset channel, each within an outcome family and
never across the pension literature as a whole. One family asks whether a woman gave birth in a given
year, which is a probability between 0 and 1. The other asks how many children a woman had in total by
the end of her childbearing years, which is a count of births. An average taken across a probability
and a count of births would be a number in no units at all.

The birth-probability summary pools five independent studies. More non-child old-age security is
associated with a 0.006954 lower probability of birth (SE 0.002037; 95% CI -0.010946 to -0.002963), a
statistically significant reduction. Out of every 1,000 women in a year, about seven fewer give birth.

The completed-fertility summary pools three independent studies. More non-child old-age security is
associated with 0.067672 fewer births per woman (SE 0.026971; 95% CI -0.120536 to -0.014808), again
statistically significant. Roughly seven-hundredths of a birth per woman works out to one birth fewer
for about every fifteen women.

Neither summary is one structural pension effect. The birth-probability pool mixes pension expansion,
pension-value exposure, pension participation, pension cuts, and long-term-care insurance. The
completed-fertility pool mixes pension-value exposure, pension cuts, and pension participation. Both
numbers therefore summarize direction and rough magnitude inside an outcome family, and reading either
one still requires knowing which treatment produced it, in which setting, and how good the
identification was. A single coefficient for "the effect of pensions" would require at least three
independent studies sharing one mechanism, one outcome, one set of units, and one treatment scale, and
the current evidence does not supply them. Binary pension expansions, continuous pension-value
exposure, pension cuts, long-term-care insurance pilots, financial access, and broad social spending
do not define a common treatment contrast.

The Ghana working paper agrees with the developing-country pattern and stays out of both summaries.
Zelu, Iranzo, and Pérez-Laborda (2023) compare private-sector women newly covered by Ghana's Act 766
reform with public-sector women who already had more generous pensions. Their controlled
difference-in-differences estimate is a 6-percentage-point reduction in the probability of pregnancy
in the previous year (SE 3 percentage points; Table 2, p. 14). Their controlled full-sample estimate
for the number of children is negative and too imprecise to separate from zero (Table 7, p. 17). Both
estimates stay provisional, because the paper is a working draft that has not completed this chapter's
extraction and risk-of-bias review.

## 7. How much of the fertility change this explains

Each study window is matched to where fertility stood relative to replacement in that country. A
setting whose first and last available in-window TFR readings both lie above 2.1 is coded
FDT-relevant. A setting whose readings both lie below 2.1 is coded SDT-relevant. A window that crosses
from above to below replacement during the study is coded FDT|SDT. The rule makes Brazil a crossing
case, Namibia and the postwar United States above-replacement cases, and Italy plus the China pension
studies below-replacement cases. Prussia, Imperial Germany, and the 1850 U.S. financial-development
evidence have no in-window TFR coverage and remain historical cases.

### 7.1 Pre-modern fertility variation

No direct pre-modern causal evidence exists. The channel almost certainly mattered as a background
condition, since a world without public pensions, formal insurance, or deep financial markets leaves
children as the only old-age support on offer. A universal condition does not explain variation. If
nearly everyone depends on children for old age, that dependence explains why fertility was high in
general better than it explains why one population differed from another.

The pre-modern verdict is therefore low. Mortality, marriage systems, and inheritance rules are more
plausible explanations for the differences across pre-modern populations, and old-age security reads
better as a baseline motive than as the main source of those differences.

### 7.2 First Demographic Transition

State pensions arrive too late to have started the First Demographic Transition. The Western decline
is under way in the late nineteenth century. Bismarck's pension system dates to the late 1880s, and
U.S. Social Security begins in 1935. Couples who were already having fewer children before either law
existed cannot have been responding to it, so a reform of that vintage can deepen or redirect a
decline in progress and cannot have begun one.

Financial development is the stronger FDT route. If the real mechanism is a non-child way to provide
for old age, households did not need a state pension to get one. They needed a bank or an insurer they
could trust across thirty years. Basso, Bodenhorn, and Cuberes (2014) speak directly to that margin in
the nineteenth-century United States, which makes financial development the better-timed substitute
for children in the FDT.

The FDT verdict is partial. The channel is plausible and empirically supported, and state pensions are
too late and their early coverage too thin to be the main cause, so the best version of the FDT
argument is broader asset substitution with financial markets doing more of the work than public
pensions.

### 7.3 Second Demographic Transition

The retirement-asset channel is weak as an explanation of the rich-country SDT. A pension system that
matured decades before the SDT began has little variation left to study, because whatever it was going
to do to the retirement value of children it had already done. The predicted direction also runs
backwards. Trimming pension generosity or pushing eligibility later should raise fertility through this
channel, since children become more valuable as old-age support again. Fertility fell instead.

The grandparent-time channel changes that conclusion. Raising the retirement age keeps a grandmother at
her job, takes away the childcare she would otherwise have supplied, and can lower her daughter's
fertility that way. All three grandparent-time studies sit in rich countries below replacement, and all
three agree once their estimates are turned to face the same direction, with the Dutch and Australian
estimates large relative to observed TFR declines in their windows and the German estimates left
unscaled.

China does not settle the SDT question. The Chinese pension and long-term-care insurance studies are
useful evidence that replacing children with non-child old-age security can still move fertility where
old-age-security policy is newly expanding. But a couple whose births are already capped by national
policy cannot respond to a new pension by having fewer children than the law permits them anyway, so
the Chinese estimates are bounded by the birth limits as well as by preferences. China reached below
replacement under that regime, which makes it weak evidence about the SDT process of postponement,
individualization, and low desired family size.

## 8. Whether the results carry to other settings

The studies are not all about the same social world. A result can be exactly right about rural Brazil
in 1995 and say nothing usable about Italy in 2005. Rural Brazil, Namibia, and rural China differ
sharply from Imperial Germany, nineteenth-century U.S. counties, and late twentieth-century Italy, and
the differences that matter are norms about family transfers, how much of the population a pension
covers, and how high fertility was to begin with.

Those differences decide whether a channel has room to operate at all. The retirement-asset channel
needs two conditions at once: children who are still a credible retirement asset, and a non-child
substitute that is arriving or expanding right now. Where public pensions and private savings already
cover everyone, neither condition holds and the channel has nothing left to do. The grandparent-time
channel needs a different set: grandparents living close by, formal childcare expensive enough to be
worth avoiding, and a retirement rule that sharply changes how older adults spend their days.

| Kind of setting | Channel most likely at work | How far the result carries |
|---|---|---|
| Rural or developing settings with family old-age support | Replacing children with non-child old-age security | High mechanism relevance, but limited direct transport to rich-country SDT. |
| Historical Western FDT settings | Financial-market or early social-insurance substitution | Relevant to FDT only if timing and coverage precede or overlap the decline. |
| Rich below-replacement settings | Grandparental childcare | Most relevant to SDT, but effect size must be checked against the scale of the fertility decline. |
| Policy-constrained low-fertility settings such as China | New policy expansion replacing children as old-age support | Useful mechanism evidence, weak as SDT evidence. |

A study can be entirely credible about its own setting and still weak evidence about a target
transition, so credibility at home and credibility abroad are judged separately.

## 9. Risk of bias and how far the designs can be trusted

Policy-generated variation across households, where one village gets the pension and the next one does
not, is usually credible even though nobody randomized it. Historical variation across regions, where
one Prussian district had more insured workers than another, is valuable for timing and exposed to a
confounder that can be named precisely: the districts with more insured workers were also the more
industrial and more urban ones, and industrialization and urbanization move fertility on their own.
Confounding, selection, and identification credibility are assessed separately against those two
profiles.

The strongest direct evidence comes from quasi-experimental pension expansions or eligibility changes
with a credible comparison group. Rossi and Godard (2022), Danzer and Zyska (2023), and the
grandparent-time studies are the clearest examples. These are rated moderate rather than low risk,
because exposure was generated by policy rather than by a coin flip, and because documentation of
missing data is not always fully audited. Zelu, Iranzo, and Pérez-Laborda (2023) also use
policy-generated difference-in-differences variation, and their Ghana working paper remains ungraded
pending review. Their comparison needs particular scrutiny, because Ghanaian public-sector and
private-sector women differ in schooling, in whether their work is salaried and formal, and in whether
they live in cities, and each of those three moves fertility without any help from Act 766.

Guinnane and Streb (2021), Fenge and Scheubel (2017), and Galofre-Vila (2023) are recorded as serious
risk in the overall risk-of-bias field, even where they are valuable for timing and mechanism. Social
insurance can change the number of births by changing who marries and at what age rather than by
changing how many children married couples have, and a crude
birth rate cannot tell the two apart, which is the difficulty in Guinnane and Streb and in Fenge and
Scheubel. American Social Security matured in the same years as postwar income growth and a marriage
boom, both of which raise fertility, so the positive fertility response Galofre-Vila observes cannot be
read as the pension's own effect. All three also risk reading individual behavior off district or
county averages. The China evidence needs careful handling for a further reason, that fertility policy
and pension policy overlap in time.

## 11. The substitute for children changes over time

The substitute keeps changing identity. Children, kin, land, and family transfers did the work in
pre-modern settings. Banks and insurers may have done more of it than state pensions during the FDT,
because they arrived early enough to affect the decline. By the SDT the job has changed hands again:
rich countries no longer need children as old-age insurance in the same way, many families still need
grandparents for childcare, and retirement rules decide how much of that help arrives.

Asking whether pensions reduce fertility, as a general question, has no answer. The answerable question
is which channel operates in which demographic regime. One pension expansion can switch both on at
once, which makes the net effect something to be measured case by case rather than predicted from
theory.

## 12. Limits and what would fix them

The limitation shared by the whole evidence base is that the studies do not estimate one common
treatment contrast, which is why this chapter reports pooled summaries within each outcome family
rather than one statistically pooled pension coefficient. More estimates on the same treatment scale
would make pooling possible for a specific treatment inside a narrow outcome family. Gathering the
mechanism-only studies such as Ruthbah (2022) and the indirect chain-link evidence into an appendix
would show how children, savings, insurance, and old-age support interact outside the primary fertility
question.
