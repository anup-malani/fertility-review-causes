**Category:** Economic  
**Main idea:** Where children are how a person survives old age, giving people another way to fund
retirement makes an extra child less necessary, so fertility falls.  
**Second idea, running the other way:** A pension also decides when grandparents stop working, and a
retired grandmother is available childcare, which makes children cheaper for her adult daughter and
can raise fertility.  
**Fertility patterns this chapter tests the hypothesis against:** pre-modern fertility variation, the
First Demographic Transition (roughly 1870 to 1965), and the Second Demographic Transition (1965 to
the present). We abbreviate them PM, FDT, and SDT.  
**Draft status:** Complete draft, rewritten in plain language for a general reader.  
**Last updated:** 2026-07-11 (source chapter); 2026-07-26 (this rewrite)

## 1. Verdict

A pension moves fertility in two opposite directions at once. Treating "pensions" as a single
treatment makes this literature look self-contradictory, and the contradiction dissolves as soon as
the two directions are separated.

A couple in a village with no pension system, no insurer worth the name, and no bank they would trust
for thirty years has exactly one retirement plan: their children. A grown son sends money home, and a
grown daughter takes them in once they can no longer farm. A child is partly an investment. Give that
couple a pension, and the pension does the job the child was going to do, so one reason to have
another child disappears and fertility falls. Economists call this crowd-out: the new asset crowds
children out of the job of supporting a person in old age. This chapter calls it the
**retirement-asset channel**, because it turns on children being an asset held for retirement.

The same pension also decides when a grandmother stops working. A grandmother still at her job cannot
mind her daughter's baby on a Tuesday afternoon; a retired grandmother can. Childcare supplied free by
a grandparent lowers what a child costs the adult daughter, and cheaper children mean more of them.
This chapter calls that the **grandparent-time channel**, because the pension changes how a
grandparent spends her days.

The two channels point opposite ways. Expand pension coverage and the retirement-asset channel pushes
fertility down while the grandparent-time channel pushes it up. Averaging estimates from the two
together would produce a number that means nothing, so this chapter never combines them.

The evidence supports a narrow version of the retirement-asset argument. Fertility falls where
children still plausibly insure old age and a credible substitute for them arrives. Settings outside
that condition do not line up: long-term-care insurance in China, social insurance in Bismarck's
Prussia, and U.S. Social Security during the baby boom do not all show the same negative fertility
response. The response depends on which kind of old-age security changes and on the demographic
setting in which it changes.

State pensions did not cause the First Demographic Transition, because most of them arrived after
that decline had already begun. The better-timed version of the argument runs through financial
development. Banks and savings instruments gave households a way to carry money from their working
years into old age without a child in the middle, and those institutions spread early enough to
matter.

The Second Demographic Transition poses a different question: whether pension policy still moves
fertility once public pensions and private savings are already everywhere. Rich low-fertility
countries leave the retirement-asset channel little room, because the substitute for children is
already in place. The stronger evidence in those settings supports the grandparent-time channel. More
grandparent availability raises fertility, and later retirement lowers it.

The table below records two separate judgments for each fertility pattern. The first is how much
confidence the evidence supports about cause, on the four-step GRADE scale this review uses
throughout: Very low, Low, Moderate, High, with in-between ratings such as Low-to-moderate where the
evidence splits. A GRADE rating is a statement about trust in the causal claim, not about the size of
the effect. The second judgment asks how much of the observed fertility change the hypothesis could
account for, which is a different question. A well-identified effect can be entirely real and still
too small, or too narrow in reach, to move a national birth rate.

| Fertility pattern | Confidence in the causal claim | How much of the change it could explain | Bottom line |
|---|---|---|---|

| SDT | Moderate for grandparental childcare; low for the mechanism where children support parents in old age | Partial for the childcare channel; not significant or contextual for that support mechanism | The grandparental childcare evidence consistently implies that more grandparent availability raises fertility. ## 2. The claim and the two channels

The old-age-security argument starts from one fact about households without pensions: an old person
who can no longer work has to be supported by somebody. Children are the somebody. A couple in such a
place, deciding whether to have a fourth child, is deciding in part how well they will eat at seventy.
Give them a public pension, a private insurance policy, or a bank that will still be there in thirty
years, and the retirement return to a fourth child falls. Fertility should fall with it.

The grandparent-time channel starts from the same policy and reaches the opposite prediction.
Retirement changes how much free time an older adult has, and a retired grandparent who supplies
childcare lowers the price of a child to that grandparent's own adult children. One pension reform can
therefore raise fertility in the same country where it lowers fertility through the retirement-asset
channel. Keeping the two apart is this chapter's organizing discipline.

### 2.1 Children as a retirement plan

Two research traditions feed the retirement-asset channel, and the older one treats children as part
of a transfer system between generations rather than only as something parents enjoy having. Neher
(1971), Willis (1980), and Nugent (1985) state that view most directly, and Caldwell (1976) and Cigno
(1993) connect it to broader theory about transfers between generations. In this tradition the
fertility decision turns on the expected old-age return to a child, counting cash support, personal
care, and insurance against bad luck.

Caldwell's wealth-flow argument frames the demographic transition by asking which way money runs
between generations. High fertility can pay for itself when the net flow runs from children to
parents. When the flow reverses, or when a non-child asset takes over the retirement job, the private
demand for another birth should fall.

Testing this channel means comparing families that gain a non-child source of old-age support with
otherwise similar families that do not. Fertility should fall most where children are still a
meaningful old-age asset, so the prediction is strongest where formal pensions, private savings, and
insurance markets are thin, and weakest where all three are already mature.

### 2.2 What the formal models add

The second tradition is the economic modeling of fertility, and it supplies the policy lever the older
tradition lacks. Becker (1960), Becker and Barro (1988), and Barro and Becker (1989) set the
background framework, in which parents choose how many children to have given their resources, their
preferences, and what a child costs. Ehrlich and Lui (1991), Nishimura and Zhang (1992), and Sinn
(2004) then model how public pensions and private family transfers interact with those choices, and
Boldrin, De Nardi, and Jones (2015) tie the mechanisms to social-security systems more directly. A
"pay-as-you-go" pension system in this literature is one in which today's workers' taxes pay today's
retirees' benefits, rather than one in which each worker funds an account of their own.

These models show why a public pension can move fertility even among households that plan ahead
carefully. A pay-as-you-go system replaces the implicit family contract under which children support
parents. It also changes who has to be trusted. A child no longer has to repay their parents directly
once the state taxes the next generation and mails the check itself. Some models therefore predict
fertility decline after a pension expansion. Others ask what policy correction is needed when a
pension system leaves families too little private reason to have children.

The transfer between generations does not disappear in any of these models. The transfer moves from a
household contract, under which parents expect support from their own children, to a state contract,
under which workers finance retirees through taxes and benefits. Fertility falls because an additional
child no longer delivers the same private retirement return to the parent.

### 2.3 What counts as evidence here

The primary question of this chapter is narrow: what happens to fertility when a household's non-child
old-age security rises for reasons outside that household's control. The question excludes many papers
that mention pensions. A pension study whose outcome is savings, schooling, or elderly health tests a
link in the mechanism without measuring the fertility effect. A study in which fertility causes
pension take-up or insurance purchase runs the arrow backwards and counts as mechanism evidence. A
study in which pension eligibility changes grandparents' childcare time belongs to the grandparent-time
channel, which predicts the opposite sign.

That last exclusion does the real work. One pension can lower fertility by replacing children as
old-age insurance and raise fertility by retiring grandparents into childcare, so the two effects
cannot be averaged as though they measured one thing. Theory justifies both channels. The synthesis
keeps them in separate cells because they answer different causal questions.

### 2.4 What the theory predicts for each fertility pattern

The retirement-asset channel is plausible for pre-modern fertility and nearly useless for explaining
pre-modern differences. If almost every household lacks formal old-age security, dependence on
children explains why desired fertility was high everywhere better than it explains why one population
had more children than another. Some of that variation could still come from differences in family
systems, communal care, inheritance rules, and local expectations about who supports older adults.
This review therefore treats the channel as relevant to pre-modern fertility and as an insufficient
explanation of pre-modern variation without evidence on those institutional differences.

The public-pension version of the argument arrives too late for the First Demographic Transition, and
a broader version does not. Financial markets, savings, and insurance reduce the need to rely on
children before modern pension systems reach broad coverage, which makes asset substitution the
better-timed mechanism for the FDT.

The Second Demographic Transition leaves little room for the retirement-asset channel and a good deal
for the grandparent-time channel. A rich country in 1970 already has public pensions and private
savings, so no later reform can be the thing that first replaces children as a retirement plan. A
later reform can still change what a child costs, by moving grandparents into or out of paid work and
so changing how much unpaid childcare a family can count on.

## 3. Sorting studies by the question they answer

Pension-and-fertility papers become legible once they are sorted by the causal question they answer
rather than by their subject matter.

| Group of studies | What it measures | Role in the chapter |
|---|---|---|
| Children as old-age support | Non-child old-age security affects fertility by reducing the retirement value of children. | Direct evidence on whether pensions, insurance, or financial markets reduce fertility by replacing children as old-age support. |
| Mechanism evidence | Children affect private insurance, assets, or old-age support. | Evidence on whether children behave like old-age-security assets. It informs the mechanism but is not pooled with fertility effects. |
| Grandparental childcare | Pension or retirement policy changes childcare help and fertility. | Evidence on whether retirement policy raises fertility by increasing grandparents' childcare availability. |
| Indirect links | Pensions affect savings, schooling, migration, coresidence, health, or other links short of fertility. | Background evidence on the causal chain. It updates mechanism plausibility without estimating fertility effects. |

The filter is the causal question rather than topical relevance. A paper can be about pensions and
about fertility and still answer the wrong question for the retirement-asset channel. The synthesis
therefore keeps four things apart: primary fertility effects, evidence that the mechanism exists,
grandparent-time evidence, and links in the chain that stop short of fertility.

## 4. How the studies were found and kept

The search began broad, covering old-age security, pensions, and fertility, and one inclusion rule did
most of the screening: a paper enters the review only if it identifies one of the four questions in
Section 3. The rule matters because pension papers often study outcomes other than fertility, or study
fertility through a channel other than the retirement-asset one.

The main synthesis uses studies that estimate how fertility responds to non-child old-age security or
to grandparent availability. Studies testing whether children behave like a retirement asset are kept
and held out of the fertility pool. Historical and aggregate studies are kept for what they say about
timing and about whether results carry to other settings, and their higher risk of confounding is
carried into the bias assessment rather than into the pooled numbers. Section 13 lists the search
logs, extraction sheets, and files behind every number here.

## 5. The studies

### 5.1 Studies on replacing children as a retirement plan

this channel. The tenth, a Ghana working paper retrieved late, enters provisionally and stays out of
the pooled summaries below.

Six abbreviations recur in that table. "DiD" means difference-in-differences: measure how the outcome
changed over time in a group the policy touched, measure it over the same years in a group the policy
missed, and read the gap as the effect of the policy. "IV" means instrumental variables: find
something that shifts pension coverage and has no other route to fertility, use it as a lever, and
read the effect of coverage off the lever. An "event study" traces the outcome year by year before and
after a reform, which shows whether the two groups were already drifting apart beforehand. "PSM" means
propensity-score matching, which pairs each treated household with an untreated household that looked
similar before the policy arrived. "LTCI" means long-term-care insurance, which pays for nursing and
personal care in old age rather than for cash income. "CMBR" means the crude marital birth rate,
births per married woman in a year.

Most of these are quasi-experimental designs, and the word carries a specific claim. Nobody randomized
who received a pension. The researchers argue instead that a policy drew a line between two groups for
reasons unrelated to how many children those groups wanted, so comparing across the line isolates the
pension. Whether a Namibian district fell inside a 1992 pension extension has little to do with the
family plans of the women living there, and the design rests on that.

Each study contributes its own preferred estimate where the paper names one, in the paper's own units
and with the paper's own measure of uncertainty. Every estimate is oriented so that a negative number
means lower fertility after an increase in non-child old-age security. The main analytic limit is that
the studies do not all estimate the same policy contrast.

### 5.2 Evidence that children behave like a retirement asset

Ci (2024) stays outside the fertility synthesis because its outcome is not fertility. Ci asks whether
older parents with more children buy less private insurance, less private pension coverage, and less
private health insurance. A yes makes the premise of the whole channel more plausible: if children and
purchased old-age security are substitutes, then giving a household one should reduce its appetite for
the other. The primary question of this chapter runs the other way, from old-age security to
fertility, so Ci informs the mechanism without measuring the effect.

Ruthbah (2022), from Bangladesh, appears to be a parallel mechanism paper. This review treats it as a
candidate addition to the mechanism evidence rather than as part of the primary fertility-effect
synthesis.

### 5.3 Studies on grandparent time

Three studies anchor the grandparent-time channel, and the SDT case rests on them: Eibich and Siedler
(Germany), Ilciukas (Netherlands), and Akyol and Atalay (Australia). None of the three asks whether
parents use children as pensions. Each asks whether pension or retirement rules change how much
childcare a grandparent can supply, and whether that change then shows up in the adult child's births.

Comparing the three requires first turning them all the same way around. Two of the reforms made
grandparents more available: Eibich and Siedler study German parents reaching retirement, and Akyol
and Atalay study Australian grandmothers becoming eligible for a pension. The Dutch reform Ilciukas
studies did the opposite. It pushed the retirement age up, so mothers stayed at work and were less
available to mind a grandchild. Left as its authors reported it, the Dutch estimate answers
the question "what happens when grandparents become less available," while the other two answer "what
happens when they become more available." Three studies pointed at two opposite questions would look
like disagreement even if all three described identical behavior. Flipping the sign on the Dutch
estimate points all three at one question. A finding that less grandparent availability lowers
fertility becomes, after the flip, a finding that more grandparent availability raises it. The flip
changes the direction the comparison is stated in and leaves the estimate's size and precision
untouched. With all three pointed the same way, all three imply that more grandparent availability
raises fertility.

All three settings are rich countries whose fertility sits below replacement, meaning below about 2.1
births per woman, the level at which one generation roughly replaces itself. That classification comes
from the total fertility rate, or TFR, which is the number of children a woman would have if she lived
her whole life through the birth rates observed in a single calendar year.

The scale check asks whether effects of this size are big enough to matter. It sets the
availability-oriented estimates against how much the country's TFR actually fell over the same years.
Ilciukas's Netherlands estimates and Akyol and Atalay's Australia estimates are large relative to the
fertility declines observed in those windows. Eibich and Siedler's Germany estimates support the same
direction, and Germany's TFR rises slightly across their 1984-2017 window in the German
Socio-Economic Panel, so no fertility decline exists there to divide into and no comparable scale
calculation can be made.

A scale check is a weaker exercise than a decomposition share, and the difference matters for how much
weight the result carries. A decomposition share would say what fraction of the Dutch fertility
decline this channel accounts for, which requires knowing how many grandmothers the reform actually
bound and how their individual responses add up nationally. A scale check only asks whether an effect
of the estimated size, applied over a plausible range, lands in the same neighborhood as the decline.
It can show that the grandparent-time channel is not trivially small wherever the target window has
falling TFR, and it cannot say the channel produced any particular share of that fall.

### 5.4 Links in the chain short of fertility

The indirect evidence covers what pensions do to household savings, to children's schooling, and to
whether the generations live under one roof. These studies answer whether the intermediate links in
the mechanism exist at all. None of them says how much fertility changes when old-age security
changes.

## 6. Putting the numbers together

The synthesis keeps every estimate close to the way its own paper reported it, comparing effects only
after preserving each paper's outcome wording, treatment contrast, uncertainty, and follow-up window.
Unit conversions are mechanical: percentage-point effects are re-expressed as probabilities, and
completed-fertility estimates stay in births per woman.

### 6.1 The two pooled summaries

Two pooled summaries are reported for the retirement-asset channel. Pooling means averaging several
studies' estimates into a single number, weighting each study by how precisely it was measured, and
attaching one margin of error to the result. Averaging works only when the numbers being averaged
answer the same question, so pooling here happens within an outcome family, meaning within a set of
studies that measured the same thing, and never across the pension literature as a whole. One family
asks whether a woman gave birth in a given year, which is a probability between 0 and 1. The other
asks how many children a woman had in total by the end of her childbearing years, which is a count of
births. An average taken across a probability and a count of births would be a number in no units at
all.

The birth-probability summary pools five independent studies. More non-child old-age security is
associated with a 0.006954 lower probability of birth, with a standard error of 0.002037 and a 95
percent confidence interval running from -0.010946 to -0.002963. Put in people rather than decimals:
out of every 1,000 women in a year, about seven fewer give birth. The standard error reports how
precisely that average was pinned down, so a large one means the studies scattered widely or were
small and a small one means they agreed and were well measured. The confidence interval is the range
of true values the data are consistent with, and this one says the real effect is very unlikely to be
weaker than about three fewer births per 1,000 women or stronger than about eleven. The whole interval
sits below zero, so the summary points to lower fertility rather than to no effect at all.

The completed-fertility summary pools three independent studies. More non-child old-age security is
associated with 0.067672 fewer births per woman, with a standard error of 0.026971 and a 95 percent
confidence interval running from -0.120536 to -0.014808. Roughly seven-hundredths of a birth per woman
works out to one birth fewer for about every fifteen women. The interval runs from about 0.015 to
about 0.121 fewer births per woman and again lies entirely below zero.

Neither summary is one structural pension effect. The birth-probability pool mixes pension expansion,
pension-value exposure, pension participation, pension cuts, and long-term-care insurance. The
completed-fertility pool mixes pension-value exposure, pension cuts, and pension participation. Both
numbers therefore summarize direction and rough magnitude inside an outcome family, and reading either
one still requires knowing which treatment produced it, in which setting, and how good the
identification was.

The stricter pooling rule still binds. A single coefficient for "the effect of pensions" would need at
least three independent studies sharing the same mechanism, the same outcome, the same units, the same
treatment scale, and a usable standard error. The current evidence does not meet that bar.

The Ghana working paper agrees with the developing-country pattern and stays out of both summaries.
Zelu, Iranzo, and Pérez-Laborda compare private-sector women newly covered by Ghana's Act 766 reform
with public-sector women who already had more generous pensions. Their controlled
difference-in-differences estimate is a 6-percentage-point reduction in the probability of pregnancy
in the previous year (standard error 3 percentage points; Table 2, p. 14). Their controlled
full-sample estimate for the number of children is negative and too imprecise to separate from zero
(Table 7, p. 17). Both estimates stay provisional, because the paper is a working draft that has not
completed this chapter's extraction and risk-of-bias review.

The two channels stay in separate cells throughout. Retirement-asset estimates answer whether
non-child old-age security lowers fertility by replacing children as old-age insurance.
Grandparent-time estimates answer whether grandparent availability raises fertility by lowering what a
child costs. Mechanism-validation and indirect-chain evidence stay outside the fertility-effect
synthesis altogether.

Every grandparent-time estimate is expressed as the effect of greater grandparent availability,
whichever way its own reform pushed. Two of the three need no adjustment, since retirement in Germany
and pension eligibility in Australia both made grandparents more available. Ilciukas is flipped, since
the Dutch reform pushed mothers' retirement later and made grandparents less available. Those
availability-coded effects are then held against the TFR change in the same country over the same
years. Ilciukas's Netherlands estimates and Akyol and Atalay's Australia estimates are large next to
the fertility declines their windows contain. Eibich and Siedler's Germany estimates are left
unscaled, because German TFR does not fall over their window at all.

The rest of the evidence is handled in words rather than numbers wherever the outcome, the treatment
scale, or the uncertainty measure is not comparable enough to average. Historical aggregate outcomes
and mechanism-only estimates remain useful, and they answer different questions from the pooled
fertility estimates.

Treatment comparability is the limitation that survives every choice above. Binary pension expansions,
continuous pension-value exposure, pension cuts, long-term-care insurance pilots, financial access,
and broad social spending do not define one common treatment contrast.

## 7. How much of the fertility change this explains

Demographic significance is judged by matching each study window to where fertility stood relative to
replacement in that country. A setting whose first and last available in-window TFR readings both lie
above 2.1 is coded FDT-relevant. A setting whose readings both lie below 2.1 is coded SDT-relevant. A
window that crosses from above to below replacement during the study is coded FDT|SDT. The rule makes
Brazil a crossing case, Namibia and the postwar United States above-replacement cases, and Italy plus
the China pension studies below-replacement cases. Prussia, Imperial Germany, and the 1850 U.S.
financial-development evidence have no in-window TFR coverage and remain historical cases. China stays
hard to read, because its low fertility was produced partly by policy that limited births.

Four verdicts follow from that classification. Pre-modern fertility has no direct evidence at all. The
FDT verdict is partial, on three grounds: the primary evidence supports the retirement-asset channel
in settings above replacement or crossing it, state pensions are usually late for the Western FDT, and
the pooled numbers do not deliver one structural treatment coefficient. For the SDT, that same channel
is not significant, or contextual at best. The below-replacement evidence that exists comes from
China, where policy limited births, and a rich country whose pension system matured decades earlier
offers almost no fresh variation to study on this margin. The grandparent-time channel is the live SDT
channel. Its evidence comes from rich countries below replacement, it consistently implies that
greater grandparent availability raises fertility, and the scale check makes it large in the
Netherlands and Australia.

### 7.1 Pre-modern fertility variation

No direct pre-modern causal evidence exists. The channel almost certainly mattered as a background
condition, since a world without public pensions, formal insurance, or deep financial markets leaves
children as the only old-age support on offer. A universal condition does not explain variation. If
nearly everyone depends on children for old age, that dependence explains why fertility was high in
general better than it explains why one population differed from another.

The pre-modern verdict is therefore low. Mortality, marriage systems, and inheritance rules are more
plausible explanations for the differences across pre-modern populations, and old-age security reads
better as a baseline motive than as the main source of those differences.

### 7.2 First Demographic Transition

State pensions arrive too late to have started the First Demographic Transition. The Western decline
begins in the late nineteenth century. Bismarck's pension system dates to the late 1880s, and U.S.
Social Security begins in 1935. A reform of that vintage can deepen or redirect a decline already
under way. It cannot explain why the decline began.

Financial development is the stronger FDT route. If the real mechanism is a non-child way to provide
for old age, households did not need a state pension to get one. They needed a bank or an insurer they
could trust across thirty years. Basso, Bodenhorn, and Cuberes speak directly to that margin in the
nineteenth-century United States, which makes financial development the better-timed substitute for
children in the FDT.

The FDT verdict is partial. The channel is plausible and empirically supported, and state pensions are
too late and their early coverage too thin to be the main cause, so the best version of the FDT
argument is broader asset substitution with financial markets doing more of the work than public
pensions.

### 7.3 Second Demographic Transition

The SDT needs two judgments, one per channel.

The retirement-asset channel is weak as an explanation of the rich-country SDT. A pension system that
matured decades before the SDT began has little variation left to study, because whatever it was going
to do to the retirement value of children it had already done. The predicted direction also runs
backwards. Trimming pension generosity or pushing eligibility later should raise fertility through this
channel, since children become more valuable as old-age support again. Fertility fell instead.

The grandparent-time channel changes that conclusion. Raising the retirement age keeps a grandmother at
her job, takes away the childcare she would otherwise have supplied, and can lower her daughter's
fertility that way. All three grandparent-time studies sit in rich countries below replacement, and all
three agree once their estimates are turned to face the same direction. Ilciukas's Netherlands
estimates and Akyol and Atalay's Australia estimates are large relative to observed TFR declines in
their study windows. Eibich and Siedler's German evidence runs the same way, and German TFR rises
slightly across their window, so their estimates are never converted into shares of a fertility
decline.

China does not settle the SDT question. The Chinese pension and long-term-care insurance studies are
useful evidence that replacing children with non-child old-age security can still move fertility where
old-age-security policy is newly expanding. China reached below replacement under a fertility regime
constrained by policy, which makes it weak evidence about the SDT process of postponement,
individualization, and low desired family size.

## 8. Whether the results carry to other settings

The studies are not all about the same social world, which is the main obstacle to carrying any of
them elsewhere. Researchers call this external validity, or transportability: a result can be exactly
right about rural Brazil in 1995 and say nothing usable about Italy in 2005. Rural Brazil, Namibia,
and rural China differ sharply from Imperial Germany, nineteenth-century U.S. counties, and late
twentieth-century Italy, and the differences that matter are norms about family transfers, how much of
the population a pension covers, and how high fertility was to begin with.

Those differences decide whether a channel has room to operate at all. The retirement-asset channel
needs two conditions at once: children who are still a credible retirement asset, and a non-child
substitute that is arriving or expanding right now. Where public pensions and private savings already
cover everyone, neither condition holds and the channel has nothing left to do. The grandparent-time
channel needs a different set: grandparents living close by, formal childcare expensive enough to be
worth avoiding, and a retirement rule that sharply changes how older adults spend their days.

| Kind of setting | Channel most likely at work | How far the result carries |
|---|---|---|
| Rural or developing settings with family old-age support | Replacing children with non-child old-age security | High mechanism relevance, but limited direct transport to rich-country SDT. |
| Historical Western FDT settings | Financial-market or early social-insurance substitution | Relevant to FDT only if timing and coverage precede or overlap the decline. |
| Rich below-replacement settings | Grandparental childcare | Most relevant to SDT, but effect size must be checked against the scale of the fertility decline. |
| Policy-constrained low-fertility settings such as China | New policy expansion replacing children as old-age support | Useful mechanism evidence, weak as SDT evidence. |

A study can be entirely credible about its own setting and still weak evidence about a target
transition, so credibility at home and credibility abroad are judged separately.

## 9. Risk of bias and how far the designs can be trusted

Two kinds of variation carry most of the weight in this literature, and they fail in different ways.
Policy-generated variation across households, where one village gets the pension and the next one does
not, is usually credible even though nobody randomized it. Historical variation across regions, where
one Prussian district had more insured workers than another, is valuable for timing and more exposed
to the risk that the districts differed in other ways as well. Confounding, selection, and
identification credibility are each assessed against those two profiles.

The strongest direct evidence comes from quasi-experimental pension expansions or eligibility changes
with a credible comparison group. Rossi and Godard, Danzer and Zyska, and the grandparent-time studies
are the clearest examples. These are rated moderate rather than low risk, because exposure was
generated by policy rather than by a coin flip, and because documentation of missing data is not always
fully audited. Zelu, Iranzo, and Pérez-Laborda also use policy-generated difference-in-differences
variation, and their Ghana working paper remains ungraded pending review; their public-sector against
private-sector comparison needs particular scrutiny, because the two groups differ substantially in
what kinds of jobs they hold.

The historical evidence is important and more exposed to confounding, to measurement error, and to the
risk of reading individual behavior off district averages. Guinnane and Streb, Fenge and Scheubel, and
Galofre-Vila are therefore recorded as serious risk in the overall risk-of-bias field, even where they
are valuable for timing and mechanism. The China evidence is useful and needs careful handling,
because fertility policy and pension policy overlap in time and because the results carry poorly to the
SDT. Mechanism studies such as Ci are relevant on their own terms and do not estimate the fertility
outcome.

## 11. The substitute for children changes over time

The evidence supports a substitution story in which the substitute keeps changing identity. Children,
kin, land, and family transfers did the work in pre-modern settings. Banks and insurers may have done
more of it than state pensions during the FDT, because they arrived early enough to affect the
decline. By the SDT the job has changed hands again. Rich countries no longer need children as old-age
insurance in the same way, many families still need grandparents for childcare, and retirement rules
decide how much of that help arrives.

Asking whether pensions reduce fertility, as a general question, has no answer. The answerable
question is which channel operates in which demographic regime. The retirement-asset channel lowers
fertility where old-age support is scarce and children are useful assets. The grandparent-time channel
raises fertility where retired grandparents supply time to their adult children. One pension expansion
can switch both on at once, which makes the net effect something to be measured case by case rather
than predicted from theory.

## 12. Limits and what would fix them

The limitation shared by the whole evidence base is that the studies do not estimate one common
treatment contrast. Binary pension expansions, cuts to pension wealth, and long-term-care insurance
pilots all bear on old-age security and are not the same treatment, which is why this chapter reports
pooled summaries within each outcome family rather than one statistically pooled pension coefficient.

Two extensions would strengthen the chapter. More estimates on the same treatment scale would make
pooling possible for a specific treatment inside a narrow outcome family. Gathering the mechanism-only
studies such as Ruthbah and the indirect chain-link evidence into an appendix would show how children,
savings, insurance, and old-age support interact outside the primary fertility question.
