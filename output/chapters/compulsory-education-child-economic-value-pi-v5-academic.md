**Category:** Economic  
**Primary mechanism:** Compulsory attendance and child-labor restrictions reduce children's work,
lowering the economic return parents expect from an additional child, and so reducing completed
fertility.  
**Cross-references:** Tempo effects and birth postponement (compulsory schooling). Geruso and Royer
(2018) belongs to both chapters, Cummins (2025) is routed there by mechanism, and the six
own-schooling studies set aside here test the mechanism that chapter examines.

## 1. The claim

This chapter examines the effect of a school-attendance or child-labor statute on parents' fertility decisions.
The causal chain has three steps or links. A school-attendance or child-labor law takes a child out of paid work. The earnings the parents no
longer collect make each child worth less to them. Parents who expect less money from each child
have fewer children. Every step has to hold for the hypothesis to be true.

Compulsory schooling in this account changes how many children a family ends up with, and not the
ages at which those children are born. Parents here are not choosing when to have a child; they are
choosing how many to have, given what each one is now worth to them. The law need not change any
parent's own schooling for the hypothesis to be true, and a study showing that a woman's own
schooling changed her own fertility neither confirms nor refutes the hypothesis. Section 3 turns the
difference between a parent's own schooling and a child's economic value into the test this chapter
applies to every study in the corpus.

## 2. Theoretical mechanism

Most of the theory behind compulsory schooling as a cause of lower fertility runs the same four
steps: children produce income, a binding schooling requirement removes their work time, the return
to an additional child falls, and parents shift from having more children toward spending more on
each one. The quantity of children and the quality of each are substitutes in the household's
budget, so a law that raises the price of quantity buys quality instead. Every model below either
adds a route to that prediction or denies that the prediction follows.

Hazan and Berdugo (2001) give the formative statement of the mechanism: child labor and high
fertility hold each other up in a trap that a one-generation prohibition can break. Poor parents
send children to work; working children are cheap on net, so parents have many of them; many
uneducated children keep wages low and keep the next generation poor. A prohibition that binds for
one generation moves the economy to a second steady state with little child labor and low fertility,
and the new steady state holds after the prohibition lapses, because a generation that went to
school earns enough that its own children need not work. See also Fioroni (2017), which reaches the
same conclusion by the same price logic in a model without the trap: a working child pays part of
its own way, so a law that removes the work raises the net cost of a child and parents buy fewer of
it.

Mookherjee, Prina, and Ray (2012) reach the same conclusion, that restricting children's work lowers
fertility, through the mix of occupations an economy produces rather than through the household's
budget. Restricting children's work raises the schooling of the next generation. More schooling
moves that generation into the occupations schooling opens. An economy weighted toward skilled work
settles at a lower number of children per family. The fertility change in their model is a
by-product of an occupational change, so it arrives with a lag and depends on how readily the
economy absorbs skilled workers.

Emerson and Knabb (2013) make the fall in fertility after a child-labor ban permanent by changing
what parents believe rather than what they earn. Households in their model form expectations about
what a schooled child will earn, and those expectations hold the high-fertility, low-schooling
arrangement in place. A temporary ban paired with mandatory education gives a household a generation
of schooled children to observe, which moves the expectations, and the low-fertility pattern then
survives the end of the ban. The difference from Hazan and Berdugo is which force keeps the new
arrangement standing: there, the income an educated generation earns; here, a belief that outlives
the policy that produced it.

Cigno and Rosati (2005) treat the number of children, how many of them survive, and how much
schooling each receives as one household decision, so a restriction on children's work moves all
three margins at once rather than fertility alone. A household that can no longer collect a child's
earnings reallocates across the three margins together, and the size of the fertility response
depends on that reallocation rather than on the price of a child by itself. Cigno and Rosati (2024b)
add a policy-design result to the same framework: a partial restriction on children's work can
sustain a higher-education equilibrium on its own, while a total ban may require complementary
insurance and education policy to hold.

While the prior papers offer theories for why child labor or schooling laws lower fertility, there
are other papers that suggest these statutes have ambiguous effects on fertility. Baland and
Robinson (2000) prove that a child-labor ban can raise welfare and still leave its fertility effect
unsigned. In their economy parents cannot borrow against what a child will earn as an adult, so
putting the child to work is how a poor household moves resources from the child's future into the
household's present, and a ban shuts that route down. Shutting it down raises what a child costs the
household now and raises what the same child is worth to it later, because the child goes to school
instead and earns more as an adult; the two changes push the number of children in opposite
directions. Takakura (2023) obtains opposite signs for different households, because the restriction
binds only where children were working. An unskilled household loses the earnings its children
brought in, so each child costs it more and it has fewer; a skilled household, whose children were
already in school, loses nothing directly and is reached only through the equilibrium a
better-educated next generation produces, which in his model raises its fertility. The national
total therefore depends on how many parents of each kind the restriction binds.

Greenwood and Seshadri (2004) make compulsory-schooling and child-labor laws a consequence of
technological change rather than a cause of the fertility decline, which is a problem for any
estimate identified from legislative dates. In their account, technological progress raised
employers' demand for skilled labor and lowered their demand for the small hands of children,
children left work in response to that shift in labor demand, and effective regulation arrived after
much of the withdrawal from work had already occurred. On that history, a researcher who dates the
treatment to the passage of a statute assigns to the statute a decline that had already begun.
Legislatures choose when to pass these laws, and they choose in response to the same economic
conditions that move fertility, so the treatment is endogenous. Section 3 returns to the problem as
the first of four costs of the design the literature actually uses.

## 3. Empirical strategy

### 3a. The treatment

The literature on compulsory schooling and fertility uses two distinct legal instruments, a
school-leaving law and a child-labor law, and an estimate means something different depending on
which one produced it. A **school-leaving law** sets the age below which a child must remain
enrolled in school. A **child-labor law** restricts what work a child may do, by minimum age, by
hours worked, or by occupation. The two are different levers on the same margin: a school-leaving
law occupies the child's time, and a child-labor law forbids the employment directly. Legislatures
frequently passed both in the same wave of reform, which Section 3d takes up as a limitation and
Section 7c measures.

### 3b. The outcome

The outcome that tests this chapter's hypothesis is completed sibship size, the number of children
the parents of an affected child ended up having. The hypothesis claims that a law reduced what a
child was worth to its parents, and the parents' response to a change in that worth is the number of
children they choose to have. Siblings measure how many children the child's parent has, which is
the quantity the hypothesis is about. Completed sibship size is also a lifetime measure, counted
after the parents have finished childbearing, so it is unaffected by a shift in the ages at which
the births occurred.

### 3c. The ideal experiment

The ideal experiment takes otherwise identical households, randomly lowers what their children could
earn, and then counts how many children those households go on to have. Everything else is held
fixed: the parents' own schooling, their expectations about what their children will become, and the
local labor market. Because only the earning capacity of children varies across households, any
resulting difference in family size traces to that capacity and to nothing else, which separates the
economic value of a child from every other thing a schooling law changes.

### 3d. The practical design and its four costs

Nobody randomizes a child's earning capacity, so the literature uses the staggered adoption of
school-leaving and child-labor laws across states or countries and compares cohorts the laws bound
against cohorts they did not. The comparison is a natural experiment: the timing of a legislature's
action supplies variation the researcher did not create. Four limitations follow directly from that
substitution, and each governs a later section of this chapter.

Legislatures choose when to pass these laws, so the treatment is endogenous, and places that
legislated may differ from places that did not in ways the comparison cannot rule out. We label this
problem **adoption endogeneity**. A state that passed a child-labor restriction in 1910 may have
been the state where manufacturing was already mechanizing, where wages were already rising, or
where fertility was already falling, which is the historical ordering Greenwood and Seshadri
describe; the design compares such a state with one where none of that was true. Section 7d reports
what adoption endogeneity does to the risk-of-bias rating of the one on-target study, and Section 9
reports what it does to the causal rating.

School-leaving laws and child-labor laws arrived together, so an estimate of their combined effect
cannot say how much of the fertility response came from the earnings a child lost and how much from
the schooling a child gained. We label this problem **policy bundling**. Section 7c reports the one
specification in the record that comes closest to breaking it.

A schooling law also raises what parents expect a child to become, so lower fertility may reflect a
costlier and better-educated child rather than a less valuable one. We label this competing
explanation the **child-quality channel**. The two accounts predict the same fall in fertility and
differ in what they say the parents were responding to, so an estimate consistent with the
hypothesis is not by itself evidence for the hypothesis. Section 7c and the verdict in Section 10
both turn on the review's inability to tell the two accounts apart.

Most available designs measure the wrong person's fertility, because the cleanest quasi-experiments
change a girl's own schooling and then count her own children. We label this mismatch the
**wrong-generation problem**. Three generations are involved, and the law acts on the middle one:

```
parent ──(1)──▶ female student ──(2)──▶ her own children
```

Arrow (1) is the hypothesis of this chapter: a school-leaving or child-labor law bound the female
student, and the quantity that tests the hypothesis is how many children her parents had. Arrow (2)
is the hypothesis of the companion chapter on tempo effects: the same law bound the same female
student, and the quantity is how many children she herself went on to have. A study that follows a
girl and counts her own children twenty years later estimates arrow (2) and says nothing about arrow
(1). Sections 6 and 7 apply the wrong-generation problem as the test that decides which studies are
evidence for this hypothesis at all, rather than as one more qualification on an estimate.

## 4. Search strategy

This chapter and the companion chapter on birth postponement share one literature search, because
both hypotheses turn on the same school-leaving and child-labor laws. Geruso and Royer (2018) is
counted in both chapters, since it reports teenage fertility and completed fertility.

The search began from citations rather than from a keyword query. Seven papers, each located
independently of the others, served as anchors, and the search followed citations in both directions
from them: every work those seven cited, and every work that cited them. Our review calls the method
a cold-start citation-frame audit. Three screens then narrowed the result. A blinded screen of
titles and abstracts sorted every record as relevant, uncertain, or not relevant; a focused review
reconsidered the exceptions that screen set aside; and a strict test asked of each surviving paper
whether it studied an actual schooling or child-labor law and reported a fertility outcome.

Six papers that change a female student's own schooling entered the kept set because they report
completed or desired fertility under a compulsory-schooling reform, which is a test of contextual
relevance rather than of mechanism. Every one of the six falls under the wrong-generation problem of
Section 3d, and Section 6 sets them aside for that reason.

## 7. Quantitative synthesis

### 7a. Pooling

There is no need to pool estimates here, because only one study meets our search criterion.

### 7b. Shanan's estimates, in the order the chain states them

Shanan (2023) studies American states over the 1880 to 1940 cohorts, a period when children worked
for pay and when states adopted compulsory-schooling laws and child-labor restrictions together, and
it measures a woman's exposure over her own ages 20 to 30, so the restrictions in force are the ones
that would have governed her children's work.

The compulsory-schooling and child-labor restrictions moved children out of work and into
classrooms, which is the first step the hypothesis claims. Children covered by the restrictions were
0.029 less likely to be employed (SE 0.008) against a baseline employment rate of 0.118, a fall of
about a quarter, and 0.042 more likely to attend school (SE 0.008) against a baseline attendance
rate of 0.882. Both effects are statistically significant, and both refer to the generation of
children the laws bound.

Women whose children were covered by the restrictions had fewer children over their lifetimes, which
is the last step the hypothesis claims. A woman exposed to any restriction at ages 20 to 30 had
0.301 fewer children (SE 0.044) against a sample mean of 3.112, and a one-year rise in the average
age at which children in her state left school lowered her completed fertility by 0.108 children (SE
0.024). Both effects are statistically significant, and both refer to the generation of parents
whose children the laws bound.

Measured year by year rather than over a lifetime, the same two policies lower the chance a woman
gives birth, and the demographic-significance calculation in Section 8.2 rests on the annual
version. Exposure to any restriction lowered the annual probability of a birth by 0.85 percentage
points (SE 0.11) against a 14.3% baseline, and a one-year rise in the average school exit age
lowered the same probability by 0.27 percentage points (SE 0.03).

Shanan never measures the middle step, the earnings a child no longer brought home, and infers it
from the two steps on either side. The study puts no figure on what a child's work was worth to the
child's parents, and does not trace the fertility effect through that lost income.

### 7c. The joint specification and policy bundling

Entering both policy margins in the same regression leaves each of them negative and statistically
significant, which is as far as the record goes toward breaking the policy bundling of Section 3d.
Holding the average school exit age fixed, being covered by any restriction lowers the annual birth
probability by 0.0050 (SE 0.0013); holding coverage fixed, a one-year rise in the exit age lowers it
by 0.0018 (SE 0.0004).

The joint specification separates two legal instruments without separating the two economic
channels, because neither coefficient measures what a child's work was worth to the child's parents.
The earnings channel of Section 1 and the child-quality channel of Section 3d therefore remain
joined inside both estimates.

### 7d. Design quality of the only on-target study

Shanan's design has one weakness that matters more than the rest: American states did not decide at
random when to restrict children's work. A state that legislated early may have differed from a
state that legislated late in ways that were already moving fertility, and the study cannot rule
that out. The laws it studies also bundle a work restriction with a schooling requirement, which
Section 7c shows the joint specification cannot pull apart. The estimate is the best one available,
and it is not a clean one.

In the terms of our review's framework, Shanan is rated SERIOUS overall for risk of bias, driven by
SERIOUS risk of confounding. The two inputs to that judgment are the adoption endogeneity and the
policy bundling of Section 3d. On the four-level scale of Section 6, the chapter's only on-target
study sits one step from the worst available rating.

## 8. Demographic significance

Our review evaluates every hypothesis against three phenomena: pre-modern fertility variation before
1870, the First Demographic Transition of roughly 1870 to 1965 (FDT), and the Second Demographic
Transition from 1965 to the present (SDT).

### 8.1 Pre-modern

Children had economic value in pre-modern societies, and no compulsory-schooling law existed there
to reduce it, because laws of that kind are modern. The hypothesis therefore has no treatment to be
identified from before 1870. **Verdict: insufficient data.**

### 8.2 FDT

Shanan's own decomposition attributes 8% of the 1900 to 1930 decline in the annual chance of a birth
among 30-year-old native-born American women to the spread of the restrictions, and three numbers
produce it. Exposure to the restrictions rose by 48 percentage points over those three decades. Full
exposure lowers the annual birth probability by 0.85 percentage points. The observed birth
probability fell by 5 percentage points over the same window. So `0.48 * 0.85 / 5 = 0.08`.

Three features of the calculation limit what the 8% establishes, and none of the three gives a
reason to think the true share is smaller. It covers women at a single age, 30, rather than across
the whole childbearing span, so it is silent about how much of the total decline in births the
restrictions account for. The outcome is a period rate, the chance of a birth in a given year,
rather than the number of children a woman ends up with, and the hypothesis in Section 1 concerns
the second of those two quantities. The uncertainty attached to the 8% is understated: Shanan
reports no bootstrap interval, and propagating only the uncertainty in the estimated coefficient,
while treating the 48-point rise in exposure and the 5-point fall in birth probability as known,
gives an analytic 95% interval of 6.1% to 10.2%. Holding two of the three inputs fixed makes that
interval narrower than the truth.

Our review has two further routes to a demographic-significance verdict, we checked both here, and
neither strengthens the case beyond what the decomposition already supports. The first route asks
whether an effect of the estimated size, applied over the range the cause actually moved, could
produce the decline that occurred. The second asks how much of the variation in fertility the cause
accounts for once standard controls are included.

Cigno and Rosati (2024a) report that restrictions on children's work explain about 10% of the
American fertility decline, and their figure is the same calculation reported here at one
significant figure rather than a second estimate. Their underlying primary study is Shanan, whose
own calculation gives 8%. The literature holds one calculation here, not two.

**Verdict: partial**, at a decomposition share of 8%.

### 8.3 SDT

The SDT is a fertility transition rather than a date, and a setting belongs to it when fertility
there is falling from just above replacement to below it and continuing down. A compulsory-schooling
law passed in a poor country this decade says nothing about the SDT unless that country's fertility
is making the crossing at the time.

Where the SDT is best documented, in rich countries, children's market work is near zero before the
crossing begins, so a school-leaving law removes almost no earnings a parent was counting on and the
hypothesis is close to inapplicable. School attendance in those countries is already near universal,
and a law that pushes the leaving age from 16 to 17 changes a teenager's schedule rather than a
household's income. The narrow scope bounds the range over which the hypothesis could ever apply
rather than leaving a hole in the evidence.

Where children still worked at the time of a reform, and the mechanism could therefore still
operate, our corpus holds two study windows and cannot place either in the SDT. Taiwan in 1968 and
China in 1986 are both settings where children worked, and neither study window has been classified
by the fertility level that governs SDT membership. Chinese fertility over the relevant years was
also under the one-child policy, which our review requires be flagged, and under a binding state
mandate a crossing below replacement reflects the mandate as much as the process the SDT names.
Every study of both reforms counts the female student's own children in any case, so none would test
completed sibship size even in a setting that qualified.

**Verdict: insufficient data**, for two distinct reasons: the hypothesis is close to inapplicable
where the SDT is best documented, and it is unclassified and untested where it might still apply.

## 9. GRADE rating

We have low confidence that these laws caused the fall in fertility during the FDT, for three
reasons a reader can check. Only one study measures the fertility of the right generation, and a
single study in a single country cannot show that a result holds anywhere else. That study cannot
rule out the possibility that the states which legislated were already changing for other reasons.
And the step in the middle of the argument, the money a child stopped bringing home, was never
measured.

In the terms of our review's framework, causal credibility for the FDT is **Low**, the unanimous
judgment of a three-rater panel scoring independently. The GRADE scale our review uses runs from
Very low through Low and Moderate to High. Shanan supplies one credible historical quasi-experiment
reaching from a policy through children's work to completed fertility, which is the pattern the Low
rating describes, and our review sets replication across two or more settings as the boundary
between Low and Moderate. The panel reached Low without the risk-of-bias assessment of Section 7d
stated in front of it, and Shanan's SERIOUS overall rating turns on the same confounding the panel's
own reasoning names.

**Pre-modern and SDT receive no rating**, because neither has a study to rate.

## 10. Verdict

The hypothesis is credible at Low confidence for the FDT and untested for the other two phenomena.

| Phenomenon | Causal credibility | Demographic significance |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-modern | Insufficient data | Insufficient data |
| FDT | Low | Partial; the restrictions account for 8% of the 1900–1930 decline in the annual birth probability at age 30 |
| SDT | Insufficient data | Insufficient data |

The evidence supports a historically real relationship running from these laws through children's
work to completed fertility, at modest scale. How much of the relationship runs through the earnings
a child no longer brought home, rather than through a parent's expectation of a better-educated
child, is unresolved on the evidence in hand.

## 11. Open questions and recommended studies

An independent replication of Shanan's design in a second country or period would raise the causal
rating above Low, because our review sets replication across two or more settings as the boundary
between Low and Moderate. Such a replication needs a country whose subnational units adopted
school-leaving or child-labor laws at different dates, and census or registration data that link a
woman to the number of children she had by the end of her childbearing years. The estimate to report
is the effect of exposure, over the woman's own childbearing ages, on her completed fertility.

A study that measures what children actually earned, and traces the fertility effect through that
measure, would establish the middle step that Shanan infers from the two ends. Historical census
returns record children's occupations, and where state and industry wage series survive they record
what those occupations paid, so a researcher can construct the earnings a child of a given age in a
given state could expect. Regressing completed fertility on that constructed earnings measure,
instrumented by the restrictions, would give the effect of a dollar of forgone child earnings rather
than the effect of a statute. American states over the 1880 to 1940 cohorts, the setting Shanan
already uses, have the census returns the construction requires.

A decomposition of completed cohort fertility can be specified from Shanan's published −0.301
without collecting any new data, and it would test the hypothesis on the lifetime outcome the
hypothesis names rather than on the annual birth rate of 30-year-old women. The analysis applies the
−0.301 to the change in exposure across successive birth cohorts of American women and compares the
implied change with the observed decline in completed fertility for those cohorts. The result is
directly comparable to the 8% in Section 8.2 and is measured on the quantity Section 3b defines.

For the SDT, our search needs a schooling or child-labor reform in a setting whose fertility is
verified from data to be crossing below replacement at the time of the reform, with completed
sibship size as the outcome. Income and calendar date do not substitute for the fertility check, and
Section 8.3 explains why the two study windows already in the corpus cannot be classified without
it.

For the pre-modern period, the hypothesis needs a policy that forced children into school, we doubt
one exists at a useful scale, and a search may return nothing. A weaker alternative is worth naming
for what it is: variation in children's productive contribution, by crop, by inheritance regime, or
by proximity to manufacturing, would identify the value of children without identifying the effect
of a schooling mandate. Such a design tests an adjacent claim about the value of children, not the
claim in Section 1 about the effect of a law.

- Lay-readability gate: `output/compulsory-education-chapters-lay-readability-review.csv`

Four departures from our protocol affect this chapter:

- The search is the cold-start citation-frame audit described in Section 4 rather than the
  pre-registered keyword flow, so the counts in Section 5 are not those of a conventional
  database-keyword search.
- The structured extraction sheet the protocol specifies is unstarted, so the routing in Section 6
  rests on title and abstract judgments plus a narrative full-text evidence file rather than on the
  controlled fields built to settle it.
- No classification of study windows by fertility level was run for this hypothesis, although one
  exists for two other hypotheses in the review, which is why Section 8.3 cannot place Taiwan 1968 or
  China 1986.
- The risk-of-bias assessment is preliminary.

[Note to co-authors: our protocol defines demographic significance by a decomposition share of at
least 10%, a slope-sufficiency verdict of sufficient, or a conditional R² of at least 0.15. The 8%
point estimate is below the first of those, the analytic upper bound of 10.2% is above it, and the
other two routes met no threshold. Sections 8.2 and 10 report the share plainly; the protocol text
still defines significance by the bar, and the two disagree until that edit lands.]
