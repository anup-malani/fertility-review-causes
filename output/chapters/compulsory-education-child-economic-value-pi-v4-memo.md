# Compulsory Education and the Economic Value of Children

> **Version: PI rewrite v4, memo register.** Drafted from an approved outline for a generalist reader
> with undergraduate economics training. One of two register experiments from the same outline.
> Substance, estimates, citations, GRADE ratings, and verdicts are unchanged from the source chapter;
> the prose is new. Not the chapter of record.

**Category:** Economic  
**Primary mechanism:** Compulsory attendance and child-labor restrictions reduce children's work,
lowering the economic return parents expect from an additional child, and so reducing completed
fertility.  
**Cross-references:** Tempo effects and birth postponement (compulsory schooling). Geruso and Royer
(2018) is kept by both chapters, Cummins (2025) is routed there by mechanism, and the six
own-schooling studies set aside here are on-mechanism there.

## 1. The claim

The hypothesis asserts a chain of three steps: a school-attendance or child-labor law takes a child
out of paid work; the earnings the parents no longer collect make each child worth less to them; and
parents who expect less money from each child have fewer children. The first step is a fact about
children's time, the second about a household's budget, and the third about a fertility decision.
The claim is true only if all three steps hold, and a study that establishes one of them establishes
only that one.

The hypothesis asks how many children the parents of an affected child went on to have, so the
quantity that tests it is the number of siblings a child covered by the law turned out to have.
Siblings measure how many children the child's parent has. A study that follows a girl bound by a
school-leaving law and counts her own children twenty years later answers a different question,
about how her own schooling changed her own fertility, and that question belongs to the chapter on
tempo effects and birth postponement. In both chapters the count covers a woman's whole childbearing
life, not the ages at which her births occurred.

## 2. Theoretical mechanism

The predicted sign comes from one step that most models in this literature share: children produce
income, a binding schooling requirement removes their work time, the return to an additional child
falls, and parents shift from having more children toward spending more on each one. The quantity of
children and the quality of each are substitutes in the household's budget, so a law that raises the
price of quantity buys quality instead. Every model below either adds a route to that prediction or
denies that the prediction follows.

Hazan and Berdugo (2001) give the formative statement, in which child labor and high fertility hold
each other up in a development trap that a temporary ban on child work, or a compulsory-schooling
requirement, can break. Poor parents send children to work; working children are cheap on net, so
parents have many of them; many uneducated children keep wages low and keep the next generation
poor. A prohibition that binds for one generation moves the economy to a second steady state with
little child labor and low fertility, and the new steady state persists after the prohibition
lapses.

Mookherjee, Prina, and Ray (2012) route the same prediction through the mix of occupations an
economy produces: tighter regulation of children's work raises schooling, more schooling moves the
next generation into skilled occupations, and an economy weighted toward skilled work settles at a
lower number of children per family. The fertility change in their model is a by-product of an
occupational change, so it arrives with a lag and depends on how readily the economy absorbs skilled
workers.

Fioroni (2017) reaches the prediction through the price of a child: a child who works pays for part
of its own upkeep, so a law that removes children's work raises the net cost of a child, and the
number parents choose falls for the ordinary reason that a higher price reduces quantity demanded.
The route runs entirely through current prices and requires no assumption about what parents expect
their children to become.

Emerson and Knabb (2013) put the weight on what parents come to expect: a temporary ban on children's
work paired with a schooling requirement changes the kind of child a household plans for, and once
households expect schooled children rather than employed ones, the low-fertility pattern holds even
after the ban ends. Their model therefore predicts that a restriction lifted after one generation
leaves fertility permanently lower, which the price route does not.

Cigno and Rosati (2005) treat the number of children, how many of them survive, and how much
schooling each receives as a single household decision taken jointly, which makes the fertility
response to a schooling law depend on how the household reallocates across all three margins at
once. Their later work adds that a partial restriction on children's work can sustain a
high-schooling outcome on its own, while a total ban may require insurance and education policy
alongside it (Cigno and Rosati 2024b).

Two models in the corpus deny that the negative sign is a theorem. Baland and Robinson (2000) show
that a child-labor ban can raise welfare and still leave its fertility effect ambiguous, because the
ban changes both the cost of a child and the return to educating one, and the two effects pull in
opposite directions. Takakura (2023) obtains opposite signs for different households: a ban lowers
fertility among unskilled parents, whose children were the ones working, and raises it among skilled
parents, so the national total depends on which parents a given restriction binds.

Greenwood and Seshadri (2004) make the law a consequence of economic change rather than its cause.
In their account, technological progress raised employers' demand for skilled labor and cut the
value of unskilled children's hands; children left work for that reason; and effective regulation
arrived after much of the withdrawal from work had already happened. On that history, an estimate
identified from the date a legislature acted charges the law with a decline that rising demand for
skill had already started, which is a challenge to every design described in Section 3.

## 3. Empirical strategy

The hypothesis is tested with two distinct legal instruments that the literature usually runs
together. A school-leaving law sets the age below which a child must remain enrolled. A child-labor
law restricts what work a child may do, by minimum age, by hours, or by occupation. The two are
different levers on the same margin: one occupies the child's time, the other forbids the employment
directly. They frequently arrived in the same legislative wave, which constrains what a single
estimate can be said to identify.

The outcome that tests the hypothesis is completed sibship size, the number of children the parents
of an affected child ended up having by the close of their childbearing years. The hypothesis claims
that a law reduced what a child was worth to its parents, and parents respond to a change in what a
child is worth by changing the number of children they choose to have. Siblings measure how many
children the child's parent has, which is the quantity the hypothesis is about.

The ideal experiment would take otherwise identical households, randomly lower what their children
could earn, and then count how many children those households went on to have. It would hold fixed
the parents' own schooling, their expectations about what their children would become, and the local
labor market, so that the only thing differing across households is the earning capacity of a child.
That experiment isolates the economic value of a child from everything else a schooling law changes.

Nobody randomizes a child's earning capacity, so the practical design substitutes the staggered
adoption of school-leaving and child-labor laws across states or countries and compares cohorts the
laws bound against cohorts they did not. Four limitations follow from that substitution, and each
one governs a later section of this chapter.

1. **Legislatures do not adopt laws at random.** Places that legislated may differ from places that
   did not in ways the comparison cannot rule out, including the economic changes Greenwood and
   Seshadri (2004) describe. This limitation drives the risk-of-bias rating at the close of Section 7
   and the GRADE rating in Section 9.
2. **The two law types arrive together.** When a state raises the school-leaving age and restricts
   children's employment in the same session, the earnings a child lost cannot be separated from
   what the schooling requirement changed on its own. Section 7 reports the one specification in the
   record that comes close to separating them.
3. **A schooling law also raises what parents expect a child to become.** Lower fertility after such
   a law may reflect a costlier, better-educated child rather than a less valuable one, and the two
   readings imply different mechanisms with the same sign. Section 7 and the verdict in Section 10
   both return to this limitation.
4. **Most available designs measure the wrong person's fertility.** The cleanest quasi-experiments
   change a girl's own schooling and then count her own children, which measures how many offspring
   a female child subject to the law went on to have, not how many siblings she had.

The fourth limitation decides which studies count as evidence here, and it disqualifies most of the
published literature on compulsory schooling and fertility. Those studies estimate the right
quantity for a different hypothesis, about how a woman's own schooling changes her own fertility,
and they are weighed in the chapter on tempo effects and birth postponement. The chapters share a
literature and divide it by whose fertility each estimate describes.

## 4. Search strategy

The literature for this chapter was assembled by a citation-frame audit rather than by the keyword
query our protocol pre-registers, and the counts in Section 5 should be read as the output of that
audit. Seven papers, each found independently, served as anchors, and the search followed citations
in both directions from every one of them: each paper the seven cited, and each paper that cited
them. Those records went to a blinded screen of titles and abstracts, then to a focused review of
the exceptions the screen set aside, then to a strict test asking of each surviving paper whether it
studied an actual schooling or child-labor law and reported a fertility outcome.

This chapter and the chapter on birth postponement share one search, because both hypotheses turn on
the same school-leaving and child-labor laws. Geruso and Royer (2018) is kept by both, because it
reports teenage fertility for one chapter and completed fertility for the other.

The sixteen papers kept here divide into ten theory papers and six that estimate fertility under a
schooling reform, and the six entered on a weaker test than the one this chapter applies. Reporting
a fertility outcome under an actual reform makes a paper contextually relevant to the question. It
does not make the paper evidence about the economic value of children, for the reason set out as the
fourth limitation in Section 3.

## 7. Quantitative synthesis

There is no need to pool estimates here, because only one study meets our search criterion.

Shanan (2023) studies American states across the 1880 to 1940 birth cohorts, a period when states
passed compulsory-schooling laws and child-labor restrictions together and children still worked for
pay. Exposure is measured over a woman's own ages 20 to 30, her main childbearing years, so the
restrictions in force are the ones that would govern the work of the children she was deciding
whether to have.

The restrictions moved children out of paid work and into classrooms: children covered by them were
0.029 less likely to be employed (SE 0.008) against a baseline employment rate of 0.118, a fall of
about a quarter. They were also 0.042 more likely to attend school (SE 0.008) against a baseline
attendance rate of 0.882. Both effects are precisely estimated. The first step of the chain in
Section 1, from law to the child's work, holds in this setting.

Exposure to any restriction over a woman's ages 20 to 30 lowered her completed fertility by 0.301
children (SE 0.044) against a sample mean of 3.112. A one-year rise in the average age at which the
children in her state left school lowered it by a further 0.108 children (SE 0.024). Both estimates
describe the mother's lifetime childbearing, and both policy variables describe the generation of
her children. The third step of the chain, from what a child is worth to how many
children a woman has, holds in the same study that establishes the first.

Measured year by year rather than over a lifetime, the same exposure lowered a woman's annual
probability of giving birth by 0.85 percentage points (SE 0.11) against a baseline of 14.3%. The
decomposition in Section 8.2 rests on that period estimate.

Entering both policy margins in the same specification leaves each of them negative and several
times its standard error, which establishes that neither margin carries the whole effect. Any
restriction lowers the annual birth probability by 0.0050 (SE 0.0013), and a one-year rise in the
children's average exit age lowers it by 0.0018 (SE 0.0004), each holding the other fixed. That
specification is the closest the record comes to separating lost child earnings from the schooling
requirement, and it falls short of separating them. The middle step of the chain, the fall in what a
child was worth to its parents, is never measured in dollars and is inferred from the two steps on
either side of it, so the second and third limitations in Section 3 both remain in force.

Shanan carries a risk-of-bias rating of SERIOUS overall, driven by SERIOUS confounding, for residual
endogeneity in which states adopted these laws and for the bundling of a work restriction with a
schooling requirement. Those are the first and second limitations in Section 3. The chapter's only
on-target study therefore sits one step from the worst rating on the four-level scale.

## 8. Demographic significance

This chapter reaches a verdict for each of three phenomena: pre-modern fertility variation before
1870, the First Demographic Transition of roughly 1870 to 1965 (FDT), and the Second Demographic
Transition from 1965 to the present (SDT).

### 8.1 Pre-modern

Children had economic value in pre-modern societies, and no compulsory-schooling law existed to
reduce it, because such laws are modern. The hypothesis names a treatment the period does not
contain. **Verdict: insufficient data**, for causal credibility and for demographic significance
alike.

### 8.2 FDT

The spread of these restrictions accounts for 8% of the 1900–1930 fall in the annual chance of a
birth among 30-year-old native-born American women. Three numbers produce the figure: exposure to
the restrictions rose by 48 percentage points over those three decades, full exposure lowers the
annual birth probability by 0.85 percentage points, and the observed birth probability fell by 5
percentage points, giving `0.48 * 0.85 / 5 = 0.08`.

Four qualifications attach to the 8%, and none of them makes the true share smaller; the first three
establish that the figure answers a narrower question than the hypothesis asks and carries more
uncertainty than its stated interval shows. The calculation covers women at a single age, 30, and
not the whole childbearing span. It measures the chance of a birth in a given year rather than the
number of children a woman ends up with, and this hypothesis is about the second. Shanan attaches no
uncertainty interval to the share, and the sensitivity check our review ran does not repair the
omission. Carrying through only the uncertainty in Shanan's coefficient, while treating the 48-point
rise in exposure and the 5-point fall in birth probability as known, gives an analytic 95% interval
of 6.1% to 10.2%. Holding two of the three inputs fixed makes that interval narrower than the truth.

The fourth qualification is that our review has two other routes to judging whether a cause is large
enough to matter for a decline, and both were checked here without strengthening the case. One asks
whether an effect of the estimated size, applied over the range the cause actually moved, could
produce the decline that occurred. The other asks how much of the variation in fertility the cause
accounts for once standard controls are included. Neither met a threshold.

The 10% that Cigno and Rosati (2024a) report for the American fertility decline looks like a second
estimate of the same quantity and is not one, because their underlying primary study is Shanan
(2023) and the 10% is Shanan's own 8% carried at one significant figure. There is one calculation
here, not two, and a reader who meets both numbers should count them once.

**Verdict: partial**, on a share of 8% of the 1900–1930 decline in the annual birth probability at
age 30.

### 8.3 SDT

The SDT is a fertility transition rather than a stretch of the calendar: a setting belongs to it
when fertility there is falling from just above replacement to below it and continuing down.
National income and calendar period stand in for that condition badly, and a compulsory-schooling
law passed in a poor country this decade says nothing about the SDT unless that country's fertility
is making the crossing at the time.

Where the SDT is best documented, in rich countries, children's market work is near zero before the
crossing begins, so a schooling law removes almost no earnings a parent was counting on and the
hypothesis is close to inapplicable. School attendance in those settings is already universal, and a
law pushing the leaving age from 16 to 17 changes little about what a child contributes to the
household. The near-absence of children's market work limits the range over which the hypothesis can
apply, and it leaves no gap in the evidence to be filled.

Elsewhere the mechanism could still operate, and the corpus does not reach those settings. Two of
its reforms fall in places where children still worked, Taiwan in 1968 and China in 1986, and
neither setting has been classified by the fertility level that governs the distinction, so neither
can be placed in the SDT on the evidence in hand. Every study of both reforms counts the mother's
own children in any case, so none of them would test sibship size even in a setting that qualified.

**Verdict: insufficient data**, for two distinct reasons: the hypothesis is close to inapplicable
where the SDT is best documented, and it is unclassified and untested where it might still apply.

## 9. GRADE rating

Three raters scored the FDT causal claim independently and all three returned **Low**, the
second-lowest of the four GRADE levels. GRADE runs from Very low through Low and Moderate to High,
and it rates the causal claim rather than the existence of a correlation. Shanan (2023) supplies one
credible historical quasi-experiment reaching from a policy through children's work to completed
fertility, and our review sets replication across two or more settings as the boundary between Low
and Moderate, which no second study here crosses.

Two further reasons hold the rating down, and both are limitations named in Section 3: states may
have adopted these laws for reasons the design cannot rule out, and the fall in what children were
worth is inferred from the two ends of the chain rather than measured in the middle. The panel
reached Low without Shanan's risk-of-bias rating stated in front of it, and that rating, SERIOUS
overall on SERIOUS confounding, turns on the same policy-adoption endogeneity the panel's own
reasoning names. The rating is therefore referred back to the panel with the risk-of-bias assessment
attached.

**Pre-modern and SDT are unrated**, because neither has a study to rate.

## 10. Verdict

The hypothesis is causally credible at Low confidence for the FDT and untested for the other two
phenomena.

| Phenomenon | Causal credibility | Demographic significance |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-modern | Insufficient data | Insufficient data |
| FDT | Low | Partial; 8% of the 1900–1930 decline in the annual birth probability at age 30 |
| SDT | Insufficient data | Insufficient data |

The evidence supports a historically real relationship running from policy through children's work
to completed fertility, at modest scale. How much of that relationship runs through lost child
earnings, as against a parent's expectation of a better-educated child, is unresolved on the
evidence in hand.

## 11. Open questions and recommended studies

An independent replication of Shanan's design in a second country or period would let the causal
rating rise above Low, because our review sets replication across two or more settings as the
boundary between Low and Moderate. The replication needs three things: sub-national units that adopted school-leaving or
child-labor laws on a staggered schedule, census or registration records that report how many
children a woman bore over her whole life, and cohorts old enough to have finished childbearing by
the time they were counted. The estimate to report is the effect of exposure on completed fertility,
with the child-work first stage shown in the same paper.

A study that measures what children actually earned, and traces the fertility effect through that
measure, would establish the middle step of the chain that Shanan infers from its two ends.
Household budget surveys, census earnings by age, and firm payrolls recording juvenile wages each
carry the earnings figure, and the design matches those records to the same states and cohorts whose
fertility is observed. The number to report is how much of the fertility effect survives once the
change in what children earned is held fixed.

A decomposition of completed cohort fertility can be specified from Shanan's published −0.301
without collecting new data, and it would test the hypothesis on the lifetime outcome the hypothesis
names rather than on a period rate at a single age. The inputs are the same three the 8% calculation
uses, with completed fertility in place of the annual birth probability: the rise in exposure across
cohorts, the completed-fertility coefficient, and the observed fall in completed fertility over the
same cohorts.

For the SDT, the search needs a schooling or child-labor reform in a setting whose fertility is
verified to be crossing below replacement at the time of the reform, with sibship size as the
outcome. Income and calendar date do not substitute for that fertility check, which is why Taiwan
1968 and China 1986 cannot be placed on the evidence in hand.

For the pre-modern period, the hypothesis needs a policy that forced children into school, and we
doubt one exists at a useful scale, so a search may well return nothing. Variation in what children
produced, by crop, by inheritance regime, or by proximity to manufacturing, would identify the value
of children without identifying the effect of a schooling mandate, which tests an adjacent claim
rather than this one.
